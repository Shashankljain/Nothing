1. Case presentation and history taking

Patient details

- Name: Ramlal
- Age: 40 years
- Sex: Male
- Religion: Hindu
- Residence: Perila
- Occupation: Handyman
- Chief complaint: Swelling in the scrotal region for 2 years.

History of presenting illness

The patient was apparently healthy 2 years ago when he accidentally noticed a small swelling on the right side of the scrotum.

- The swelling gradually increased in size over the years.
- It has remained unchanged at its present size since then.
- It does not increase in size on straining.
- There is no reduction in size on lying down (supine position).
- There is no reduction in size on compression.
- There is no history of rapid increase in size.

Mentor’s question: What swellings decrease in size on lying down?

Student’s answer:

- Inguinal hernia.
- Varicocele.
- Lymph varix (lymphovarix).
- Congenital hydrocele – decreases slowly over minutes or hours.

Inguinal hernia, varicocele and lymphovarix usually decrease rapidly in the supine position or with compression.

Mentor’s question: Are there other swellings in the body that decrease on lying down?

- Umbilical hernia.
- Ventral hernia.
- Incisional (postoperative) hernia.
- Varicose veins of the lower limbs.

History of complications

There is no history of:

- Pain.
- Fever.
- Vesicles or discharge from the skin.
- Ulceration.
- Sinus or fistula formation.
- Difficulty during urination.
- Difficulty during sexual intercourse.

History of associated events

No history of trauma.

Mentor asks: Why is a history of trauma important?

Because trauma or hematocele may simply draw the patient’s attention to a pre-existing swelling.

No history of fever with chills associated with pain and swelling.

This helps assess infective causes.

No history of swelling of the feet.

This is asked to rule out filariasis.

2. Filariasis and scrotal swelling

Mentor’s question: How does filariasis cause scrotal swelling?

There are three important mechanisms:

1. Accumulation of fluid in the tunica vaginalis:

- Hydrocele.
- Chylocele.

2. Dilatation of lymphatic vessels:

- Lymphovarix.
- Lymphoedema of the scrotum.

3. Late-stage changes:

- Elephantiasis of the scrotal skin.

Important sequence to remember:

- Hydrocele and chylocele.
- Lymphovarix and lymphoedema.
- Elephantiasis of the scrotum.

3. History of sexually transmitted infections

There is no history of:

- Exposure to sexually transmitted diseases.
- Burning micturition.
- White urethral discharge.

These questions help rule out gonococcal epididymitis.

Mentor’s question: Could this swelling be gonococcal epididymo-orchitis?

Student’s answer:

No, because:

- The swelling is painless.
- It has been present for a long duration.
- There is no history of exposure to sexually transmitted diseases.

Mentor’s question: Is there another sexually transmitted disease that causes scrotal swelling?

Student’s answer:

Yes. Testicular gumma in tertiary syphilis can cause scrotal swelling, although it is very rare.

4. History of tuberculosis

There is no history suggestive of renal or pulmonary tuberculosis.

Questions asked to rule out renal tuberculosis

- Frequency of micturition.
- Pyuria.
- Haematuria.
- Dysuria.
- Joint pain.

Questions asked to rule out pulmonary tuberculosis

- Cough with expectoration.
- Haemoptysis.
- Evening rise of temperature.
- Night sweats.
- Loss of appetite.
- Weight loss.
- History of contact with a tuberculosis patient.

Mentor’s question: What is the relationship between pulmonary tuberculosis and scrotal swelling?

Genitourinary tuberculosis is usually secondary to a primary focus elsewhere in the body, commonly the lungs.

How does tuberculosis cause scrotal swelling?

According to the discussion, tuberculosis causes:

- Nodular enlargement of the epididymis.
- Secondary hydrocele.
- Thickening of the spermatic cord with cord-like induration.

5. History of malignancy

There is no history of:

- Rapid weight loss.
- Upper abdominal mass.

Mentor’s question: Why did you ask these questions?

To suspect a malignant testicular tumour.

Mentor’s question: If this patient has a testicular tumour, which type is most likely?

Student’s answer:

Seminoma is more likely because the patient is around 40 years old.

- Seminoma: commonly occurs in middle-aged men.
- Teratoma: more commonly occurs in younger age groups, around 20–30 years.

6. Other history

- No history of previous puncture or aspiration of the swelling.
- No history of surgery related to the swelling.
- Family history and personal history do not reveal any additional information.

7. Local examination

The mentor interrupts the general examination and asks the student to proceed directly to local examination.

A. Examination in standing position

Mentor’s question: Why do you examine scrotal swelling in the standing position?

Three reasons:

- To observe whether the swelling increases in size.
- To detect an associated hernia or varicocele, which may be missed in the supine position, even after coughing.
- To assess the complete size of a hernia, which can be evaluated better in the standing position.

Findings

- Single swelling on the right side of the scrotum.
- Size: 10 × 8 × 5 cm.
- Well-defined swelling.
- Spherical in shape.
- Smooth surface.
- Slight constriction in the middle.

Important examination point: Always mention the dimensions of a swelling in three planes whenever possible.

Mentor’s question: Suppose there is a nodule on the surface of the swelling. What would you suspect?

Student’s answer:

Depending on consistency:

- Hard nodule: malignant testicular tumour.
- Soft, cystic nodule: hydrocele with hernia (as discussed in the recording).

Water-sac hernia

The mentor explains that in prolonged, severe hydrocele:

- There may be atrophy of the supporting tissues.
- The tunica vaginalis may protrude because of weakness involving the cremasteric muscle and spermatic fascia.
- The sac may become covered only by skin.

This condition is called a water-sac hernia.

B. Examination of the overlying skin

Findings

- Skin wrinkles are reduced.
- No thickening.
- No redness.
- No oedema.
- No vesicles.
- No ulcers.
- No sinuses or fistulae.
- No scars of previous surgery or infection.

Mentor’s question: What conditions cause these skin changes?

1. Wrinkling of skin

- Reduced or absent wrinkling: tuberculous epididymitis.
- Increased wrinkling: filarial scrotal oedema.

2. Causes of oedema of scrotal skin

- Filarial elephantiasis.
- Generalised anasarca.
- Extravasation of urine.
- Oedema around an infective focus.

3. Sinus

A sinus on the posterior surface is a diagnostic sign of tuberculous epididymitis.

4. Ulcer

An ulcer on the anterior surface may occur due to rupture of a testicular gumma.

Can tuberculosis produce a sinus on the anterior surface?

Yes.

Normally, the epididymis lies posterior to the testis. However, in approximately 7–8% of men, the testis may be rotated so that the epididymis lies anteriorly.

In such cases, a tuberculous sinus may open on the anterior surface.

This can be confirmed by palpating the opposite testis.

C. Skin excoriation in large hydrocele

Mentor’s question: What causes skin excoriation in large hydrocele?

Three reasons:

1. Urine-related excoriation

- In a very large hydrocele, the penis becomes buried within the scrotum.
- The patient cannot direct the urinary stream properly.
- Urine flows over the scrotal skin, causing excoriation.

2. Friction

- The scrotal skin rubs against the medial surface of the thighs during walking.

3. Irritating local applications

- Patients may apply irritating herbal preparations to the swelling.
- These can damage the skin and cause excoriation.

D. Cough impulse

Findings

- No visible expansile impulse over the swelling.
- No visible expansile impulse over the inguinal canal on coughing.

This makes an associated hernia less likely.

8. Palpation of the swelling

Important: During palpation, always check local temperature and tenderness first.

Findings

- No local rise in temperature.
- No tenderness on palpation.

The mentor specifically reminds the student that temperature and tenderness should always be assessed first.

A. Ability to get above the swelling

Finding

The upper border of the swelling can be reached.

Mentor’s question: What do you mean by getting above the swelling?

The student explains:

It is examined by placing:

- The thumb anteriorly.
- The remaining fingers posteriorly.

The examiner attempts to palpate the upper limit of the swelling within the scrotum.

Only the structures of the spermatic cord are palpable below the external inguinal ring.

What are the cord structures that you can feel?

1. Vas deferens:

- Feels like a firm, whip-like structure.

2. Cremasteric muscle fibres:

- Feel like multiple threads.

What does this finding indicate?

If the upper border of the swelling can be reached, it indicates that the swelling is confined to the scrotum.

This is an important point in differentiating a purely scrotal swelling from an inguinoscrotal swelling.

B. Surface and consistency

Findings

- Swelling is well-defined.
- Surface is smooth.
- Consistency is cystic.

Mentor’s question: Which scrotal swellings have cystic consistency?

Student’s answer:

- All types of hydrocele.
- Chylocele.
- Recent hematocele.
- Spermatocele.
- Epididymal cyst.
- Hydatid cyst of Morgagni.
- Cystic areas in teratoma.

C. Reducibility and compressibility

Finding

The swelling is neither firm nor compressible.

Mentor’s question: What is the difference between reducibility and compressibility?

This is an important viva question.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Reducibility|Compressibility|
|Swelling decreases in size on pressure.|Swelling decreases in size on pressure.|
|It does not regain its original size spontaneously.|It regains its original size spontaneously after pressure is released.|
|It may regain size with gravity or coughing.|The swelling refills with fluid or blood.|
|Usually associated with displacement of contents, particularly viscera.|Usually associated with emptying of fluid or blood from the swelling.|

Which swellings show compressibility or a positive emptying sign?

Classically:

- Cavernous haemangioma.
- Lymphangioma.
- Narrow-necked meningocele.
- Capillary haemangioma.

D. Fluctuation test

Finding

The swelling is fluctuant.

Mentor’s question: What is fluctuation?

Fluctuation is the presence of a transmitted impulse in two planes at right angles to each other.

What does fluctuation indicate?

It indicates that the swelling contains fluid.

How is the fluctuation test performed?

The student describes the following technique:

- First, fix the swelling.
- Hold and steady the swelling between the ring and middle fingers of both hands.
- Place the thumbs and index fingers of both hands at the upper and lower poles of the swelling.
- Apply pressure with one finger.
- Feel for transmission of the impulse at the diagonally opposite finger.
- Repeat the process in the perpendicular plane.

Why is the scrotum fixed during the test?

The scrotum is freely mobile.

If it is not fixed, the entire swelling may move during examination, giving a false impression of fluctuation.

Exam point: Always fix the swelling before performing fluctuation testing in scrotal swellings.

9. Transillumination test (Diaphanoscopy)

How is the test performed?

The student describes the following procedure:

- Pull the scrotal skin over the swelling with the left hand.
- Take a flashlight in the right hand.
- First place the flashlight against the normal, healthy side of the scrotum.
- Observe the normal translucency of the skin.
- Then place the flashlight against the lateral surface of the swelling.
- Observe the appearance of the transmitted light.

Finding in this patient

A red glow is seen throughout the swelling.

This indicates that the swelling is translucent.

If the glow is doubtful or absent:

- Perform the test in a completely dark room.
- Alternatively, observe through rolled black paper.

Mentor’s instruction for examination: A roll of black paper may be provided in the examination room. Use it if you are asked to demonstrate diaphanoscopy.

Why should normal skin translucency be checked first?

Because translucency varies from person to person depending on:

- Thickness of the skin.
- Power of the flashlight.
- Lighting conditions in the examination room.

Thin skin or a powerful flashlight may produce a false impression of positive transillumination.

Interpretation of positive transillumination

The student concludes:

- The swelling contains clear fluid.
- The wall of the sac is not thickened.
- The testicle cannot be separated from the swelling because the fluid surrounds it.

Positive transillumination is a characteristic diagnostic finding in hydrocele of the tunica vaginalis.

Limitations and errors of transillumination

A. False-negative transillumination

A hydrocele may show negative transillumination in the following situations:

- Cloudy fluid within the sac.
- Thickened sac wall due to infection.
- Calcification of the sac wall.
- Elephantiasis of the scrotum.

Technical errors causing false-negative results:

- Performing the test in a brightly illuminated room.
- Holding the flashlight from behind the swelling.

Why is holding the flashlight from behind incorrect?

Because the testicle lies posteriorly and is attached to the tunica vaginalis from behind. It may block the light.

B. False-positive transillumination

May occur in:

1. Congenital hernia in infants:

- Thin-walled intestine.
- Thin overlying skin.

2. Normal translucency of the scrotal skin:

- Particularly in fair-skinned individuals with thin skin.

This is why the normal side must be checked first.

Transillumination in epididymal cysts

Mentor’s question: What is the characteristic appearance of epididymal cysts on transillumination?

- A single epididymal cyst shows bright transillumination.
- Multiple small cysts produce a mosaic appearance.

This mosaic appearance is called the Chinese lantern appearance.

Which scrotal swellings show transillumination?

- All types of hydrocele.
- Epididymal cyst.
- Hydatid cyst of Morgagni.
- Sometimes spermatocele.

Which other swellings in the body show bright transillumination?

The mentor mentions:

- Thin-walled hydrocele.
- Epididymal cyst.
- Cystic hygroma of the neck.
- Ranula (cystic swelling in the floor of the mouth).
- Thin-walled meningocele.

Mentor’s question: Which scrotal swellings prevent separate palpation of the testis?

- Hematocele.

11. Examination of the spermatic cord

Findings

- Spermatic cord is slightly thickened.
- No knotting.
- No bag-of-worms sensation.

Mentor’s question: Why is the spermatic cord thickened?

Student’s answer:

Probably due to hypertrophy of the cremasteric muscle fibres, which bear the weight of the hydrocele.

The absence of a bag-of-worms sensation makes varicocele less likely.

12. Examination of inguinal lymph nodes

Findings

Superficial horizontal inguinal lymph nodes are enlarged bilaterally.

Characteristics:

- Size: 0.5–1 cm.
- Consistency: Firm.
- Painless.
- Discrete.
- Mobile.

Mentor’s question: Is this significant?

Student initially says that it may not be significant, but suggests filarial lymphadenitis as a possible cause.

The mentor asks whether infection of the hydrocele sac could also cause enlargement.

The student clarifies:

The inguinal lymph nodes receive lymph from the cutaneous lymphatics of the scrotum.

Therefore, enlargement may occur due to:

- Filarial lymphadenitis.
- Infection involving the scrotal skin or hydrocele sac.

The important anatomical point highlighted is that the superficial inguinal nodes drain the skin of the scrotum.

Important diagnostic statement

A persistent, painless swelling that surrounds the testis and is cystic, fluctuant and translucent on examination is suggestive of hydrocele.

15. Definition of hydrocele

Mentor’s question: What is a hydrocele?

A hydrocele is an abnormal accumulation of fluid in a part of the processus vaginalis, usually within the tunica vaginalis, where it is called a vaginal hydrocele.

Composition of hydrocele fluid

According to the discussion, hydrocele fluid contains:

- Water.
- Inorganic salts.
- Proteins:

- Approximately 6% albumin.
- Fibrinogen.

- Cholesterol crystals.
- Sometimes tyrosine crystals.

Characteristics of hydrocele fluid

- Clear fluid.
- Pale yellow or amber in colour.
- Has a shimmering appearance due to cholesterol crystals.
- Coagulates when a drop of blood is added because of its high fibrinogen content.

The student explains that the choice of operation depends on the nature and thickness of the hydrocele sac.

1. Simple eversion of the sac

The sac is everted surgically.

2. Lord’s plication operation

Plication of the sac is performed.

B. Large sac

3. Subdartos pocket procedure

19. Recurrence after hydrocele surgery

Mentor’s question: Suppose the patient develops hydrocele again several years after surgery. What will you do?

Student’s answer:

I would suspect another underlying condition because a vaginal hydrocele should not recur after successful eversion of the sac.
