# 4. HISTORY TAKING – ABDOMINAL DISTENSION

## 4.1 First Question: Localized or Generalized?

Always determine whether abdominal distension is:

1. Localized.
2. Generalized.

### Localized Distension

May occur due to:

- Tumour arising from an abdominal organ.
- Organ enlargement.
- Localized collection.
- Other space-occupying lesions.

### Generalized Distension

May occur due to:

- Fluid.
- Gas.
- Faeces.
- Fat.
- Fetus in females.

The mentor emphasized asking leading questions to identify the region where the patient feels the abdomen is most distended.

## 4.2 Causes of Generalized Abdominal Distension

| Cause | Clinical explanation |
|---|---|
| Fluid | Ascites or other fluid accumulation |
| Gas | Intestinal gas accumulation |
| Faeces | Faecal loading or intestinal obstruction |
| Fat | Obesity, especially central obesity |
| Fetus | Pregnancy in females |

### Flowchart

    GENERALIZED ABDOMINAL DISTENSION
                    |
          -----------------------
          |     |      |        |
        Fluid  Gas  Faeces     Fat
                                  |
                           Central obesity

    In females, also consider pregnancy.

## 4.3 How to Differentiate the Causes Through History

### A. Fat

- General obesity usually involves other body areas as well.
- If fat accumulation is predominantly abdominal, it is called central obesity.
- Patients may simply describe their abdomen as becoming bigger rather than describing progressive bloating.

**Viva:** What is obesity predominantly involving the abdominal region called?

**Answer:** Central obesity.

### B. Gas

- May occur due to gastrointestinal pathology.
- ==Belching== may suggest upper gastrointestinal symptoms.
- Lower gastrointestinal obstruction or intestinal dysfunction can cause ==accumulation of gas proximal to the obstruction.==
- In elderly patients, progressive constipation is an important clue.

**Clinical reasoning:**

    Lower GI obstruction
             |
             v
    Accumulation of intestinal contents and gas
             |
             v
    Proximal bowel dilatation
             |
             v
    Abdominal distension

The mentor explained that upper GI symptoms such as belching alone are less likely to explain persistent generalized abdominal distension.

In this patient, there was no history of progressive constipation, making gas-related distension less likely.

### C. Fluid

Fluid accumulation becomes an important possibility in a patient with:

- Generalized abdominal distension.
- Jaundice.
- Suspected liver disease.

Additional supporting history includes:

1. History of swelling of both lower limbs.
2. History of medications prescribed for abdominal distension.
3. History of increased urine output after taking such medication.
4. History of reduction in abdominal distension following diuretic use.

If the patient reports increased urination and reduction in abdominal size after taking diuretics, this supports fluid accumulation.

### D. Pedal Oedema and Abdominal Fluid

Fluid accumulation in the abdomen may coexist with swelling of both lower limbs.

This may occur in generalized fluid accumulation (==anasarca==).

However, pedal oedema is not present in every patient with ascites.

> [!question] Viva: Why ask about bilateral pedal oedema in a patient with abdominal distension?
> **Answer:** It may indicate ==generalized fluid retention== and supports the possibility of ascites, particularly in a patient with suspected liver disease.

## 4.4 Conclusion Regarding Abdominal Distension

The patient's distension was:

- Generalized.
- Gradually progressive.
- Associated with jaundice.
- Clinically suspicious for fluid accumulation.

Therefore, ascites was considered an important possibility.

## 5.3 Types of Biliary Obstruction

Obstruction to bile flow may be:

1. Intrahepatic.
2. Extrahepatic.

### A. Intrahepatic Obstruction (intrahrpatic biliary canaliculi obstruction)

A typical example discussed by the mentor was the ==early phase of hepatitis.==

- Swelling of liver cells may compress small bile ducts.
- This may produce cholestasis and jaundice.

### B. Extrahepatic Obstruction

In surgical practice, ==surgical jaundice== commonly refers to extrahepatic biliary obstruction.

Possible causes include:

- ==Carcinoma head of pancreas.==
- Common bile duct calculus.
- Periampullary carcinoma.
- Other lesions causing compression or obstruction of the extrahepatic biliary tract.

## 5.4 Important Clinical Point

Absence of pruritus, pale stools or dark urine does ==not== completely rule out obstruction.

Their presence may support the diagnosis, but their absence does not exclude it.

## 5.5 Why Was Pancreatic Disease Considered?

## 16.2 Importance of Full Flanks

Full flanks in a uniformly distended abdomen may suggest free fluid.

However, inspection alone cannot confirm ascites.

## 16.3 Why Look for Caput Medusae?

Caput medusae refers to dilated veins around the umbilicus due to portosystemic collateral circulation.

It may occur in portal hypertension.

### Paraumbilical Portosystemic Anastomosis

The paraumbilical veins are associated with the portal venous system.

They communicate with systemic veins of the abdominal wall.

| Portal circulation | Systemic circulation |
|---|---|
| Paraumbilical veins | Superficial epigastric veins |
| Veins associated with falciform ligament | Veins of anterior abdominal wall |

The umbilical vein is obliterated after birth.

The paraumbilical veins may become enlarged in portal hypertension.

## 16.4 Why Look for Dilated Flank Veins?

Dilated veins over the flanks may indicate collateral circulation due to obstruction of the inferior vena cava.

The direction of blood flow should be examined.

### Examination of Direction of Blood Flow in Abdominal Wall Veins

1. Ask the patient to stand if necessary to make the veins more visible.
2. Identify a dilated superficial vein.
3. Compress the vein between two fingers to empty a segment.
4. Release one end while keeping the other end compressed.
5. Observe the direction and speed of filling.
6. Repeat by releasing the opposite end.
7. Compare the filling pattern.

This helps determine the direction of venous blood flow.

The patient may be asked to stand because the veins may become more prominent and easier to examine.

## 16.5 Two-Finger Test

The mentor discussed the technique of emptying and examining superficial abdominal wall veins using the fingers.

The purpose is to assess the direction of blood flow.

> [!question] Viva: Why do you examine the direction of blood flow in dilated abdominal wall veins?
> **Answer:** To help identify the direction of collateral venous flow and distinguish the possible site of venous obstruction.

## 16.6 Why Mention the Absence of Visible Peristalsis?

Visible peristalsis may occur in intestinal obstruction, particularly in thin patients.

Its absence is a relevant inspection finding.

## 16.7 Why Examine Hernial Orifices?

To identify associated hernias, which may contribute to abdominal swelling or obstruction.

### Difference Between Edge and Surface

| Feature | What to describe |
|---|---|
| Edge | Sharp, rounded, irregular |

## 19.1 Clinical Tests for Ascites

### A. Shifting Dullness

**Principle:** Free fluid shifts to the dependent portion of the abdomen when the patient changes position.

**Method:**

1. Patient lies supine.
2. Percuss from the central abdomen towards the flank.
3. Identify the transition from tympany to dullness.
4. Ask the patient to turn to one side.
5. Wait briefly for fluid to shift.
6. Percuss again.
7. A change in the area of dullness supports free fluid.

### B. Fluid Thrill

**Principle:** A wave transmitted through fluid can be felt on the opposite side of the abdomen.

**Method:**

1. Place one hand on one flank.
2. Ask an assistant or the patient to place the ulnar edge of a hand firmly along the midline.
3. Tap the opposite flank.
4. Feel for a transmitted impulse.

### C. Fluid Wave

A fluid wave may be elicited by tapping one side of the abdomen and feeling for transmission on the opposite side.

> [!question] Viva: What are the clinical methods used to detect ascites?
> **Answer:**
> - Shifting dullness.
> - Fluid thrill.
> - Fluid wave.

# 25. EXAMINATION OF THE GALLBLADDER

- Right hypochondrial mass.

## 25.2 Normal Anatomical Position of the Gallbladder

The fundus of the gallbladder is normally located near:

- Lateral to the lateral border of the rectus abdominis muscle.

## 25.3 Normal Gallbladder Versus Enlarged Palpable Gallbladder

The mentor made an important correction.

Do not simply use the normal gallbladder landmark to describe an enlarged palpable gallbladder.

The mentor emphasized that the examiner should stand at the patient's feet.

- Observe movement with respiration.
- Pay attention to craniocaudal movement.

The most important movement to appreciate is craniocaudal movement.

# 26. COURVOISIER'S LAW

The mentor emphasized that finding a palpable gallbladder in a jaundiced patient may change the diagnosis.

## 26.1 Statement

Courvoisier's law:

"In a patient with jaundice and a palpable gallbladder, the cause is more likely to be malignant obstruction than gallstone disease."

## 26.2 Explanation

In long-standing gallstone disease, repeated inflammation may make the gallbladder fibrotic and contracted.

Therefore, it may not become distended even when the common bile duct is obstructed.

In malignant obstruction, the gallbladder may remain distensible and become palpable.

## 26.3 Important Causes of a Palpable Gallbladder with Jaundice

- Carcinoma head of pancreas.
- Periampullary carcinoma.
- Distal common bile duct malignancy.

## 26.4 Limitations

Courvoisier's law is a clinical principle, not an absolute rule.

There are exceptions, and a palpable gallbladder does not automatically establish malignancy.

> [!question] Viva: What is Courvoisier's law?
> **Answer:** In a jaundiced patient, a palpable gallbladder is more suggestive of malignant extrahepatic biliary obstruction than gallstone disease.

# 30. TRIPHASIC CT SCAN

If ultrasound reveals a focal liver lesion, further characterization with contrast-enhanced CT or MRI is required.

The mentor specifically discussed triphasic CT in suspected HCC.

## 30.1 What Is Triphasic CT?

It is a contrast-enhanced CT examination that evaluates liver lesions during different vascular phases.

The discussion included:

1. Non-contrast phase.
2. Arterial phase.
3. Portal venous phase.
4. Delayed phase (washout phase).

Some descriptions refer to the study as triphasic because they count the three post-contrast phases.

## 30.2 Phases

| Phase | Main purpose |
|---|---|
| Non-contrast | Baseline appearance |
| Arterial phase | Detect arterial enhancement |
| Portal venous phase | Assess portal venous enhancement and lesion appearance |
| Delayed phase | Assess washout and delayed enhancement |

## 30.3 Characteristic Enhancement Pattern in HCC

A characteristic pattern in HCC is:

- Arterial phase hyperenhancement.
- Washout on portal venous or delayed imaging.

### Flowchart

    TRIPHASIC CT
          |
          v
    Arterial phase
          |
          v
    Arterial hyperenhancement
          |
          v
    Portal venous / delayed phase
          |
          v
    Washout
          |
          v
    Pattern supports HCC

## 30.4 Timing

The mentor discussed obtaining images at different time intervals after contrast administration.

Exact timing varies according to the imaging protocol and scanner.

## 30.5 Role in Secondary Liver Lesions

Delayed-phase imaging also helps characterize liver lesions and may demonstrate secondary deposits.

## 30.6 Is Biopsy Always Necessary?

No.

In an appropriate high-risk clinical setting, characteristic imaging findings may establish the diagnosis of HCC without biopsy.

The decision depends on the patient's risk profile, imaging pattern and current diagnostic criteria.

> [!question] Viva: What is the characteristic CT enhancement pattern of HCC?
> **Answer:** Arterial phase hyperenhancement followed by washout in the portal venous or delayed phase.

Cytology must be interpreted along with:

- Clinical findings.
- Imaging.
- Ascitic fluid analysis.

# 42. IMPORTANT TAKE-HOME POINTS FROM THE MENTOR

1. Start a clinical case presentation directly with age, sex, occupation and relevant particulars.
2. Analyse all chief complaints together before discussing them individually.
3. In abdominal distension, first differentiate localized from generalized distension.
4. Remember the five broad causes of generalized abdominal distension: fluid, gas, faeces, fat and fetus.
5. Ask about pedal oedema and diuretic use when considering fluid accumulation.
6. Do not simply list negative history; understand why each question is asked.
7. Always document alcohol quantity and duration.
8. Ask about blood transfusion, sexual exposure and other hepatitis risk factors.
9. Ask about dog contact when considering hydatid disease.
10. Describe an abdominal mass anatomically before assigning its organ of origin.
11. Describe the surface and edge separately.
12. Assess respiratory movement of a hepatic mass.
13. Do not forget to examine the spleen and other abdominal organs.
14. Assess ascites using appropriate clinical tests.
15. Examine the gallbladder specifically in a patient with jaundice and a palpable liver.
16. Remember the normal gallbladder surface landmark: right ninth costal cartilage.
17. For a palpable gallbladder mass, stand at the patient's feet and assess craniocaudal respiratory movement.
18. Remember Courvoisier's law and its limitations.
19. Investigations should confirm diagnosis, assess disease extent and determine fitness for treatment.
20. In suspected HCC, understand the triphasic CT enhancement pattern.
21. AFP and PIVKA-II are useful tumour markers but are not diagnostic in isolation.
22. A negative ascitic fluid cytology does not exclude malignancy.
23. Revise liver anatomy, surface anatomy, clinical examination, HCC and liver metastases.
