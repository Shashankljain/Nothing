# BREAST LUMP – SURGICAL CASE PRESENTATION
> **Subject:** General Surgery  
> **Topic:** Clinical examination and management of breast lump  
> **Case:** 50-year-old woman with right breast lump  
> **Format:** Tutor–Student Discussion  
> **Source:** Clinical case discussion by Dr. Ganesh Shamya

---

# 1. CASE INTRODUCTION

A 50-year-old woman, a housewife, presented with complaints of a lump in her right breast for 3 months.

### History of presenting illness

- Patient was apparently normal 3 months ago.
- Initially noticed a small lump in the right breast.
- No other symptoms at the time of onset.
- Lump gradually increased in size over 3 months.

> [!question] Tutor asks
> Is the age of the patient and painless nature of the lump important?

> [!success] Student answers
> Yes. A painless breast lump that is gradually increasing in size in a 50-year-old woman is highly suspicious of malignancy.

> [!important] Tutor's explanation
> Even if the lump had remained stable for 1–2 years, malignancy would still need to be considered. A slow-growing carcinoma is an important differential diagnosis.

## 1.1 Age-related breast lesions

| Lesion | Age group / occurrence mentioned in discussion |
|---|---|
| Hard fibroadenoma | 20–30 years |
| Soft fibroadenoma | 35–45 years |
| Fibroadenosis | More common around reproductive age, particularly near menarche and menopause |
| Carcinoma | Can occur at any age; more frequent after 40 years |

> [!tip] Viva pearl
> A new breast lump in a postmenopausal woman should always be investigated to exclude malignancy.

> [!warning] Transcription clarification
> The source contains the word "cervical" in the age-related discussion. This appears to be a transcription error; the intended term is unclear.

- No retraction.

> [!important] Tutor's explanation
> The discussion describes -
> - displacement of the nipple ==towards== the tumour as a sign suggestive of malignancy. 
> - Benign tumours may displace the nipple in the ==opposite== direction.

## 8.1 Nipple deviation vs displacement

| Feature | Deviation | Displacement |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Change in direction of the nipple | Actual change in the position of the nipple |
| Assessment | Direction in which the nipple points | Position compared with the opposite nipple |
| Measurement | Based on direction | Can be compared using distance from clavicle and midline |
| Timing mentioned | Earlier sign | May occur later |

> [!tip] Viva pearl
> The tutor emphasizes that deviation may appear before actual displacement.

## 8.2 Examination for nipple discharge

> [!question] Tutor asks
> How did you check for nipple discharge?

> [!success] Student answers

1. Stroke the breast from the periphery towards the centre in each quadrant.
2. Squeeze the areola beneath the nipple.
3. Examine the patient's clothing for evidence of discharge.

## 8.3 Nipple retraction

> [!question] Tutor asks
> Under what conditions does nipple retraction occur?

### Types of nipple retraction

| Congenital retraction                         | Acquired retraction                    |
| --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Present from birth                            | Develops recently                      |
| Usually bilateral                             | Usually unilateral                     |
| Due to impaired evagination of the nipple bud | Clinically significant                 |
| Usually not suggestive of malignancy          | May indicate ==carcinoma or fibrosis== |

### Causes of acquired retraction

1. Carcinoma of the breast
2. Chronic abscess

### Mechanism

| Condition | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Congenital | Impaired evagination of nipple bud during development |
| Carcinoma | Infiltration and contraction of the main milk ducts |
| Chronic abscess | Fibrosis and retraction of subareolar tissue |

## 10.2 Peau d'orange / Lemon-peel sign

> [!question] Tutor asks
> What is the lemon-peel symptom?

> [!success] Student answers
> It is a characteristic dimpled appearance of the skin resembling orange peel.

### Mechanism

```text
Carcinoma of breast
       |
       v
Obstruction of cutaneous lymphatic vessels
       |
       v
Oedema of dermis
       |
       v
Skin cannot expand around hair follicles
and sweat gland openings
       |
       v
Dimpling of skin
       |
       v
PEAU D'ORANGE
```

**Why are dimples seen?**

- Hair follicles and sweat gland openings are firmly attached to deeper structures.
- The surrounding skin becomes oedematous.
- These fixed points remain depressed, producing the characteristic appearance.

## 10.3 Ulceration of breast skin

The discussion mentions ulceration in:

- Carcinoma of the breast.
- Rapidly growing fibroadenoma.
- Phyllodes tumour.

### Mechanism in benign tumours

- Rapid tumour growth.
- Pressure on overlying skin.
- Atrophy of skin at the point of maximum tension.
- Ulcer formation.

### Clinical differentiation: pressure ulceration vs malignant skin infiltration

> [!question] Tutor asks
> How would you clinically distinguish between these two conditions?

> [!success] Student answers
> Try to pass a probe underneath the edge of the skin:
> - In a benign tumour with pressure ulceration, it may be possible.
> - In carcinoma with skin infiltration, it may not be possible.

# 14. SKIN TETHERING VS FUSION

Finding: The lump is connected to the skin but not fused to it.

> [!question] Tutor asks
> What does tethering mean?

> [!success] Student answers
> Tethering means the skin can be moved relative to the tumour to a limited extent, but it becomes retracted when:
> - Moving the skin.
> - Raising the arm above the head.
> - Trying to gather the skin into a fold over the tumour.

### Mechanism of tethering

- Carcinoma grows into Cooper's ligaments.
- Cooper's ligaments are attached to the skin.
- The skin becomes pulled or retracted.
- However, the tumour has not yet infiltrated the skin itself.

### Fusion of skin

- When tumour infiltration reaches the skin, the skin and tumour cannot be moved independently.
- The skin cannot be pinched and lifted above the tumour.
- The skin is said to be fused with the tumour.

## 14.1 Tethering vs fusion

| Feature | Tethering | Fusion |
|---|---|---|
| Skin mobility | Limited mobility present | No independent mobility |
| Skin movement | Skin retracts when moved | Skin and tumour move together |
| Pinching skin | May still be possible | Difficult or impossible |
| Pathology described | Involvement of Cooper's ligaments | Infiltration of skin |
| Significance | Suggestive of malignancy | Strongly suggestive of malignant skin involvement |

> [!question] Tutor asks
> What is the significance of fixation?

> [!success] Student answers
> Fixation to skin is almost a diagnostic sign of breast carcinoma, although it may sometimes be seen in traumatic fat necrosis and chronic abscess.

# 15. FIXATION TO BREAST TISSUE AND DEEP STRUCTURES

### Finding

- Lump is fixed in the breast tissue.
- Lump is fixed to the pectoralis muscle.
- Lump is not fixed to the chest wall.

## 15.1 How to assess fixation to breast tissue

> [!question] Tutor asks
> How did you check fixation to breast tissue?

> [!success] Student answers

1. Secure the breast tissue around the mass using the left hand.
2. Spread the thumb and remaining fingers on either side of the mass.
3. Press firmly to stabilize the breast tissue.
4. Try to move the mass in all directions within the fixed breast tissue.
5. If the mass remains motionless, it is fixed to the breast tissue.

## 15.2 How to assess fixation to pectoralis major

### Procedure

1. Ask the patient to keep her hand on the thigh on the same side.
2. Assess mobility of the mass along and perpendicular to the pectoral muscle fibres.
3. Ask the patient to press firmly with the hand against the thigh.
4. This contracts the pectoralis major muscle.
5. Reassess the mobility of the mass.

> [!important] Interpretation
> Limited mobility indicates fixation to the pectoralis major muscle or its fascia.

**Tutor's explanation:**

- A lump mobile over a relaxed muscle is not attached to the chest wall.
- Reduced mobility on muscle contraction suggests attachment to the pectoral muscle or fascia.

## 15.3 Assessment of serratus anterior

If the tumour is in the ==outer lower quadrant==, the serratus anterior muscle may also need to be examined.

### Procedure

1. Ask the patient to stand approximately 2 feet away from a wall.
2. Ask her to press against the wall with an outstretched arm on the affected side.
3. Check for restriction in the mobility of the lump.

### Summary of deep fixation examination

| Structure | Examination method |
|---|---|
| Breast tissue | Stabilize surrounding breast tissue and move the lump |
| Pectoralis major | Ask patient to press hand against thigh and contract muscle |
| Chest wall | Assess mobility over relaxed muscle |
| Serratus anterior | Ask patient to push against a wall |

# 16. AXILLARY LYMPH NODE EXAMINATION

### Findings

- One mobile, firm lymph node is palpable in the medial chain of the right axilla.
- Size: 3 × 2 cm.
- Other lymph nodes in both axillae are not palpable.
- Neck lymph nodes are not palpable.

> [!question] Tutor asks
> What groups of lymph nodes in the axilla did you examine?

> [!success] Student answers
> Five main groups:
> 1. Apical
> 2. Central
> 3. Pectoral / thoracic
> 4. Brachial
> 5. Subscapular

## 16.1 Five groups of axillary lymph nodes

| Group | Location |
|---|---|
| Apical | At the apex of the axilla |
| Central | Medially along the chest wall / central axilla |
| Pectoral (anterior / thoracic) | Beneath the anterior axillary fold |
| Brachial (humeral) | Laterally along the brachial vessels |
| Subscapular (posterior) | Beneath the posterior axillary fold |

## 16.2 Technique of axillary lymph node palpation

### Patient position

- Shoulder abducted.
- Forearm flexed.
- Patient's arm supported on the examiner's forearm.

### Sequence

| Step | Examination |
|---|---|
| 1 | Examiner holds the patient by the opposite shoulder with the right hand |
| 2 | Left hand palpates the central group by sliding fingers along the chest wall deep into the axilla |
| 3 | Fingers are pushed as high as possible to palpate the apical group |
| 4 | Hand is turned palm forward beneath the anterior axillary fold to palpate the pectoral group |
| 5 | Right hand is inserted into the axilla to palpate the brachial / shoulder group against the humerus |
| 6 | Examiner stands behind the patient and palpates the subscapular group beneath the posterior axillary fold |

## 16.3 Other lymph node groups

After axillary palpation:

1. Palpate the deltopectoral groove just below the clavicle.
2. Examine supraclavicular lymph nodes on both sides.
3. Stand behind the patient.
4. Place fingers behind the middle third of the clavicle.
5. Palpate the opposite axilla in a similar manner.

> [!tip] Viva pearl
> Always examine both axillae and the supraclavicular lymph nodes in a patient with a suspected breast malignancy.

# 19. MANCHESTER CLASSIFICATION OF BREAST CANCER

> [!question] Tutor asks
> Explain the Manchester classification of stages.

> [!success] Student answers
> It is a four-stage classification based on clinical examination.

## 19.1 Stages

| Stage     | Features described in the discussion                                                                                                                                                            |
| --------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Stage I   | Tumour limited to the breast. No palpable lymph nodes. Skin changes, if present, are directly over the tumour, connected to it and smaller than the tumour.                                     |
| Stage II  | Similar to Stage I, but palpable ==🔴mobile== ==ipsilateral axillary lymph== nodes are present.                                                                                                 |
| Stage III | Skin involvement larger than tumour size, ==fixation== or lemon-peel sign, skin ulceration, fixation to pectoral fascia or muscles, and fixed ipsilateral axillary lymph nodes.                 |
| Stage IV  | Fixation to chest wall, supraclavicular lymph node involvement, ==opposite axillary lymph node== involvement, distant metastasis, skin satellite nodules or involvement of the opposite breast. |

### Quick flowchart

```text
BREAST CARCINOMA
       |
       v
STAGE I
Tumour limited to breast
No palpable nodes
       |
       v
STAGE II
Mobile ipsilateral axillary nodes
       |
       v
STAGE III
Skin involvement / fixation
Pectoral muscle fixation
Fixed ipsilateral axillary nodes
       |
       v
STAGE IV
Distant spread or specified
advanced nodal / skin involvement
```

> [!warning] Important
> This is the historical Manchester classification as presented in the source. It should not be confused with the current AJCC TNM staging system.

# 20. STAGING VS GRADING

> [!question] Tutor asks
> What is the difference between staging and grading of a malignant neoplasm?

> [!success] Student answers

| Staging                                             | Grading                                            |
| --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Indicates ==invasiveness== and spread of the tumour | Indicates ==cellular== changes                     |
| Assesses extent of disease                          | Assesses ==degree of differentiation / anaplasia== |
| Includes tumour size, lymph nodes and metastasis    | Based on histological appearance of tumour cells   |

> [!tip] Viva pearl
> **Staging = Extent of spread.**  
> **Grading = Degree of cellular abnormality.**
