### Phase 3: Associated Pathologies, Occupational Mechanics, and Secondary Causes

- **Student Presentation of Associated Pathologies**:
    
    - No history of similar swelling in the opposite leg.
    - No history of bleeding per rectum (rules out ==hemorrhoids== / piles).
    - No history of scrotal swelling or dragging pain in the scrotum on prolonged standing (rules out ==varicocele==).
    - Occupational history: Works in a standing position without elastic stockings for 8 hours a day for the last 3 years.
- **Mentor Question**: Why does a standing position cause the development of varicose veins?
    
- **Student Response & Pathophysiology**:
    
    - In a standing position, the continuous weight of the blood column from the heart to the leg exerts continuous ==hydrostatic pressure== on the lower limb veins.
    - This continuous pressure produces dilation of the vein walls and ==increases the perimeter/circumference== of the vein, resulting in valvular incompetence and pathological **regurgitation of blood** from deep to superficial systems.
- **Mentor Question**: What are the common causes of secondary varicose veins?
    
- **Student Response**: Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pressure on the veins higher up.
    
- **Mentor Question**: What history did you ask for that point?
    
- **Student Response**:
    
    - _==For DVT==_: History of fever with calf pain and swelling, history of prolonged bed recumbency, recent fracture, or major surgical operation.
    - _==For pressure on veins==_: History of any lump in the abdomen or pelvis, and history of bladder or bowel disturbances.
    - _In ==Females==_: History of taking oral contraceptive pills (OCPs), and history of recent or present pregnancy accompanied by leg swelling.
- **Mentor Question**: How does pregnancy cause varicose veins?
    
- **Student Response & Hormonal/Mechanical Mechanics**:
    
    - Action of hormones **==Progesterone==** and **==Relaxin==**, which cause smooth muscle relaxation in venous walls.
    - In the last trimester, mechanical pressure of the gravid uterus on the iliac veins, and pressure of the colon due to constipation, aggravate the condition.
    - Varicosities arising purely during pregnancy usually decrease or regress significantly post-delivery.
- **Student Continuation of History**:
    
    - No history of taking any medical or surgical treatment for the swelling.
    - Personal history is not contributory.
    - No history of varicose veins in the family.
- **Mentor Question**: Is there any significance of family history in varicose veins?
    
- **Student Response**: Indicates a **congenital familial weakness of the walls and valves in the ==ileal veins==**, so that normal physiological pressure acts upon the saphenofemoral valve, which gives way early in life, causing primary varicose veins.

### Phase 4: Local Inspection Findings, Pulsatile Veins, Ankle Flare, Blowouts, and Hyperpigmentation

- **Student Presentation of Local Inspection**:
    
    - On inspection, there is a tortuous, dilated, non-pulsatile vessel extending from in front of the medial malleolus, running along the medial aspect of the leg and thigh up to the saphenous opening, with dilatation of the dorsal venous arch.
- **Mentor Question**: Can varicose veins be pulsatile?
    
- **Student Response**: **Yes sir, when secondary to an ==Arteriovenous (AV) fistul==a, varicose veins are pulsatile.**
    
- **Mentor Question**: Is an ankle flare present?
    
- **Student Response**: No.
    
- **Mentor Question**: What is an ankle flare?
    
- **Student Response & Definition**:
    
    - **Ankle Flare**: A ==diffuse, hemangiomatous==-like swelling/plexus feeling over the hollow between the medial malleolus and heel, produced by dilatation of small subcutaneous veins between the medial malleolus and heel.
- **Mentor Question**: And what is its significance?
    
- **Student Response**: Indicates **incompetence of the lower ankle perforators** (Cockett's perforators). Classically seen in varicose veins secondary to deep vein thrombosis (DVT).
    
- **Mentor Question**: And what is a blowout?
    
- **Student Response & Definition**:
    
    - **Blowout (Blow-out)**: A visible, localized, saccular dilatation of a superficial vein situated ==directly over an incompetent perforator==.
    - **Mechanism**: The forced spurt of blood through the perforator (which is at right angles to the superficial vein) produces bulging of the weak superficial vein wall, termed a blowout.
    - **Location**: Blowouts are usually situated over sites of constant medial leg perforators. A blowout over the saphenous opening is termed a **==Saphena Varix==**.
- **Student Inspection Continued**:
    
    - No dilated veins visible over the lower abdomen or hypogastrium.
- **Mentor Question**: If present and they collapse when the patient lies down or the leg is elevated, what is the significance?
    
- **Student Response**: Indicates **thrombosis or obstruction in the Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) or iliac veins**.
    
- **Student Inspection of Skin**:
    
    - Skin over the medial aspect of the lower third of the leg shows hyperpigmentation, scaly skin, no oozing, no ulceration, no edema. No deformities of ankle or foot.
- **Mentor Question**: Why is the skin pigmented?
    
- **Student Response & Pathophysiology**:
    
    - Due to increased ambulatory venous pressure, episodes occur with micro-extravasation of Red Blood Cells (RBCs) and high molecular weight proteins into the dermis.
    - Red cell breakdown releases **==hemosiderin==**.
    - Ingestion of hemosiderin by dermal ==macrophages== combined with a local melanocyte reaction in the skin produces characteristic **dark brown hyperpigmentation** in the lower third of the leg.
- **Mentor Question**: What is stasis eczema?
    
- **Student Response & Definition**:
    
    - **Stasis Eczema**: A non-contagious inflammatory condition of the skin in response to exogenous or endogenous stimuli like ==dry skin, trauma, scratching, or application of herbal preparations==. Skin is dry, scaly, and oozing.

#### Clinical Demonstration of Specific Diagnostic Tests

- **1. Cough Impulse Test at Saphenous Opening**:
    
    - Student: Cough impulse test is positive.
    - **Mentor Question**: What is the cough impulse test?
    - **Student Response**: With the patient in a standing position, the dip of the middle finger is placed lightly just below the saphenous opening.
    - **Mentor Question**: How do you locate the saphenous opening?
    - **Student Response**: The saphenous opening is **==3.8 cm (1.5 inches) below and lateral to the pubic tubercle==**.
    - **Procedure**: Pressure is applied just below the saphenous opening, and the patient is asked to cough. If an expansile fluid thrill is felt under the finger, the test is positive and indicates **incompetence of the saphenofemoral valve**.
- **2. Couvelier's Sign & Saphena Varix**:
    
    - **Mentor Question**: Is it the same as Cruveilhier's sign?
    - **Student Response**: No, Cruveilhier's sign is a specific test for **Saphena Varix**.
    - **Mentor Question**: What is Saphena Varix?
    - **Student Response**: It is a single varicosity or blowout situated close to the saphenous opening.
    - **Mentor Question**: What is Cruveilhier's sign?
    - **Student Response**: Fingers are placed over the saphena varix and the patient is asked to cough. A fluid thrill is felt resembling a **"cat's purr"** or the feeling of a jet of water. Indicates incompetence of the saphenofemoral valve.
    - **Mentor Question**: What is its clinical utility?
    - **Student Response**: Differentiates a saphena varix from a femoral hernia.

#### Differentiating Saphena Varix from Femoral Hernia

| Diagnostic Feature        | Saphena Varix                                                    | Femoral Hernia                               |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Consistency**           | Extremely soft, easily collapsible                               | Firm, doughy, or tympanitic                  |
| **Skin Appearance**       | ==Bluish== tinge visible through thin skin                       | Normal skin color                            |
| **Cough Impulse Sign**    | Positive **Cruveilhier's Sign** (fluid thrill like a cat's purr) | Expansile cough impulse (no fluid thrill)    |
| **Reducibility Behavior** | Disappears ==immediately== on supine posture                     | Requires ==manual== reduction or irreducible |

### Phase 7: Morrissey's Test, Trendelenburg Test Variations, and Eponyms

- **4. Morrissey's Test**:
    
    - **Mentor Question**: What is Morrissey's test?
    - **Student Response**: Make the patient lie down supine, elevate the leg to 30° to 45° to drain the veins, then apply digital pressure over the saphenous opening with the ==thumb== and ask the patient to stand up and ==cough==. If the saphenofemoral valve is incompetent, retrograde venous pressure causes blood to rise slowly under the thumb or fall back rapidly when the thumb is released.
- **5. Trendelenburg Tourniquet Test (Trendelenburg I & II)**:
    
    - **Mentor Question**: How do you perform the Trendelenburg test?
    - **Student Response & Procedure**:
        1. Make the patient lie down supine and empty the veins completely by elevating the leg to 30°.
        2. Maintain pressure with the thumb (or a tourniquet) over the saphenous opening (3.8 cm below and lateral to the pubic tubercle).
        3. Ask the patient to stand up quickly.
        4. **First Part of Test**: Maintain pressure for 30 seconds. If veins fill up rapidly from below, it indicates incompetence of ==perforating veins==.
        5. **Second Part of Test**: Release pressure after 30 seconds. If veins fill up rapidly from above downward, it indicates incompetence of the ==saphenofemoral valve.==

#### Trendelenburg Test Interpretation Matrix

| Test Result                       | Behavior WITH Pressure (30 sec)                        | Behavior AFTER Release of Pressure          | Anatomical Interpretation                                |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Trendelenburg Positive**        | Veins remain empty                                     | Veins fill **rapidly from ABOVE DOWNWARD**  | **Isolated Saphenofemoral Junction (SFJ) Incompetence**  |
| **Trendelenburg Double Positive** | Veins fill **rapidly from BELOW UPWARD** within 30 sec | Veins fill **EVEN MORE RAPIDLY from above** | **Combined SFJ & Perforator Incompetence**               |
| **Trendelenburg Nil**             | Veins fill rapidly from below upward                   | No additional rapid filling from above      | **Isolated ==Perforator== Incompetence** (SFJ Competent) |
| **Trendelenburg Negative**        | Veins fill slowly from below (>30 sec)                 | No rapid filling from above                 | **Normal (Competent SFJ and Perforators)**               |

- **Mentor Question**: Is there any fallacy to this test?
- **Student Response**: Pressure applied at the ==wrong site or inadequate pressure== gives a false positive result. Pressure applied too tightly may occlude deep veins or adjacent perforators.

- **6. Multiple Tourniquet Test (Pratt's / Comparative Tourniquet Test)**:
    - **Mentor Question**: Next test?
    - **Student Response**: Multiple tourniquet test or comparative tourniquet test. Demonstrates incompetence of saphenofemoral, Hunterian, Dodd's, and Boyd's perforators.
    - **Method**: Patient lies down supine, leg elevated to empty veins. Apply 3 to 4 tourniquets:
        1. Tourniquet 1: Just below saphenous opening (tests **Hunterian / Mid-thigh perforator**).

        3. Tourniquet 3: Just below knee (tests **Boyd's / Below-knee perforator**).
        4. Tourniquet 4: Lower leg (tests **Cockett's / Ankle perforators**).
    - Isolate segments to observe which specific segment fills rapidly when the patient stands up.

### Phase 8: Deep Vein Examination & Perthes Test Decision Rules

- **Examination for Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)**:
    - **Mentor Question**: Examination for deep vein thrombosis?
    - **Student Response**: Look for calf swelling, tenderness along the line of posterior tibial veins, popliteal vein, and femoral vein. Perform Homan's sign, Ochsner's / Neuhof's sign, and Perthes test.

#### Clinical Tests for Active DVT

1. **Homan's Sign**: Passive dorsiflexion of the foot with the knee extended produces severe pain in the calf muscle.
2. **Mose’s sign** - squeezing calf muscle side to side , severe pain
3. **Ochsner's / Neuhof's Sign (Compression Sign)**: Direct anteroposterior compression / squeezing of the relaxed calf muscle against the tibia elicits deep localized tenderness.
4. **Posterior Tibial Vein Palpation**: Patient lies supine, knee flexed, foot flat on the couch (relaxing calf muscles). Apply direct pressure with the flat hand over the calf against the tibia bone; ==deep tenderness== is elicited if thrombosis is present.

---

#### Perthes Test (Deep Vein Patency Test)

- **Mentor Question**: What is Perthes test?
- **Student Response**: Perthes test is performed to detect occlusion of deep veins. Recanalization after DVT takes time, but if ==deep vein occlusion persists==, Perthes test is positive.
- **Procedure**:
    1. Apply a tourniquet around the upper thigh (just below SFJ) to occlude superficial veins.
    2. Ask the patient to walk briskly for about 5 minutes (or perform 10 to 12 heel raises).
- **Interpretation**:
    - **Normal / Negative Perthes Test**: Superficial varicose veins **collapse and empty completely** because the active calf muscle pump propels blood into patent deep veins through competent perforators.
    - **Positive Perthes Test**: Superficial veins become **MORE engorged, tense, and painful** because blood cannot pass through blocked deep veins and is forced outward into superficial veins.

- **Mentor Question**: If Perthes test is positive, does it mean anything to you?
- **Student Response & CRITICAL VIVA WARNING**:
>     - **IMPORTANT**: **A Positive Perthes Test is an ==ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION TO SURGERY OR SCLEROTHERAPY==!**
    - **Reason**: Because deep veins are blocked, superficial varicose veins are acting as the sole collateral pathways for venous return. Their removal or ligation will be catastrophic, resulting in **==VENOUS GANGRENE==**.

### Phase 9: Final Diagnosis, Diagnostic Workup, and Complete Treatment Protocols

- **Systemic Screening Completed**:
    
    - No enlargement of inguinal lymph nodes, no intra-abdominal mass, no scrotal swelling, testicles normal, no varicocele.
    - Rectal examination & proctoscopy: Requested to rule out co-existing hemorrhoids (piles).
- **Final Clinical Diagnosis Formulation**:
    
    > **"Primary uncomplicated varicose veins of the left long saphenous venous system with incompetence of the saphenofemoral valve and technical / below-knee perforators, accompanied by hyperpigmentation of the lower leg skin."**
    
- **Definition of Varicose Veins**:
    
    - Dilated, elongated, and tortuous veins of the superficial venous system.
    - **Common Anatomical Sites of Varicosities**:
        1. Long Saphenous System (lower limb).
        2. Short Saphenous System (lower limb).
        3. Lower Esophageal Veins (Esophageal varices in portal hypertension).
        4. Pampiniform Plexus (Varicocele in scrotum).
        5. Superior Hemorrhoidal Plexus (Piles / Hemorrhoids).

---

#### Diagnostic Workup Protocol

1. **Routine Preoperative Tests**: Complete Blood Count (CBC), Urine Routine, Blood Sugar level, Chest X-ray, ECG.
2. **Specific Imaging & Diagnostic Modalities**:
    - **==Color Doppler Ultrasonography==**: **Gold standard, non-invasive imaging modality**. Precisely maps the anatomical location of incompetent SFJ, SPJ, and perforator sites, and verifies deep vein patency.
    - **Ascending / Descending Venography (Phlebography)**: Radiopaque contrast injected into superficial foot veins with an ankle tourniquet applied. Used when Doppler findings are inconclusive or deep venous anomalies are suspected.
    - **==I-131 Fibrinogen Test & Serum D-Dimer==**: Performed if active DVT is clinically suspected.

---

#### Operative & Interventional Management Protocol

1. **Standard Open Surgical Repair**:
    
    - **Trendelenburg Operation (Flush Ligation)**: High ligation of the Long Saphenous Vein flush at the Saphenofemoral Junction. All 5 anatomical tributaries must be identified and divided flush at the femoral vein to prevent recurrence:
        1. Superficial Epigastric vein.
        2. Superficial Circumflex Iliac vein.
        3. Superficial External Pudendal vein.
        4. Deep External Pudendal vein.
        5. Accessory Saphenous vein.
    - **Stripping of the Long Saphenous Vein**: Performed using a flexible **Myers Stripper** from the groin down to just below the knee level (avoids saphenous nerve injury below the knee).
    - **Subfascial Perforator Ligation**:
        - _Open Method_: Cockett & Linton subfascial perforator ligation.
        - _Endoscopic Method_: **SEPS (Subfascial Endoscopic Perforator Surgery)** — subfascial visualization and clipping of perforators through small endoscopic ports.
2. **Endovenous Thermal Ablation (EVLT / RFA)**:
    
    - **EVLT (Endovenous Laser Therapy)** or **RFA (Radiofrequency Ablation)**: Catheter inserted into the vein under ultrasound guidance; thermal energy ablates the endothelial lining, producing vein occlusion. Superior cosmetic and functional outcomes.
3. **Injection Sclerotherapy (Compression Sclerotherapy)**:
    
    - **Indications**: Small, isolated residual or spider varicosities **==<3 mm in diameter==**.
    - **ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION**: Incompetent Saphenofemoral Junction (SFJ) or main trunk varicosities. Sclerotherapy without SFJ ligation leads to rapid recurrence.
    - **Sclerosant Agents**: Sodium Tetradecyl Sulfate (STSD) or Polidocanol.
    - **Mechanism**: Sclerosant injected into an empty vein causes chemical endothelial destruction, followed by firm compression bandaging for 3 to 6 weeks to induce fibrotic occlusion.

---

#### Management Protocol for Varicose Ulcer

- **Golden Rule**: _"All varicose ulcers will heal with rest and leg elevation."_ The main clinical challenge is preventing recurrence.
- **Conservative Protocol**:==BISGARD’s method==
    1. Wound swab culture and sensitivity, broad-spectrum antibiotics.
    2. Complete bed rest with leg elevation above heart level.
    3. Regular saline dressings and gentle massage of leg tissues surrounding the ulcer towards the heart.
    4. Active foot, toe, and ankle movements to promote calf muscle pump action.
    5. Compression bandaging (**==Unna's Boot==**) to reduce chronic edema.
- **Definitive Surgery**: Once the ulcer has completely healed, proceed with **Trendelenburg operation and subfascial ligation of all incompetent perforators** (open or SEPS) to prevent ulcer recurrence.

---

#### Absolute and Relative Contraindications to Varicose Vein Surgery

1. Active Deep Vein Thrombosis (Positive Perthes test).
2. Pregnancy (relative contraindication).
3. Recent attack of superficial thrombophlebitis (<3 months).
4. Severe peripheral arterial occlusive disease.
5. Unfit, bedridden, or elderly patients with limited life expectancy.

### II. Summary of All Important Facts Mentioned

- Surgery or sclerotherapy must be postponed for at least 3 months following superficial thrombophlebitis.
- Pulsatile varicose veins indicate an underlying Arteriovenous (AV) Fistula.

- Saphena varix exhibits Cruveilhier's sign (fluid thrill on coughing like a cat's purr).

- Trendelenburg operation requires flush ligation of LSV at SFJ with division of all 5 tributaries.
- Injection sclerotherapy is strictly contraindicated if SFJ is incompetent or vein diameter is >3 mm.
- All varicose ulcers heal with rest and leg elevation; surgery is performed after ulcer healing to prevent recurrence.
