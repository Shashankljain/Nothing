## 1. Definition, Core Questions & Etiological Classification of Stroke

### World Health Organization (WHO) Definition of Stroke

- **Stroke Definition**: An acute onset or rapidly progressing focal neurological deficit (very rarely a global deficit) attributable strictly to a **vascular etiology**. The official WHO definition states that stroke is a focal neurological deficit that _cannot be attributed to anything other than vascular cause_.
- **Core Diagnostic Questions in Neurology**: Whenever evaluating a patient with hemiplegia or a cerebrovascular accident, two fundamental questions must be answered:
    1. **Nature of Illness**: Is the lesion ischemic (thrombotic vs. embolic) or hemorrhagic (intracerebral vs. subarachnoid bleed)?
    2. **Site of Illness (Site of Lesion / Localization)**: Where along the neuraxis (cortex, internal capsule, subcortex, brainstem, or spinal cord) is the anatomical lesion located?

## 2. Differentiating Nature of Illness from History & Risk Factor Analysis

### History-Based Differentiating Criteria

```
                        DIFFERENTIATING NATURE OF STROKE
                                       │
     ┌─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                 ▼                                 ▼
Thrombotic Stroke                 Embolic Stroke                    Hemorrhagic Stroke
• Occurs at 4:00 AM (sleep)       • Occurs during physical activity • Occurs during physical activity
• Stepwise progression             • Maximum deficit at onset        • Rapid progression with edema
• Ischemic core & penumbra        • Sudden embolus lodgment         • Signs of raised ICP present
```

1. **Timing of Onset**:
    - **Thrombotic Stroke**: Classically occurs **early morning around 4:00 AM** while the patient is asleep; the patient wakes up with the neurological deficit.
        - _Pathophysiological Mechanism_: Endothelial expression of **Plasminogen Activator Inhibitor-1 (PAI-1)** peaks around 4:00 AM, suppressing native fibrinolysis (tissue plasminogen activator activity) and favoring thrombosis.
    - **Embolic & Hemorrhagic Strokes**: Classically occur **during physical activity**; they do not occur during sleep.
2. **Progression of Deficit**:
    - **Embolic Stroke**: The neurological deficit is **MAXIMUM AT ONSET**. There is no progression of weakness after the initial sudden event.
    - **Thrombotic Stroke**: Characterized by a **stepwise progression ("stroke in progression")**. The central ischemic core is surrounded by an **ischemic penumbra**; as the penumbra progressively succumbs to ischemia, the clinical deficit worsens over hours.
    - **Hemorrhagic Stroke**: Deficit progresses rapidly over minutes to hours due to expanding hematoma volume and surrounding vasogenic edema.
3. **Signs of Raised Intracranial Pressure (ICP)**:
    - Presence of severe headache, projectile vomiting, and deteriorating sensorium points strongly toward **Hemorrhagic Stroke** (or a massive ischemic infarction with mass effect).

### Risk Factor Profiling & Prothrombotic Etiologies

- **Non-Modifiable Thrombotic Risk Factors**: Advanced age, male gender.
- **Hypertension**: **95% of strokes in hypertensive patients are THROMBOTIC**. (Hypertension is also the leading cause of non-traumatic intracerebral hemorrhage when uncontrolled).
- **Modifiable Thrombotic Risk Factors**: Diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, cigarette smoking, alcohol abuse, post-menopausal state.
- **Prothrombotic States in Young Patients (Age <45 Years)**:
    - _Physiological / Pharmacological_: Pregnancy, Oral Contraceptive Pills (OCPs).
    - _Hematological_: Antiphospholipid Antibody Syndrome (APLA), Inherited Thrombophilias (Protein C/S deficiency, Factor V Leiden), Polycythemia vera, Leukemias, Essential Thrombocythemia.
    - _Metabolic_: **Hyperhomocysteinemia** (extremely common in India, especially among **pure vegetarians** due to Nutritional **Vitamin B12 deficiency**).
    - _Infectious Vasculitis_: **Tuberculosis (TB)** and **Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)** (the two commonest infectious causes of cerebral vasculitis and stroke in India).
- **Hemorrhagic Stroke Subtypes & Locations**:
    - **Hypertensive Intracerebral Hemorrhage**: 90% occur in deep gray structures: **Basal Ganglia / Putamen**(commonest site), **Pons**, **Thalamus**, and **Cerebellum**. **IMPORTANT**: _Hypertension NEVER produces lobar hemorrhage_.
    - **Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy**: Pathognomonic cause of **Lobar Hemorrhage** in elderly non-hypertensive patients (e.g., 85-year-old presenting with lobar bleed).
    - **Berry Aneurysms**: Located in the Circle of Willis at the base of the brain; rupture produces **Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)**, not parenchymal hemorrhage.
    - **Arteriovenous (AV) Malformations**: Most common cause of parenchymal intracerebral hemorrhage in young patients.

## 3. Fundamental Terminology in Stroke Localization

```
                         HEMIPLEGIA TERMINOLOGY MATRIX
                                       │
     ┌─────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                 ▼                                 ▼
Dense Hemiplegia                  Brachial Hemiplegia               Crural Hemiplegia
• Upper Limb = Lower Limb         • Upper Limb > Lower Limb         • Lower Limb > Upper Limb
• Power equal in both             • MCA Cortical Territory          • ACA Cortical Territory
• Internal Capsule Pathognomonic
```

- **Hemiplegia vs. Hemiparesis**:
    - **Hemiplegia**: Complete paralysis of one side of the body (Muscle Power = **0/5**).
    - **Hemiparesis**: Partial weakness of one side of the body (Muscle Power = **1/5, 2/5, 3/5, or 4/5**, but not 0/5).
- **Dense Hemiplegia [IMPORTANT]**:
    - _Definition_: Equal grade of weakness in both the upper limb and the lower limb (Upper Limb Power = Lower Limb Power, e.g., both 0/5, or dense hemiparesis with both 3/5).
    - _Clinical Significance_: **Dense hemiplegia is PATHOGNOMONIC of an INTERNAL CAPSULE lesion**. The entire corticospinal tract supplying the contralateral half of the body is tightly packed into a very small area at the genu and posterior limb of the internal capsule.
- **Non-Dense Hemiplegia**:
    - **Brachial Hemiplegia**: Hemiplegia where the **Upper Limb (and face) is significantly more involved than the Lower Limb**. Indicates a **Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA)** cortical lesion.
    - **Crural Hemiplegia**: Hemiplegia where the **Lower Limb is significantly more involved than the Upper Limb**. Indicates an **Anterior Cerebral Artery (ACA)** cortical lesion.
- **Crossed vs. Uncrossed Hemiplegia**:
    - _Decussation Benchmark_: Pyramidal tract decussation occurs at the **lower medullary junction**.
    - **Crossed Hemiplegia**: Lesion is **ABOVE the decussation** (in the midbrain, pons, or upper medulla). Cranial nerve deficit is on one side (ipsilateral to lesion), while hemiplegia is on the opposite side (contralateral to lesion).
    - **Uncrossed Hemiplegia**: Lesion is **BELOW the decussation** (in the lowermost medulla). Cranial nerve deficit (e.g., CN 12) and hemiplegia occur on the **SAME side** as the lesion.
    - **Cruciate Hemiplegia**: Rare lesion occurring **AT the decussation**. Produces upper limb weakness on one side and lower limb weakness on the opposite side (cross pattern).

## 4. Anatomical Sites of Hemiplegia & Functional Anatomy

### Sites of Hemiplegia

```
                           ANATOMICAL SITES OF HEMIPLEGIA
                                          │
     ┌────────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                    ▼                                    ▼
Anterior Circulation                      Posterior Circulation                Spinal Cord
• Internal Capsule (Most common)          • Midbrain                           • Cervical Cord
• Cortex                                  • Pons                                 (Above C4 level)
• Subcortex                               • Medulla
```

1. **Internal Capsule** (Most common overall site).
2. **Cortex**.
3. **Subcortex** (Watershed areas).
4. **Brainstem**: Midbrain, Pons, Medulla.
5. **Spinal Cord**: Must be in the **cervical cord ABOVE C4 level**.
    - _Anatomical Reason_: C5 is the root value for the motor supply of the deltoid muscle (upper limb motor entry). A spinal cord lesion below C4 spares the upper limb fibers, producing paraplegia rather than hemiplegia.

### Pyramidal Tract Pathway & Functional Homunculus

```
                         CORTICOSPINAL TRACT PATHWAY
                                      │
     ┌────────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                                                 ▼
Motor Cortex (Betz Cells)                                       Internal Capsule
  ├─ Medial Surface (ACA) → Leg Representation                    ├─ Genu & Posterior Limb
  └─ Lateral Surface (MCA) → Face, Arm, Trunk                     └─ Fibers tightly packed
                                                                       │
                                                                       ▼
                                                                Brainstem & Cord
                                                                  ├─ Medial Midbrain
                                                                  ├─ Medial Pons
                                                                  ├─ Medial Medulla (Decussation)
                                                                  └─ Lateral Corticospinal Tract
```

- **Motor Homunculus Arrangement**:
    - **Medial Cortical Surface**: Controls the **Leg / Lower Limb**. Supplied by the **Anterior Cerebral Artery (ACA)**.
    - **Lateral Cortical Surface**: Controls the **Face, Arm, and Trunk**. Supplied by the **Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA)**.
- **Corticospinal Pathway**: Arises from Betz cells in the precentral gyrus → passes down as the corona radiata → converges into the **genu and posterior limb of the internal capsule** → descends medially through the cerebral peduncle of the **midbrain** → basis pontis of the **pons** → pyramid of the **medulla** → decussates at the lower medullary junction → enters the spinal cord as the lateral corticospinal tract.

## 5. Rules of Fours (Gates' Rules) for Brainstem Localization

Dr. Archith Boloor outlines the fundamental **Rules of Fours** for brainstem organization:

```
                           THE RULES OF FOURS (GATES)
                                       │
     ┌───────────────────┬─────────────┴─────────────┬───────────────────┐
     ▼                   ▼                           ▼                   ▼
Rule 1: Anatomic    Rule 2: Medial      Rule 3: Medial 4 M's        Rule 4: Lateral 4 S's
Location            CN Nuclei (÷12)     (Medial Medullary)          (Lateral Medullary)
• 4 Above Pons      • CN 3, 4, 6, 12    • Motor pathway             • Spinothalamic tract
  (CN 1, 2, 3, 4)   • Located medially    (Corticospinal)           • Spinal tract of CN 5
• 4 In Pons           with pyramidal    • Motor CN (12)             • Sympathetic tract
  (CN 5, 6, 7, 8)     tract             • MLF                       • Spinocerebellar tract
• 4 In Medulla                          • Medial lemniscus
  (CN 9, 10, 11, 12)
```

1. **Rule 1: Distribution of Cranial Nerves**:
    - **4 Above Pons**: CN 1, 2 (direct brain extensions), CN 3, 4 (Midbrain).
    - **4 In Pons**: CN 5, 6, 7, 8.
    - **4 In Medulla**: CN 9, 10, 11, 12.
2. **Rule 2: Medial Cranial Nerve Nuclei (Divisors of 12)**:
    - **Only 4 cranial nerve nuclei are located MEDIALLY**: **CN 3, 4, 6, and 12** (all numbers divide evenly into 12, except 1 and 2).
    - _Clinical Significance_: Because the corticospinal tract runs medially in the brainstem, medial vascular strokes involve these 4 motor cranial nerves along with hemiplegia.
    - All other cranial nerve nuclei (CN 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11) are located **LATERALLY** in the brainstem.

## 6. Cranial Nerve Involvement & Facial Nerve Exception Mechanics

### Why Most Cranial Nerves Are Spared in Single UMN Strokes

- Most cranial nerve motor nuclei receive **bilateral corticonuclear input** (from both right and left motor cortices).
- A unilateral hemisphere or internal capsule stroke does NOT produce cranial nerve palsies because the ipsilateral uninjured cortex continues to supply the nucleus.
- _Exceptions_: Facial Nerve (CN 7) lower face supply and Hypoglossal Nerve (CN 12) genioglossus supply.

---

### Facial Nerve Nucleus Innervation Mechanics

```
                       FACIAL NERVE NUCLEUS INNERVATION
                                       │
     ┌─────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                                                   ▼
Upper Part of Nucleus                                             Lower Part of Nucleus
• Receives BILATERAL cortical supply                              • Receives CONTRALATERAL cortical supply ONLY
• Supplies UPPER FACE (Forehead & Eyes)                           • Supplies LOWER FACE (Mouth angle & cheek)
• SPARED in UMN Stroke                                            • PARALYZED in UMN Stroke
```

- **Upper Motor Neuron (UMN) 7th Nerve Palsy**:
    - Lesion is located above the pontine nucleus (cortex or internal capsule).
    - **Upper face is SPARED** (patient can wrinkle forehead and close eye tightly) because the upper nucleus receives bilateral cortical input.
    - **Contralateral lower face is PARALYZED** (drooping of angle of mouth, flattening of nasolabial fold) because the lower nucleus receives contralateral input ONLY.
- **Lower Motor Neuron (LMN) 7th Nerve Palsy**:
    - Lesion involves the facial nerve nucleus in the pons or the peripheral nerve.
    - **Entire ipsilateral half of the face is PARALYZED** (both upper face and lower face involved; inability to wrinkle forehead + mouth drooping).

## 7. Exhaustive Step-by-Step Stroke Localization

### A. Internal Capsule Stroke

```
                       INTERNAL CAPSULE STROKE QUADRAD
                                       │
     ┌───────────────────┬─────────────┴─────────────┬───────────────────┐
     ▼                   ▼                           ▼                   ▼
1. Contralateral     2. Contralateral UMN        3. Contralateral    4. Contralateral
   Dense Hemiplegia     7th Nerve Palsy             Hemisensory Loss    Homonymous Hemianopia
   (UL = LL)            (Lower face)                (Thalamocortical)   (Optic Radiation)
```

1. **Vascular Anatomy & End Artery Status**:
    - Internal capsule is supplied by multiple arterial systems: **Lenticulostriate branches of MCA** (Charcot's artery of cerebral hemorrhage), **Recurrent artery of Heubner (branch of ACA)**, **Anterior Choroidal Artery (branch of ICA)**, and **Posterior Communicating Artery**.
    - All these perforating vessels are **END ARTERIES** with zero collateral anastomoses.
2. **Clinical Features**:
    - **Contralateral Dense Hemiplegia** (UL Power = LL Power).
    - **Contralateral UMN 7th Nerve Palsy** (lower face drooping).
    - **Contralateral Hemisensory Loss** (involvement of thalamocortical sensory projections in the posterior limb).
    - **Contralateral Homonymous Hemianopia** (involvement of optic radiation fibers looping around the capsule).
3. **The Hemorrhage Diagnostic Rule [IMPORTANT]**:
    - Individual arterial ischemic occlusions usually involve isolated limbs/sections of the internal capsule, so patients rarely show all 4 features simultaneously.
    - **IMPORTANT**: If a patient presents with **ALL 4 CLASSICAL FEATURES** of an internal capsule stroke simultaneously, the etiology is **HEMORRHAGIC STROKE** (or a space-occupying hematoma/mass compressing the entire capsule).

---

### B. Cortical Stroke

1. **Anterior Cerebral Artery (ACA) Cortical Infarct**:
    - Involves the medial surface of the motor cortex (leg area).
    - Produces **Crural Dominant Hemiplegia** (Lower Limb weakness significantly greater than Upper Limb; monoplegia of lower limb).
2. **Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA) Cortical Infarct**:
    - Involves the lateral surface of the motor cortex (face and arm areas).
    - Produces **Brachial Dominant Hemiplegia** (Upper Limb and Face weakness significantly greater than Lower Limb).
3. **Pathognomonic Cortical Signs**:
    - **Aphasia**: Occurs in **Dominant Hemisphere** (Left hemisphere in >95% of individuals) lesions.
        - _Superior Division MCA Infarct_: **Broca's Expressive Aphasia** (non-fluent speech, preserved comprehension).
        - _Inferior Division MCA Infarct_: **Wernicke's Receptive Aphasia** (fluent paraphasic speech, impaired comprehension).
        - _Main MCA Stem Infarct_: **Global Aphasia**.
    - **Apraxia**: Inability to execute previously learned skilled motor tasks, characteristic of non-dominant parietal/frontal cortical lesions.
    - **Seizures [IMPORTANT]**: Presence of a seizure at onset or during history **DEFINITIVELY LOCALIZES THE LESION TO THE CORTEX**. The ischemic/necrotic border zone acts as an irritable epileptogenic focus.
    - **Hemi-Neglect (Spatial Agnosia)**: Pathognomonic sign of **Non-Dominant (Right) Parietal Lobe Infarct**. The patient denies ownership of the left side of their body and turns their head persistently to the right.
4. **Internal Carotid Artery (ICA) Occlusion**:
    - Produces complete MCA + ACA territory deficits (global aphasia, dense hemiplegia, hemisensory loss) PLUS **Ipsilateral Monocular Blindness / Amaurosis Fugax** (due to occlusion of the **Ophthalmic Artery**, the first intra-cranial branch of ICA).
5. **Unpaired Anterior Cerebral Artery (ACA) Thrombosis**:
    - Anatomical variant where a single dominant ACA supplies both medial cortical hemispheres via the anterior communicating artery.
    - Occlusion of an unpaired ACA produces **PARAPLEGIA** (bilateral lower limb weakness mimicking a spinal cord lesion).

---

### C. Subcortical Stroke

- Occurs in **Watershed / Borderzone Territories** between major arterial supply zones (ACA-MCA or MCA-PCA) during episodes of systemic severe hypotension or multi-infarct states.
- Clinically produces partial motor deficits without cortical signs (no aphasia, no seizures) and without capsular density; confirmed on MRI.

---

### D. Brainstem Syndromes (Posterior Circulation)

#### 1. Midbrain Strokes

```
                           WEBER SYNDROME (MIDBRAIN)
                                       │
     ┌─────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                                                   ▼
Ipsilateral CN 3 Ophthalmoplegia                                 Contralateral Hemiplegia
• Ptosis, Mydriasis, Down-and-Out Eye                            • Upper Limb & Lower Limb Weakness
```

- **Weber Syndrome** (Ventral Midbrain Infarct):
    - **Ipsilateral 3rd Nerve Palsy**: Ptosis, dilated non-reactive pupil, eye fixed in "down and out" position.
    - **Contralateral Hemiplegia**: Involves the corticospinal tract in the cerebral peduncle.
- **Other Midbrain Syndromes**:
    - _Claude / Benedict Syndromes_: Involvement of **Red Nucleus** → produces **Ipsilateral CN 3 palsy + Contralateral Coarse Intention Tremors / Ataxia**.
    - _Hemi-Parkinsonism_: Involvement of **Substantia Nigra** → produces unilateral rigidity and resting tremors.
    - _Parinaud Syndrome (Dorsal Midbrain / Tectal Lesion)_: Involves pre-tectal area → produces **Upgaze Palsy**, pupil dilation, light-near dissociation, and **Convergence-Retraction Nystagmus (Kestenbaum Sign / Koerber-Salus Sign)**.

---

#### 2. Pontine Strokes

```
                        MILLARD-GUBLER SYNDROME (PONS)
                                       │
     ┌─────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                                                   ▼
Ipsilateral LMN 7th Nerve Palsy                                  Contralateral Hemiplegia
• Complete half-face paralysis (Forehead + Mouth)                • Pyramidal tract involvement
• ± Ipsilateral CN 6 Palsy (Lateral rectus)
```

- **Millard-Gubler Syndrome** (Ventral Pontine Infarct):
    - **Ipsilateral LMN 7th Nerve Palsy**: Entire half of the face is paralyzed (inability to wrinkle forehead + mouth drooping).
    - **Ipsilateral 6th Nerve Palsy**: Paralysis of lateral rectus muscle (eye cannot abduct).
    - **Contralateral Hemiplegia**: Corticospinal tract involvement in basis pontis.
- **Pontine Hemorrhage Triad**:
    1. **Pinpoint Pupils** (loss of descending sympathetic fibers / bilateral Horner's).
    2. **Hyperpyrexia** (disruption of hypothalamic thermoregulatory pathways).
    3. **Rapid Coma / Quadriplegia**.

---

#### 3. Medullary Strokes

```
                         MEDULLARY STROKE SYNDROMES
                                      │
     ┌────────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                                                 ▼
Medial Medullary (Dejerine) — 4 M's                              Lateral Medullary (Wallenberg) — 4 S's
• Motor pathway (Contralateral Hemiplegia)                       • Spinothalamic (Contralateral Body Pain/Temp)
• Motor CN 12 (Ipsilateral Tongue Wasting/Dev)                   • Spinal CN 5 (Ipsilateral Face Pain/Temp)
• MLF (Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia - INO)                       • Sympathetic (Ipsilateral Horner's)
• Medial Lemniscus (Contralateral Proprioception Loss)           • Spinocerebellar (Ipsilateral Ataxia)
                                                                 • NO MOTOR WEAKNESS (Plantar Flexor!)
```

- **Medial Medullary Syndrome (Dejerine Syndrome) — The 4 M's Rule**:
    1. **M**otor Pathway (Corticospinal Tract) → **Contralateral Hemiplegia**.
    2. **M**otor Cranial Nerve (CN 12 Nucleus) → **Ipsilateral LMN 12th Nerve Palsy** (tongue atrophy, fasciculations, and deviation TOWARD the side of lesion).
    3. **M**edial Longitudinal Fasciculus (MLF) → **Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia (INO)** (failure of ipsilateral eye adduction on horizontal gaze + horizontal nystagmus in contralateral abducting eye; classic lesion in Multiple Sclerosis).
    4. **M**edial Lemniscus → **Contralateral Loss of Posterior Column Sensations** (vibration and joint position sense lost; pain and temperature preserved).

---

- **Lateral Medullary Syndrome (Wallenberg Syndrome) — The 4 S's Rule [IMPORTANT]**:
    
    1. **S**pinothalamic Tract → **Contralateral Loss of Pain & Temperature on Body**.
    2. **S**pinal Tract & Nucleus of Trigeminus (CN 5) → **Ipsilateral Loss of Pain & Temperature on Face**.
        - _Crossed Sensory Loss_: Ipsilateral face + Contralateral body loss of pain/temp is pathognomonic of lateral medullary stroke.
        - _Dissociative Sensory Loss_: Pain and temperature are lost, while posterior column sensations (vibration/proprioception) are completely preserved.
    3. **S**ympathetic Tract → **Ipsilateral Horner's Syndrome** (Partial Ptosis, Miosis, Anhidrosis, Loss of Ciliospinal Reflex, Mild Enophthalmos).
    4. **S**pinocerebellar Tract → **Ipsilateral Cerebellar Ataxia**.
    
    - **Additional Manifestations**: Involvement of Nucleus Ambiguus (CN 9, 10, 11) → dysphagia, hoarseness of voice, nasal regurgitation, loss of gag reflex.
    - **Pathognomonic History Symptom**: **PERSISTENT HICCUPS** (irritation of NTS / nucleus ambiguous).
    - **Vascular Etiology**: Caused by occlusion of the **Vertebral Artery** (most common cause in current medical literature) or the **Posterior Inferior Cerebellar Artery (PICA)**.
    - **THE CRITICAL UNIQUE FEATURE OF WALLENBERG SYNDROME [IMPORTANT]**: **The corticospinal tract is NOT involved**. There is **NO MOTOR WEAKNESS**, and **BOTH PLANTAR RESPONSES ARE FLEXOR**.

---

### E. Spinal Cord Stroke

- Requires a lesion in the cervical cord **above C4 level**.
- Pure arterial stroke is rare because the **Anterior Spinal Artery** supplies the anterior 2/3 of the cord (producing quadriplegia/paraplegia).
- **Brown-Séquard Syndrome** (Hemi-section of cord, usually traumatic/stab injury):
    - _Ipsilateral_: Motor hemiplegia + Posterior column sensory loss (proprioception/vibration) below lesion.
    - _Contralateral_: Loss of pain and temperature sensation starting 1–2 segments below lesion.

> - **Bulbar Palsy**: **LMN lesion** involving lower cranial nerve nuclei (CN 9, 10, 11, 12) in the medulla. Characterized by atrophic wasted tongue with fasciculations, **absent gag reflex**, and absent jaw jerk.
> - **Pseudobulbar Palsy**: **Bilateral UMN lesion** of corticobulbar tracts. Characterized by spastic non-wasted tongue, **exaggerated jaw jerk**, **exaggerated gag reflex**, and **emotional incontinence**(spontaneous unprovoked crying or laughing). Emotional fibers arising from the limbic system bypass the internal capsule and spare emotional facial expressions in UMN lesions.

> [!question] **Audience / Student Question**
> 
> What is the blood pressure management protocol in acute ischemic stroke?

> [!note] **Student**
> 
> Lower the blood pressure if it is high.

> [!abstract] **Dr. Archith Boloor Clinical Management Rule [IMPORTANT]**
> 
> **IMPORTANT**: In acute ischemic stroke, **DO NOT LOWER BLOOD PRESSURE** even if systolic BP is above 200 mmHg during the first 12 to 24 hours!
> 
> - **Reactionary Hypertension**: Elevated BP is a compensatory mechanism to drive cerebral perfusion pressure across collateral vessels supplying the **ischemic penumbra**. Aggressively lowering BP collapses penumbral perfusion and converts viable tissue into permanent infarction.
> - **Thrombolysis Cut-off**: BP must be reduced below **185/110 mmHg ONLY if thrombolysis with rTPA is actively planned** (to prevent hemorrhagic transformation).
> - **Absolute Indications for Immediate BP Lowering**: Active Intracerebral Hemorrhage, Aortic Dissection, Acute Renal Failure, or Acute Pulmonary Edema.
