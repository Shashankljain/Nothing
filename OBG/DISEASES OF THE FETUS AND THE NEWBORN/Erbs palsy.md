# Erb's Palsy [⭐ High-Yield]

### Mark Weightage

- **5 Marks**

---

### 1. Definition [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Erb's Palsy (Duchenne-Erb Palsy)**: An upper brachial plexus birth injury caused by traction or stretching of the **C₅ and C₆ (and occasionally C₇)** cervical nerve roots during difficult labor, resulting in paralysis of the shoulder and arm muscles [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458; Marrow Vol 2, p. 389].
- **Incidence**: Most common form of neonatal brachial plexus injury (accounting for >80% of all brachial plexus palsies) [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].

---

### 2. Etiology & Risk Factors [⭐ High-Yield]

#### A. Etiology

- Caused by excessive lateral flexion and traction applied to the fetal head and neck away from the shoulder during attempted delivery of the shoulders or aftercoming head [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].

#### B. Major Risk Factors

- **Shoulder Dystocia**: Most frequent intrapartum cause; impaction of the anterior shoulder behind the symphysis pubis leads to excessive neck traction [DC Dutta 1, Ch 27, p. 389; Marrow Vol 2, p. 389].
- **Fetal Macrosomia**: Birth weight >4 kg or >4.5 kg [DC Dutta 1, Ch 27, p. 389].
- **Vaginal Breech Delivery**: Traction on the fetal trunk during delivery of the aftercoming head [DC Dutta 1, Ch 26, p. 359].
- **Operative Vaginal Delivery**: Difficult mid-pelvic forceps extraction or vacuum delivery [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458; Ch 37, p. 535].
- **Maternal Factors**: Maternal obesity (BMI >30 kg/m²), gestational diabetes mellitus, and prolonged or arrested second stage of labor [DC Dutta 1, Ch 27, p. 389].

---

### 3. Anatomy & Nerve Root Involvement [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Nerve Roots Involved**: **C₅ and C₆** (and occasionally C₇) [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458; Marrow Vol 2, p. 389].
- **Anatomical Site (Erb's Point)**: The junction where six nerves meet in the upper trunk of the brachial plexus (C₅ root, C₆ root, anterior division of upper trunk, posterior division of upper trunk, suprascapular nerve, and nerve to subclavius).
- **Paralyzed Muscles**:
    - **Supraspinatus & Infraspinatus** (loss of shoulder abduction and lateral rotation).
    - **Deltoid** (loss of shoulder abduction).
    - **Biceps Brachii, Brachialis, & Brachioradialis** (loss of elbow flexion and forearm supination).
    - **Supinator** (loss of forearm supination).

### 5. Clinical Features [⭐ High-Yield]

#### A. Characteristic Deformity ("Waiter's Tip" / "Porter's Tip" / "Policeman's Tip" Hand)

The affected limb assumes a classic posture due to the unopposed action of non-paralyzed antagonist muscle groups [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458; Marrow Vol 2, p. 389]:

- **Shoulder**: **Adducted** and **internally rotated** [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458; Marrow Vol 2, p. 389].
- **Elbow**: **Extended** [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].
- **Forearm**: **Pronated** [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458; Marrow Vol 2, p. 389].
- **Wrist**: **Flexed** [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].

#### B. Associated Clinical Reflexes & Features

- **Moro Reflex**: **Absent** on the affected side [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].
- **Biceps Reflex**: **Absent** on the affected side.
- **Palmar Grasp Reflex**: **INTACT and NORMAL** (because C∯–T₁ nerve roots supplying intrinsic hand muscles are unaffected) [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].
- **Scapular Winging**: Prominent winging of the scapula due to weakness of stabilizing muscles [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].
- **Ipsilateral Phrenic Nerve Paralysis**: Occurs if accompanying injury to C₃, C₄, C₅ nerve roots exists, presenting with diaphragmatic elevation, tachypnea, and respiratory distress [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].

### 6. Diagnosis & Investigations [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Clinical Neurological Examination**: Diagnosis is made by observing the characteristic "Waiter's tip" attitude and absent Moro reflex with an intact grasp reflex [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].
2. **Radiography (X-Ray of Clavicle, Humerus, & Shoulder)**: Mandatory to exclude co-existing **fracture of the clavicle**, **fracture of the humerus**, or shoulder joint dislocation [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].
3. **Electromyography (EMG) & Nerve Conduction Studies (NCS)**: Performed at 2–3 weeks post-injury to differentiate neuropraxia (stretching) from axonotmesis or neurotmesis (tear/avulsion).
4. **MRI / Ultrasound of Brachial Plexus**: Assesses nerve root avulsion from the spinal cord in severe non-recovering cases.

### 8. Treatment & Management [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Conservative & Supportive Care (First-Line)**:
    - **Initial Rest & Protection**: Pin the sleeve of the affected arm to the front of the shirt across the abdomen for the first 7–10 days to prevent further nerve stretch injury [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].
    - **Passive Range of Motion (PROM) Exercises**: Gentle passive movements of the shoulder, elbow, and wrist initiated after **1 to 2 weeks** to prevent joint contractures and muscle atrophy [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].
    - **Splinting**: Application of an "airplane splint" (holding shoulder abducted to 90° and externally rotated with elbow flexed) to maintain functional position during sleep [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].
- **Specialist Referral & Timing**:
    - Refer to a multidisciplinary brachial plexus team if no clinical recovery is observed by **1 month** of life [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].
- **Surgical Management**:
    - Indicated if biceps function does NOT return by **3 to 6 months** of age.
    - Procedures include nerve grafting, neurotization (nerve transfers), and secondary tendon transfers (e.g., latissimus dorsi transfer for shoulder abduction).

---

### 9. Complications [⭐ High-Yield]

- Permanent joint contractures (internal rotation contracture of shoulder, flexion contracture of elbow).
- Muscle atrophy and limb length discrepancy.
- Posterior dislocation of the humeral head (glenohumeral dysplasia).
- Long-term permanent motor weakness and functional impairment [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].

### 11. Clinical Pearl & Exam Trap

### Clinical Pearl

- **The Palmar Grasp Reflex Discriminator**: In **Erb's Palsy (C₅–C₆)**, the **palmar grasp reflex is completely normal and intact** because the lower nerve roots (C₈–T₁) supplying the flexors of the hand are spared. In contrast, in **Klumpke's Palsy (C₈–T₁)**, the **palmar grasp reflex is completely absent**!

### Exam Trap

- **The Mix-Up**: Confusing the limb deformity of **Erb's Palsy** with **Klumpke's Palsy**.
- **Distinguishing Fact**:
    - **Erb's Palsy (C₅–C₆)**: **"Waiter's / Porter's tip hand"** (shoulder adducted/internally rotated, elbow extended, forearm pronated) [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458; Marrow Vol 2, p. 389].
    - **Klumpke's Palsy (C₈–T₁)**: **"Claw hand"** with associated **Horner's syndrome** (ptosis, miosis) [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].

### Quick Revision

- **Nerve Roots**: **C₅ and C₆** upper brachial plexus roots [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458; Marrow Vol 2, p. 389].
- **Primary Cause**: Undue traction on fetal neck during delivery in **shoulder dystocia** or breech extraction [DC Dutta 1, Ch 27, p. 389; Ch 33, p. 458].
- **Classic Posture**: **"Waiter's / Porter's / Policeman's tip hand"** (shoulder adducted + internally rotated, elbow extended, forearm pronated, wrist flexed) [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458; Marrow Vol 2, p. 389].
- **Key Reflex Pattern**: **Moro reflex = ABSENT**; **Palmar Grasp = INTACT** [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].
- **Management**: Initial immobilization, passive physical therapy from 1–2 weeks, referral if no improvement by 1 month [DC Dutta 1, Ch 33, p. 458].
