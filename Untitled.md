# 3. ANALYSIS OF THE THREE CHIEF COMPLAINTS

The mentor asked the student to analyse all three chief complaints together before discussing them individually.

The combination of:

- Abdominal distension.
- Jaundice.
- Right hypochondrial pain.

Suggests possible hepatobiliary system pathology.

## 3.1 Why Suspect Hepatobiliary Pathology?

Jaundice is commonly associated with disorders involving:

- Liver.
- Intrahepatic biliary system.
- Extrahepatic biliary system.
- Pancreas.

The combination of abdominal distension and jaundice makes liver pathology an important possibility.

# 4. HISTORY TAKING – ABDOMINAL DISTENSION

## 4.1 First Question: Localized or Generalized?

Always determine whether abdominal distension is:

1. Localized.
2. Generalized.

### Localized Distension

May occur due to:

- Tumour arising from an abdominal organ.
- Organ enlargement.
- Localized collection.
- Other space-occupying lesions.

### Generalized Distension

May occur due to:

- Fluid.
- Gas.
- Faeces.
- Fat.
- Fetus in females.

The mentor emphasized asking leading questions to identify the region where the patient feels the abdomen is most distended.

## 4.2 Causes of Generalized Abdominal Distension

| Cause | Clinical explanation |
|---|---|
| Fluid | Ascites or other fluid accumulation |
| Gas | Intestinal gas accumulation |
| Faeces | Faecal loading or intestinal obstruction |
| Fat | Obesity, especially central obesity |
| Fetus | Pregnancy in females |

### Flowchart

    GENERALIZED ABDOMINAL DISTENSION
                    |
          -----------------------
          |     |      |        |
        Fluid  Gas  Faeces     Fat
                                  |
                           Central obesity

    In females, also consider pregnancy.

## 4.3 How to Differentiate the Causes Through History

### A. Fat

- General obesity usually involves other body areas as well.
- If fat accumulation is predominantly abdominal, it is called central obesity.
- Patients may simply describe their abdomen as becoming bigger rather than describing progressive bloating.

**Viva:** What is obesity predominantly involving the abdominal region called?

**Answer:** Central obesity.

### B. Gas

- May occur due to gastrointestinal pathology.
- Belching may suggest upper gastrointestinal symptoms.
- Lower gastrointestinal obstruction or intestinal dysfunction can cause accumulation of gas proximal to the obstruction.
- In elderly patients, progressive constipation is an important clue.

**Clinical reasoning:**

    Lower GI obstruction
             |
             v
    Accumulation of intestinal contents and gas
             |
             v
    Proximal bowel dilatation
             |
             v
    Abdominal distension

The mentor explained that upper GI symptoms such as belching alone are less likely to explain persistent generalized abdominal distension.

In this patient, there was no history of progressive constipation, making gas-related distension less likely.

### C. Fluid

Fluid accumulation becomes an important possibility in a patient with:

- Generalized abdominal distension.
- Jaundice.
- Suspected liver disease.

Additional supporting history includes:

1. History of swelling of both lower limbs.
2. History of medications prescribed for abdominal distension.
3. History of increased urine output after taking such medication.
4. History of reduction in abdominal distension following diuretic use.

If the patient reports increased urination and reduction in abdominal size after taking diuretics, this supports fluid accumulation.

### D. Pedal Oedema and Abdominal Fluid

Fluid accumulation in the abdomen may coexist with swelling of both lower limbs.

This may occur in generalized fluid accumulation (anasarca).

However, pedal oedema is not present in every patient with ascites.

> [!question] Viva: Why ask about bilateral pedal oedema in a patient with abdominal distension?
> **Answer:** It may indicate generalized fluid retention and supports the possibility of ascites, particularly in a patient with suspected liver disease.

## 4.4 Conclusion Regarding Abdominal Distension

The patient's distension was:

- Generalized.
- Gradually progressive.
- Associated with jaundice.
- Clinically suspicious for fluid accumulation.

Therefore, ascites was considered an important possibility.

# 5. HISTORY TAKING – JAUNDICE

## 5.1 History of Jaundice

- Noticed 5 months ago.
- Gradually progressive.
- No associated itching.
- No fever.
- No history of discoloured stools.
- No history of dark urine.

## 5.2 Why Ask About Pruritus, Stool Colour and Urine Colour?

These questions help assess the possibility of impaired bile flow and obstructive jaundice.

## 5.3 Types of Biliary Obstruction

Obstruction to bile flow may be:

1. Intrahepatic.
2. Extrahepatic.

### A. Intrahepatic Obstruction

A typical example discussed by the mentor was the early phase of hepatitis.

- Swelling of liver cells may compress small bile ducts.
- This may produce cholestasis and jaundice.

### B. Extrahepatic Obstruction

In surgical practice, surgical jaundice commonly refers to extrahepatic biliary obstruction.

Possible causes include:

- Carcinoma head of pancreas.
- Common bile duct calculus.
- Periampullary carcinoma.
- Other lesions causing compression or obstruction of the extrahepatic biliary tract.

## 5.4 Important Clinical Point

Absence of pruritus, pale stools or dark urine does not completely rule out obstruction.

Their presence may support the diagnosis, but their absence does not exclude it.

## 5.5 Why Was Pancreatic Disease Considered?

The patient had two important risk factors:

- Chronic alcohol consumption.
- Tobacco use.

The mentor emphasized the association between alcohol, smoking and pancreatic disease.

Possible pancreatic causes of jaundice include:

- Chronic pancreatitis with biliary obstruction.
- Carcinoma head of pancreas.

> [!question] Viva: Why should pancreatic pathology be considered in a patient with jaundice and a history of alcohol and tobacco use?
> **Answer:** Alcohol and smoking are important risk factors for pancreatic disease. A lesion involving the head of the pancreas may cause extrahepatic biliary obstruction.

## 5.6 Alcohol Consumption

The patient consumed approximately 180 mL of whiskey daily for many years.

The mentor emphasized documenting:

- Quantity.
- Duration.
- Type of alcohol.
- Pattern of consumption.

There is no universally safe alcohol threshold that guarantees absence of liver damage.

# 7. HISTORY TAKING – RIGHT HYPOCHONDRIAL PAIN

## 7.1 Character of Pain

The patient had:

- Dull aching pain.
- Constant pain.
- Duration of 1 month.
- Insidious onset.
- No radiation.
- No nausea or vomiting.
- No relation to food.
- No aggravating or relieving factors.

## 7.2 Significance of Pain

The mentor emphasized that the character of pain helps identify whether pathology is arising from a solid organ or a hollow organ.

| Solid organ pathology | Hollow organ pathology |
|---|---|
| May produce dull, aching, persistent pain | May produce colicky pain |
| Example: liver pathology | Example: intestinal or biliary colic |
| Pain may be relatively constant | Pain may occur in episodes |

In this patient, persistent dull aching pain in the right hypochondrium suggested liver pathology.

> [!question] Viva: What does constant dull aching pain in the right hypochondrium suggest?
> **Answer:** It suggests pathology involving a solid organ in the right hypochondrium, particularly the liver.

# 8. RELEVANT NEGATIVE HISTORY

The mentor asked the student to justify every negative history rather than simply listing it.

## 8.1 Gastrointestinal Symptoms

| Negative history | Why it is asked |
|---|---|
| No nausea or vomiting | Helps assess upper GI pathology and obstruction |
| No black stools (melena) | Helps assess upper GI bleeding |
| No fever | Helps assess cholangitis, infection and pancreatitis |
| No previous episodes of intermittent abdominal pain | Helps assess recurrent pancreatic or biliary pathology |
| No fatty food intolerance | Helps assess gallbladder disease |
| No belching | Helps assess upper GI symptoms |
| No heartburn | Helps assess upper GI pathology |
| No change in frequency of stools | Helps assess intestinal pathology |
| No change in consistency of stools | Helps assess intestinal pathology |
| No change in stool colour | Helps assess intestinal and hepatobiliary pathology |

## 8.2 Why Ask About Melena?

Black, tarry stools may indicate upper gastrointestinal bleeding.

Possible causes include:

- Peptic ulcer disease.
- Upper GI malignancy.
- Variceal bleeding.
- Other upper gastrointestinal bleeding sources.

The mentor also discussed the possibility of an ampullary lesion in the context of jaundice and gastrointestinal bleeding.

## 8.3 Why Ask About Fever?

Fever in a jaundiced patient may suggest:

- Cholangitis.
- Pancreatitis.
- Liver abscess.
- Other infective conditions.

Absence of fever makes an acute infective presentation less likely but does not exclude all these conditions.

## 8.4 Why Ask About Previous Episodes of Abdominal Pain?

Recurrent abdominal pain may occur in:

- Chronic pancreatitis.
- Gallbladder disease.
- Recurrent biliary colic.

## 8.5 Why Ask About Fatty Food Intolerance?

Fatty food intolerance may be associated with gallbladder-related symptoms.

## 8.6 Why Ask About Changes in Stool?

The hepatic flexure of the colon lies in the right hypochondrium.

Therefore, colonic pathology may present with symptoms in this region.

Changes in:

- Frequency.
- Consistency.
- Colour.

May help identify intestinal pathology.

# 9. HISTORY OF RISK FACTORS FOR LIVER DISEASE

## 9.1 Blood Transfusion

No history of blood transfusion.

**Reason for asking:**

Blood transfusion may be a risk factor for transmission of hepatitis B or hepatitis C, depending on the circumstances and screening practices.

## 9.2 Sexual History

No history of casual sexual intercourse.

**Reason for asking:**

Sexual exposure may be a risk factor for hepatitis B transmission.

## 9.3 Bloody Diarrhoea

No history of bloody diarrhoea in the previous year.

**Reason for asking:**

The mentor discussed amoebic liver abscess as a possible condition to consider in the appropriate clinical context.

## 9.4 Contact with Dogs

No history of contact with dogs.

**Reason for asking:**

Contact with dogs may be relevant to echinococcal infection.

Echinococcosis may produce a hydatid cyst in the liver.

### Hydatid Cyst and Jaundice

The mentor explained that jaundice in hepatic hydatid disease may occur due to cystobiliary communication.

    Hepatic hydatid cyst
             |
             v
    Communication with biliary system
             |
             v
    Daughter cysts / cyst contents enter bile ducts
             |
             v
    Biliary obstruction
             |
             v
    Jaundice

The mentor emphasized that jaundice in this situation is not necessarily due to simple external compression of the bile ducts.

It may occur because of communication between the cyst and the biliary system.

> [!question] Viva: How can a hydatid cyst cause jaundice?
> **Answer:** By cystobiliary communication, allowing daughter cysts or cyst contents to enter the biliary tree and obstruct bile flow.

# 10. PAST HISTORY

## 10.1 Why Ask About Previous Abdominal Surgery?

The mentor discussed the possibility of adhesive intestinal obstruction.

If a patient presents with:

- Abdominal distension.
- Abdominal pain.
- Previous abdominal surgery.

One should consider postoperative adhesions causing intestinal obstruction.

    Previous abdominal surgery
                |
                v
        Formation of adhesions
                |
                v
        Intestinal obstruction
                |
                v
        Pain + distension + vomiting

The patient in this case had no previous surgery.

> [!question] Viva: Why is previous abdominal surgery important in a patient with abdominal distension and pain?
> **Answer:** It raises the possibility of postoperative adhesions causing intestinal obstruction.

## 11.2 Disturbed Sleep – Clinical Significance

The mentor asked the student not to write "disturbed sleep" without explaining the reason.

Possible causes discussed:

1. Pain.
2. Abdominal distension.
3. Difficulty breathing while lying supine due to abdominal distension.
4. Progressive liver dysfunction.

### Sleep Disturbance in Liver Disease

Sleep disturbance may occur before overt hepatic encephalopathy.

Possible pattern:

- Insomnia at night.
- Increased sleepiness during the day.
- Disturbed sleep–wake cycle.

This may be an early manifestation of hepatic dysfunction.

However, sleep disturbance is nonspecific and should not automatically be attributed to hepatic encephalopathy.

## 13.5 Karnofsky Performance Status

The Karnofsky Performance Status assesses the patient's functional ability.

A score of 90 indicates that the patient is able to carry out normal activity with minor signs or symptoms of disease.

| Score | Functional status |
|---|---|
| 100 | Normal; no complaints |
| 90 | Normal activity with minor symptoms |
| 80 | Normal activity with effort |
| 70 | Cares for self but unable to carry on normal activity |
| 60 | Requires occasional assistance |
| 50 | Requires considerable assistance |
| 40 | Disabled; requires special care |
| 30 | Severely disabled |
| 20 | Very sick; hospitalisation required |
| 10 | Moribund |
| 0 | Dead |

## 13.6 ECOG Performance Status

| ECOG | Description |
|---|---|
| 0 | Fully active |
| 1 | Restricted in strenuous activity but ambulatory |
| 2 | Ambulatory; capable of self-care but unable to work |
| 3 | Limited self-care; confined to bed or chair more than 50% of waking hours |
| 4 | Completely disabled |
| 5 | Dead |

> [!question] Viva: Why is performance status important in a patient with suspected malignancy?
> **Answer:** It helps assess functional reserve and suitability for treatment, including major surgery and systemic therapy.

# 14. GENERAL EXAMINATION – PEDAL OEDEMA

The mentor emphasized that causes of pedal oedema may be asked during the examination.

## 14.1 Causes of Bilateral Pedal Oedema

### Systemic causes

1. Congestive cardiac failure.
2. Chronic liver disease.
3. Renal disease.
4. Nephrotic syndrome.
5. Hypoproteinaemia.
6. Severe malnutrition.
7. Hypothyroidism.

### Other causes

1. Drug-induced oedema.
2. Chronic venous insufficiency.
3. Immobility.
4. Lymphatic obstruction.

## 14.2 Mechanisms of Oedema

Important mechanisms include:

- Increased hydrostatic pressure.
- Decreased plasma oncotic pressure.
- Increased capillary permeability.
- Lymphatic obstruction.
- Sodium and water retention.
