[[Genetics]]

**Chromosomal abnormalities** are numerical or structural alterations in the human genome that occur from time to time. These abnormalities represent a significant portion of embryonic and foetal wastage, with an overall incidence of approximately 5.6 per 1,000 live births.

---

### **I. Classification of Chromosomal Abnormalities** ★ Important Exam Point

Chromosomal abnormalities are broadly classified into two categories based on the nature of the error:

#### **1. Numerical Abnormalities (Aneuploidy)**

This occurs when the chromosome number is not an exact multiple of the haploid number (23).

- **Trisomy:** Presence of three chromosomes instead of the usual pair (e.g., 47 chromosomes).
- **Monosomy:** Presence of only one chromosome instead of a pair (e.g., 45 chromosomes).

#### **2. Structural Abnormalities**

These arise from breaks in the chromosome followed by reconstitution in an abnormal combination.

- **Translocation:** A portion of one chromosome breaks away and becomes attached to a non-homologous chromosome.
- **Deletion:** A piece of a chromosome becomes detached and lost, resulting in the loss of one or more genes.
- **Duplication:** Some genes appear twice in the same chromosome.
- **Inversion:** A chromosomal segment becomes inverted, altering the sequence of genes.
- **Isochromosomes:** Arise from transverse division instead of the normal longitudinal division during cell division.
- **Mosaicism:** The cells of the body are compounded of two or more genetically different types.

---

### **II. Etiology and Pathogenesis**

The primary mechanism for numerical abnormalities is **Non-disjunction**.

- **How?** During nuclear division, a pair of chromosomes fails to separate, and both are carried to one pole.
- **Result:** One daughter cell receives 47 chromosomes (trisomy) while the other receives 45 (monosomy).
- **When?** This can occur during gametogenesis (meiosis) or during mitosis.
- **Risk Factors:** Advanced **maternal age** is the most significant risk factor for autosomal trisomies like Down’s syndrome, whereas paternal age has no effect.

---

### **III. Major Clinical Syndromes** ★ Important Exam Point

#### **1. Autosomal Disorders: Down’s Syndrome (Trisomy 21)**

- **Definition:** The most common autosomal aneuploidy, characterized by an extra 21st chromosome.
- **Clinical Features:** Mental retardation, short stature, small round head, tilted eye-slits, malformed ears, and internal defects such as cardiac malformations and atresia of the alimentary tract.
- **Epidemiology:** Incidence is 1 in 3,000 for mothers aged 20, rising to 1 in 50 for mothers aged 45.

#### **2. Sex Chromosome Disorders**

|**Syndrome**|**Karyotype**|**Key Features**|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Klinefelter’s**|47, XXY|Males with non-functional testes, gynecomastia, scanty body hair, and mental retardation.|
|**Turner’s**|45, XO|Females with short stature, primary amenorrhoea, infertile, and webbed neck/renal malformations.|
|**XYY Syndrome**|47, XYY|Males with exceptional height (>6 feet) and tendencies toward aggressive or anti-social behaviour.|
|**Super Females**|XXX or XXXX|Females with mental retardation and underdeveloped external genitalia.|

### **IV. Investigations and Diagnosis**

- **Karyotyping:** The standard method of chromosomal study using ==buccal smears, peripheral blood, or bone marrow.==
- **Metaphase Arrest:** Colchicine is added to cell cultures to arrest cells in metaphase, allowing chromosomes to be visualized after treatment with hypotonic solutions.
- **Fluorescent Staining:** Treating chromosomes with quinacrine hydrochloride (Atabrine) to exhibit distinct fluorescence.
- **Prenatal Diagnosis:**
    - **Amniocentesis:** Performed at **14–16 weeks** of pregnancy; foetal cells are cultured for karyotyping.
    - **Chorionic Villus Sampling (CVS):** Another cytogenetic method for early diagnosis.

---

### **V. Preventive and Social Measures**

1. **Health Education:** Encouraging early marriage (preventing late pregnancy) to reduce the risk of Trisomy 21.
2. **Genetic Counselling:** Prospective (identifying carriers) or retrospective (advice after an affected child is born).
3. **Negative Eugenics:** Discouraging individuals with serious hereditary defects from producing children.
4. **Prenatal Screening:** Mandatory for mothers aged 35 years or older or those with a previous history of chromosomal anomalies.

---

### **High-Yield Exam Points** ★

- **Trisomy 21:** Always mention **Langdon Down (1866)** and the 1:50 risk at maternal age 45.
- **Turner’s Syndrome:** Note that 98% of XO conceptuses abort spontaneously.
- **Non-disjunction:** The hallmark mechanism for aneuploidy.
- **Amniocentesis:** The "gold standard" for prenatal chromosomal diagnosis, typically at 14–16 weeks.

### **Frequently Asked Viva Questions**

- **Q: What is a Barr Body?**
    - **A:** A dark-staining area at the periphery of the female cell nucleus (sex chromatin), not present in normal males.
- **Q: What is the difference between Genotype and Phenotype?**
    - **A:** Genotype is the fixed genetic constitution; Phenotype is the outward expression which can be influenced by the environment (the "clay" vs. the "mould").
- **Q: What is the significance of Mosaicism?**
    - **A:** It refers to individuals having two or more cell lines with different karyotypes, often resulting in milder clinical features of a syndrome.

---

### **Common Mistakes Students Make**

- **Confusing Monosomy and Trisomy:** Remember, Monosomy = 45 (missing one), Trisomy = 47 (extra one).
- **Paternal Age Myth:** Stating that paternal age increases the risk of Down’s syndrome; the sources explicitly state it is **unaffected** by the age of the father.
- **Timing of Investigations:** Stating amniocentesis is done in the 3rd trimester. It must be done early (14–16 weeks) for termination options to be viable.
