[[Genetics]]
**Down’s Syndrome**, historically referred to in the provided text as "mongolism," is a significant public health concern and the most common autosomal chromosomal disorder in humans. It is characterized by a specific set of physical features and mental retardation due to the presence of extra genetic material.

---

### **I. Introduction and History**

- **Definition:** Down’s syndrome is a chromosomal disorder caused by an abnormality in the autosomes (non-sex chromosomes), specifically involving an extra 21st chromosome.
- **History:** The condition was first described by **Langdon Down** in **1866**.
- **Synonym:** It is sometimes referred to as **Trisomy 21** because of the triple presence of chromosome 21.

---

### **II. Etiology and Pathogenesis** ★ Important Exam Point

The condition arises from a numerical chromosomal abnormality:

- **Mechanism:** It is usually caused by **==non==-==disjunction==** during nuclear division. In non-disjunction, a pair of chromosomes fails to separate, leading to daughter cells with an unequal number of chromosomes (47 instead of 46).
- **Karyotype:** Most cases result from an extra chromosome on the **21st pair**.
- **Inheritance:** For the most part, these chromosomal disorders are **==not inherited==** but occur as spontaneous errors during gamete formation. However, if a parent is a known carrier of a **chromosomal translocation**, the risk of recurrence in future children is higher.

---

### **III. Risk Factors** ★ Important Exam Point

The most critical risk factor identified is **maternal age**.

| **Risk Factor**      | **Description**                                                                   |
| :------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Maternal Age**     | The frequency of Down’s syndrome ==increases== sharply with rising maternal age.  |
| **Paternal Age**     | The frequency is **==unaffected==** by the age of the father.                     |
| **Previous History** | Parents who have already had one child with Down’s syndrome are at a higher risk. |

#### **Maternal Age vs. Risk of Down’s Syndrome**

|**Maternal Age**|**Estimated Risk**|
|:--|:--|
|**20 years**|1 in 3,000 births|
|**45 years**|1 in 50 births|

### **IV. Clinical Features**

Down’s syndrome presents with a constellation of physical and mental findings that are easily recognized in the older child and adult.
![[image 11.jpg]]
- **General Stature:** Short stature and lax limbs.
- **Craniofacial Features:**
    - Small round head.
    - Narrow, tilted eye-slits.
    - Malformed ears.
- **Extremities:** Short, broad hands.
- **Mental Health:** ==Mental retardation== is a hallmark of the syndrome.
- **Internal Malformations:**
    - **Cardiac defects** (congenital heart disease).
    - **Atresia of the alimentary tract** (blockage in the digestive system).

---

### **V. Investigations and Diagnosis**

Diagnosis is primarily focused on **==prenatal screening==** to allow parents to make informed decisions.

#### **1. Prenatal Screening (Non-Invasive)**

- **Ultrasound:** Foetal anomaly scan to look for physical markers.
- **Maternal Serum Markers:** Testing the mother's blood for:
    - Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP).
    - Chorionic gonadotropin.

#### **2. Definitive Diagnostic Tests (Invasive)** ★ Important Exam Point

- **Amniocentesis:**
    - **When?** Performed in early pregnancy, usually between **==14–16 weeks==**.
    - **How?** A sample of amniotic fluid is taken. Foetal cells are cultured and **==Karyotyping==** is performed to visualize the chromosomes.
- **Chorionic Villus Sampling (CVS):** Another method of cytogenetic analysis used for early diagnosis.

---

### **VI. Indications for Amniocentesis**

Medical professionals recommend definitive genetic testing (amniocentesis) in the following high-risk groups:

1. Mothers aged **35 years or older**.
2. Parents who already have a child with Down’s syndrome or other chromosomal anomalies.
3. Parents known to have **chromosomal translocation**.

---

### **VII. Prevention and Management**

- **Primary Prevention:** There is no "cure" for the chromosomal error, so prevention focuses on **Health Education**regarding the risks of late marriages and advanced maternal age.
- **Genetic Counselling:** Providing information to couples at risk (prospective counselling) or to those who have already had an affected child (retrospective counselling).
- **Rehabilitation:** While the condition is permanent, rehabilitation can help the individual lead a better and more useful life.

---

### **High-Yield Exam Points** ★

- **Trisomy 21:** The specific chromosomal error in Down's Syndrome.
- **Maternal Age:** The single most important epidemiological risk factor.
- **Amniocentesis:** The gold-standard diagnostic tool performed at 14-16 weeks.
- **Langdon Down:** The person who first described the condition in 1866.

### **Frequently Asked Viva Questions**

- **Q: What is the risk of Down's syndrome in a 45-year-old mother compared to a 20-year-old?**
    - **A:** The risk increases significantly from 1 in 3,000 at age 20 to 1 in 50 at age 45.
- **Q: At what stage of pregnancy is amniocentesis typically performed for Down's diagnosis?**
    - **A:** Between 14 and 16 weeks of pregnancy.
- **Q: Name two internal malformations commonly associated with Down’s syndrome.**
    - **A:** Congenital cardiac defects and atresia of the alimentary tract.

---

### **Common Mistakes Students Make**

- **Confusing Paternal and Maternal Age:** Students often wrongly state that the father's age is a risk factor. The source explicitly states it is **unaffected** by the age of the father.
- **Terminology:** Using the term "mongolism" as the primary name. While it is in older texts, university examiners prefer "Down’s Syndrome" or "Trisomy 21".
- **Timing of Amniocentesis:** Stating amniocentesis is done in the third trimester. It must be done early (14-16 weeks) to be effective for prenatal diagnosis.
