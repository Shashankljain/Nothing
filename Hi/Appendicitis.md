# Acute Appendicitis

**Mark-Weightage: 10 Marks / Long Answer (Defaulted as not specified)**

## 1. Definition [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Acute appendicitis** is defined as the acute inflammation of the vermiform appendix, primarily initiated by luminal obstruction followed by bacterial invasion, ischemic necrosis, and potential perforation.
- It represents the single most common cause of an **'acute abdomen'** in young adults and is the most frequently performed urgent abdominal surgical procedure globally.

## 2. Etiology / Causes / Risk Factors

- **Primary Initiating Event**: Obstruction of the appendiceal lumen.
- **Causes of Luminal Obstruction**:
    1. **Faecolith / Appendicolith**: Most common cause in adults; composed of inspissated faecal material, **calcium phosphates**, bacteria, and epithelial debris.
    2. **Lymphoid Hyperplasia**: Most common cause in children and young adults, frequently triggered by viral gastrointestinal or upper respiratory tract infections.
    3. **Fibrotic Stricture**: Resulting from previous self-limiting or subacute episodes of appendicitis.
    4. **Appendiceal Neoplasms**: Carcinoma of the caecum or primary appendiceal tumors (e.g., **Low-grade Appendiceal Mucinous Neoplasm [LAMN]**), particularly in patients aged **>40 years**.
    5. **Intestinal Parasites**: Luminal occlusion by _Enterobius vermicularis_ (**pinworm**) or _Ascaris lumbricoides_.
    6. **Foreign Bodies**: Ingested seeds, pins, or barium residue.
- **Risk Factors for Appendiceal Perforation**:
    - **Extremes of age**: Infants aged **<36 months** and elderly adults.
    - **Co-morbidities**: **Diabetes mellitus** and **immunosuppression**.
    - **Obstructing Faecolith**: Direct pressure necrosis.
    - **Anatomical Position**: **Pelvic appendix** position.
    - **Previous Abdominal Surgery**: Adhesions that limit the ability of the greater omentum to wall off peritoneal contamination.
- **Demographics & Epidemiology**:
    - Peak incidence occurs in teenagers and young adults aged **10–20 years**.
    - Lifetime risk is **8.6%** in males and **6.7%** in females.
    - Male-to-female ratio is **3:2** at age **25**, equalizing after middle age.

## 3. Classification & Anatomical Variations

- **Pathological / Clinical Classification**:
    1. **Acute Catarrhal (Non-Obstructive) Appendicitis**: Mild mucosal inflammation, patent lumen, potential for spontaneous or antibiotic resolution.
    2. **Acute Obstructive Appendicitis**: Rapidly progressive, abrupt onset, high risk of gangrene and early perforation.
    3. **Complicated Appendicitis**: Presence of perforation, gangrene, **phlegmonous mass**, **paracaecal / pelvic abscess**, or generalized peritonitis.
- **Anatomical Positions of Appendix (Sir C. Wakeley)**:
    1. **Retrocaecal (74%)**: Appendix lies behind the caecum; presents with "silent" anterior abdomen and loin tenderness.
    2. **Pelvic (21%)**: Appendix hangs over the pelvic brim; presents with suprapubic pain, tenesmus, diarrhea, and urinary frequency.
    3. **Paracaecal (2%)**: Lies lateral to the caecum.
    4. **Subcaecal (1.5%)**: Lies inferior to the caecum.
    5. **Preileal (1%)**: Anterior to terminal ileum; carries marked vomiting and early somatic pain.
    6. **Postileal (0.5%)**: Posterior to terminal ileum; presents with diarrhea, retching, and ill-defined periumbilical tenderness.

## 4. Pathophysiology [⭐ High-Yield]

### Mechanism Sequence

- **Luminal Obstruction**: Caused by faecolith, lymphoid hyperplasia, or stricture.
- **Mucus Accumulation**: Mucus secretion and inflammatory exudation continue behind the obstruction.
- **Intraluminal Hypertension**: Increased pressure leads to obstruction of lymphatic drainage.
- **Mucosal Edema & Ulceration**: Bacterial translocation occurs from the lumen into the submucosa.
- **Venous Congestion & Thrombosis**: Intraluminal pressure exceeds venous capillary pressure, leading to ischemia of the appendiceal wall.
- **Transmural Bacterial Invasion**: Bacteria invade the muscularis propria, producing acute suppurative appendicitis.
- **Arterial Thrombosis & Gangrene**: Ischemic necrosis of the wall results in gangrenous appendicitis.
- **Perforation**: Antimesenteric wall necrosis leads to free perforation into the peritoneal cavity.
- **Resolution / Local Containment**: Greater omentum and loops of small intestine adhere to wall off the infection, forming an **appendix phlegmon / mass** or **abscess**.

## 5. Clinical Features [⭐ High-Yield]

### Symptoms

- **Murphy's Classic Triad**: Pain, Fever, and Vomiting in chronological sequence.
- **Visceral Pain Phase**:
    - Poorly localized, colicky periumbilical pain due to midgut visceral nerve stimulation (**T10 dermatome**).
- **Somatic Pain Phase**:
    - Pain shifts and localizes to the **Right Iliac Fossa (RIF)** after **6–12 hours** as parietal peritoneum becomes inflamed.
    - Pain becomes constant, sharp, and exacerbated by coughing, walking, or sudden movement.
- **Associated Gastrointestinal Symptoms**:
    - **Anorexia**: Constant feature; refusal of food in children is highly specific.
    - **Nausea and Vomiting**: 1–2 episodes following the onset of pain.
    - **Diarrhea / Tenesmus**: Seen in pelvic or postileal appendicitis due to rectal irritation.
- **Positional Symptom Variations**:
    - **Retrocaecal**: Loin pain, dysuria, minimal anterior abdominal pain.
    - **Pelvic**: Suprapubic discomfort, urinary frequency, tenesmus, diarrhea.

### Signs

- **General Assessment**: Unwell patient, low-grade fever (**37.2–37.7°C**), mild tachycardia (**80–90 bpm**).
- **Abdominal Inspection**: Limitation of lower abdominal respiratory movement.
- **Pointing Sign**: Patient points with one finger first to the umbilicus and then to the RIF.
- **McBurney's Point Tenderness**: Maximum tenderness elicited at **McBurney's point** (junction of outer **1/3** and inner **2/3** of line joining ASIS to umbilicus).
- **Muscle Guarding & Rigidity**: Involuntary contraction of overlying abdominal wall muscles over RIF.
- **Rebound Tenderness**: Evoked by sudden release of pressure or light percussion over RIF.
- **Eponymous Clinical Signs**:
    1. **Rovsing's Sign**: Deep pressure in Left Iliac Fossa (LIF) produces pain in RIF due to displacement of colonic gas.
    2. **Psoas Sign**: Pain on passive hyperextension of right hip (indicates inflamed retrocaecal appendix irritating psoas muscle).
    3. **Obturator Sign (Zachary Cope)**: Pain on internal rotation of flexed right hip (indicates pelvic appendix irritating obturator internus).
    4. **Digital Rectal Examination**: Digital tenderness in rectovesical pouch / pouch of Douglas on the right side (essential in pelvic appendicitis).

## 6. Diagnosis / Investigations [⭐ High-Yield]

### Lab Findings

- **Full Blood Count (FBC)**: Leukocytosis (**10,000–18,000/µL**) with neutrophil "shift to the left".
- **C-Reactive Protein (CRP)**: Elevated CRP supports acute inflammation; combined normal WBC and normal CRP strongly rules out appendicitis.
- **Urinalysis**: Performed routinely to exclude UTI or urolithiasis; microscopic pyuria or hematuria can occur if appendix overlies ureter/bladder.
- **Beta-hCG (Pregnancy Test)**: Mandatory in all females of childbearing age to rule out ectopic pregnancy.

### Imaging Modalities

- **Ultrasound Abdomen / Pelvis**:
    - First-line modality in children, young adults, and pregnant females.
    - **Diagnostic criteria**: Non-compressible tubular structure in RIF with outer diameter **>6 mm**, target sign, periappendiceal free fluid, or appendicolith.
    - Diagnostic accuracy **>90%**.
- **Contrast-Enhanced CT Scan (Abdomen & Pelvis)**:
    - ==Gold standard== in adults and elderly (sensitivity & specificity **~95%**).
    - **Diagnostic criteria**: Distended appendix with outer diameter **>7 mm**, circumferential wall enhancement, periappendiceal fat stranding, cecal pole thickening, appendicolith, or fluid collection.
    - **Low-Dose CT Protocol**: Recommended in young adults to reduce radiation exposure by **80%**.
- **MRI Abdomen / Pelvis**: Indicated in pregnant patients when ultrasound is equivocal.

    - **Score 1–4**: Appendicitis unlikely; evaluate alternative causes.
    - **Score 5–6**: Equivocal / possible appendicitis; perform USG or CT scan.
    - **Score 7–10**: Highly predictive of acute appendicitis; proceed to surgical intervention.

## 8. Treatment / Management [⭐ High-Yield]

### Non-Pharmacological Management

- **NPO (Nil Per Os)** status immediately upon admission.
- **Intravenous Fluid Resuscitation**: Crystalloids (Normal Saline / Ringer Lactate) to restore intravascular volume and maintain urine output.
- **Nasogastric Decompression**: Reserved for patients with severe retching, persistent vomiting, or paralytic ileus.

### Pharmacological Management & Dosage Rule

- **Prophylactic Antibiotics**: Single preoperative dose given at induction for uncomplicated appendicitis.
- **Therapeutic Antibiotics**: Broad-spectrum coverage against Gram-negative bacilli (_E. coli_) and anaerobes (_Bacteroides fragilis_) for complicated/perforated appendicitis.

|Drug|Dose|Route|Frequency|Duration|Key Caution|
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
|**Metronidazole**|**500 mg**|IV|TID (q8h)|**5–7 days**|Avoid alcohol; metallic taste, peripheral neuropathy|
|**Ceftriaxone**|**1–2 g**|IV|OD (q24h)|**5–7 days**|Contraindicated in hyperbilirubinemia; monitor LFTs|
|**Cefotaxime**|**1 g**|IV|BD/TID (q8-12h)|**5–7 days**|Adjust dosage in severe renal impairment|
|**Amoxicillin-Clavulanate**|**1.2 g**|IV|TID (q8h)|**5–7 days**|Risk of cholestatic jaundice; check pen-allergy|
|**Paracetamol**|**1 g**|IV|QID (q6h)|As needed|Max **4 g/day**; caution in hepatic insufficiency|

### Surgical Management

- **Appendicectomy** is the definitive standard treatment.
- **Timing**: Deferring uncomplicated surgery overnight after midnight to the next morning is safe with IV antibiotics. Emergency surgery is required immediately for obstructive appendicitis or peritonitis.

#### Open Surgical Incisions

1. **Gridiron Incision (McBurney)**: Oblique incision made perpendicular to line joining ASIS and umbilicus at McBurney's point.
2. **Lanz Incision**: Transverse skin-crease incision **2 cm** below umbilicus centered on midclavicular line (cosmetically superior).
3. **Rutherford Morison Incision**: Oblique muscle-cutting incision used for fixed retrocaecal/paracaecal appendix.
4. **Lower Midline Incision**: Preferred when diagnosis is uncertain or in generalized peritonitis.

#### Laparoscopic Appendicectomy (Gold Standard)

- **Patient Position**: Supine with Trendelenburg tilt and left lateral tilt (right side up).
- Gen anaesthesia 
- **Port Placements**:
    1. **10 mm Umbilical Port**: Camera port inserted via open Hasson technique.
    2. **5 mm Suprapubic Port**: Working port.
    3. **5 mm Left Lower Quadrant Port**: Working port.
- **Operative Steps**:
    - Identify caecal taeniae coli and trace to appendiceal base confluence.
    - Dissect mesoappendix and ligate appendicular artery with clips or bipolar diathermy.
    - Ligate appendiceal base with **Roeder / Endoloop knot** or linear stapler and divide.
    - Extract appendix in retrieval bag through umbilical port to prevent wound infection.

### Management of Special Scenarios

- **Appendix Mass (Ochsner-Sherren Regime)**:
    - Non-operative conservative management: NPO, IV fluids, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, serial vital monitoring, and skin marking of mass borders.
    - **Criteria for Stopping Conservative Treatment**:
        1. Rising pulse rate.
        2. Increasing or spreading abdominal pain.
        3. Increasing size of mass.
    - **Interval Appendicectomy**: Considered **6–8 weeks** later; mandatory follow-up CT and colonoscopy in patients **>40 years** to rule out **LAMN** or caecal carcinoma.
- **Appendicitis in Pregnancy**:
    - Most common extrauterine surgical emergency in pregnancy (**0.5–1 per 1000**).
    - RIF pain remains the cardinal symptom despite uterine displacement.
    - Prompt surgery (open or laparoscopic via Hasson method) required; negative appendicectomy carries **4%**fetal loss risk, whereas perforation raises fetal loss to **>20%**.

## 9. Complications

- **Postoperative Wound Infection**: Most common complication (**5–10%**); caused by _Bacteroides fragilis_ and anaerobic streptococci.
- **Intra-Abdominal / Pelvic Abscess**: Occurs in **8%** overall and up to **30%** after perforated appendicitis; presents with swinging pyrexia **5–7 days** postoperatively; treated with USG/CT-guided percutaneous drainage.
- **Postoperative Paralytic Ileus**: Ileus lasting **>4–5 days** indicates persistent intra-abdominal sepsis.
- **Portal Pyaemia (Pylephlebitis)**: Rare, severe septic thrombophlebitis of portal vein presenting with high fever, rigors, jaundice, and multiple liver abscesses.
- **Faecal Fistula**: Secondary to appendiceal base necrosis or slipping of appendiceal base ligature.
- **Incisional / Port-Site Richter's Hernia**: Occurs rarely at **5 mm** or **10 mm** laparoscopic port sites.

## 10. Prognosis

- Excellent prognosis in early uncomplicated appendicitis with mortality **<0.1%**.
- Mortality increases to **1–3%** in elderly patients or cases with free perforation and generalized peritonitis.

### Memory Palace — "The Surgical Operating Theater"

1. **At the Entrance Door (Etiology & Luminal Obstruction)**: A giant hard stone (**Faecolith**) jams the door handle, surrounded by swollen lymphoid clusters and a wriggling pinworm (_Enterobius_).
2. **At the Scrub Sink (Visceral Phase)**: A surgeon feels dull, colicky pain around their navel (**T10 periumbilical pain**) and nauseously loses appetite for dinner (**Murphy's triad**).
3. **On the Operating Table (Somatic Shift & Signs)**: The patient lies with the right hip flexed (**Psoas sign**). Pressing the left side of the mattress causes sharp pain on the right (**Rovsing's sign**), and rotating the flexed hip inward causes deep pelvic pain (**Obturator sign**).
4. **At the Recovery Bay (Complications)**: A patient **5 days** post-op develops a swinging fever spike (**Pelvic Abscess**) and jaundice with chills (**Pylephlebitis**).

### Clinical Pearl

- **The "Pointing Sign" and Cough Sensitivity**: Always ask a patient with suspected appendicitis to point with one finger to where the pain started and where it moved. Asking the patient to gently cough elicits sharp RIF localized pain without manual palpation, serving as the single most reliable non-invasive test for parietal peritoneal irritation.

### Exam Trap

- **Pelvic Appendicitis vs. Acute Gastroenteritis / UTI**: An inflamed pelvic appendix irritates the rectum causing diarrhea and tenesmus, or irritates the ureter/bladder causing dysuria and microscopic pyuria.
- **Distinguishing Fact**: In pelvic appendicitis, abdominal rigidity is absent, but **Digital Rectal Examination** reveals severe localized tenderness in the right rectovesical/Douglas pouch, and urine culture remains sterile.

### Quick Revision

- **Acute appendicitis** is caused by luminal obstruction (most commonly a **faecolith** or **lymphoid hyperplasia**), leading to intraluminal hypertension, wall ischemia, bacterial translocation, and gangrene.
- **Murphy's triad** consists of migratory periumbilical pain shifting to RIF, anorexia/nausea, and vomiting.
- Diagnosis is primarily clinical, supported by **Alvarado score (≥7)**, ultrasound (**diameter >6 mm** in children/pregnant females), and CT scan (**diameter >7 mm** in adults).
- Definitive treatment is **laparoscopic appendicectomy**; an **appendix mass** is managed conservatively via the **Ochsner-Sherren regime**.
- Major postoperative complications include **wound infection (5–10%)**, **pelvic abscess (8%)**, and **portal pyaemia / pylephlebitis**.

### One-Line Exam Opener

- **Acute appendicitis is the most common emergency general surgical condition worldwide, presenting classically with migratory midgut-to-somatic pain, right iliac fossa localized peritonism, and requiring prompt surgical appendicectomy to prevent gangrenous perforation and systemic peritonitis.**
