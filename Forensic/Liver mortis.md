[[PM Changes]]
For a university examination in Forensic Medicine, **Livor Mortis** (commonly known as **Postmortem Staining**) is a vital topic under the "Early Changes After Death" category. It is essential for determining the time since death and the posture of the body at the moment of expiration.

---

### **Basic Concepts**

**Definition** **Livor mortis** (also known as **postmortem staining**, **hypostasis**, or **cadaveric lividity**) is a bluish or purplish-red discoloration of the skin that results from the gravitational settling of blood into the toneless capillaries and venules of the dependent parts of the dead body.

**Introduction** Livor mortis is an **early sign of death** (molecular death). During life, blood is kept in constant motion by the heart. Once the heart stops, circulation ceases, and blood stagnates in the vessels, sinking by the force of gravity to the lowest parts of the body.

**Mechanism**

1. **Stagnation:** After the heart stops, blood becomes static.
2. **Gravitational Settling:** Because the vessels lose their tone, blood sinks and fills the capillaries and small veins in parts of the body that are closest to the ground.
3. **External Appearance:** This results in a mottled appearance that eventually coalesces into a uniform stained area.

---

### **Medico-Legal Importance (MLI)**

★ **Important Exam Point:** Livor mortis is one of the "Classical Triad" (along with Algor and Rigor mortis) used to estimate the postmortem interval.

1. **Sign of Death:** Its presence is a definitive sign of molecular death.
2. **Estimation of Time Since Death (TSD):** The extent of its development and whether it is "fixed" help provide a range of hours since death.
3. **Posture of the Body:** It indicates the position of the body at the time of death (e.g., supine, prone, or vertical).
4. **Moving the Body:** If a body is found with staining on the front but is lying on its back, it indicates the body was moved after the staining became "fixed".
5. **Cause of Death (Color Changes):** The color of the staining can provide immediate clues to certain types of poisoning (see table below).
6. **Distinguishing from Injuries:** It must be differentiated from a bruise to rule out foul play.

---

### **Relevant Law (2023)**

- **BNS Section 2(6):** Defines **Death** as the death of a human being.
- **BNSS Section 194 (formerly 174 CrPC):** Relates to the **Police Inquest**. The medical officer is required to note postmortem staining during the external examination to assist the Investigating Officer (IO) in establishing the timeline and circumstances of death.
- **BSA Section 39:** Allows the doctor's findings on TSD and manner of death (based on lividity) to be presented as **Expert Opinion** in court.

### **Characteristics & Development**

#### **1. Timeline of Appearance**

- **Commencement:** Begins 30 minutes to 1 hour after death as discolored patches (1–2 cm in diameter).
- **Coalescence:** Patches increase in size and merge within 3–4 hours.
- **Full Development:** Usually well-developed within 4 hours and complete in 5–6 hours.
- **Fixation:** The staining becomes **'fixed' in 8–12 hours**. At this stage, it will not disappear if the body is moved or if pressure is applied to the skin.

#### **2. Distribution and Position**

- **Supine (On Back):** Appears in the neck and spreads over the back, except for pressure areas.
- **Prone (Face Down):** Intense lividity is seen on the front of the body, and **Tardieu's spots** (petechial hemorrhages) are common.
- **Vertical (Hanging):** Marked in the legs, external genitalia, and lower regions of the arms (**Glove and Stocking**distribution).
- **Submerged (Drowning):** Usually found on the face, upper chest, and hands as the body typically floats face-down.

#### **3. Special Phenomena**

- **Contact Pallor (Blanching):** Areas directly pressed against a surface (e.g., shoulder blades, buttocks, heels) do not show staining because the capillaries are compressed.
- **Vibices (Death Spots):** Tiny, round-to-oval bluish-black hemorrhages limited to areas of lividity caused by the mechanical rupture of capillaries.

### **Color of Postmortem Staining in Poisoning**

The color can be diagnostic for specific toxic agents:

|**Color**|**Cause/Poisoning**|
|:--|:--|
|**Bluish or Purplish-red**|Normal (most cases).|
|**Cherry Red**|**Carbon Monoxide (CO)** poisoning.|
|**Bright Red / Brick Red**|**Cyanide** poisoning.|
|**Bright Pink**|**Hypothermia**.|
|**Chocolate Brown**|Nitrites, Nitrogens, or Aniline.|
|**Black**|**Opium** poisoning.|
|**Blue-Green**|Hydrogen Sulphide (\(H_{2}S\)).|
|**Bronze**|_Clostridium perfringens_ (Septicemia).|

### **Differential Diagnosis: Staining vs. Bruise**

In university examinations, you will frequently be asked to differentiate between a bruise (contusion) and postmortem staining.

|**Feature**|**Postmortem Staining**|**Bruise (Contusion)**|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Cause**|Gravitational settling of blood.|Blunt force trauma (vessel rupture).|
|**Site**|Dependent parts only.|Anywhere on the body.|
|**Margins**|**Sharp and clearly defined**.|Ill-defined/irregular.|
|**Surface**|Not elevated.|Usually elevated (swelling).|
|**Pressure Test**|**Blanches** (disappears) if not fixed.|No change/does not blanch.|
|**Incision Test**|Blood is in vessels; can be washed away.|Extravasated blood in tissues; **cannot be washed away**.|

---

### **High-Yield Exam Points**

- ★ **Tête de négre:** The dark appearance of the skin of the head and neck in putrefied drowning victims.
- ★ **Fixation:** Occurs earlier in summer and is delayed in asphyxial deaths.
- ★ **Calorimetry:** A modern method used to determine TSD by measuring the color of staining with a colorimeter.

---

### **Quick Revision Summary**

- **Livor Mortis:** Sinking of blood due to gravity after death.
- **Time:** Starts at 1 hr, fixed by 8–12 hrs.
- **Significance:** TSD, posture at death, and cause of death (color).
- **Key Colors:** Cherry red (CO), Bright red (Cyanide), Pink (Hypothermia).
- **Golden Rule:** Incision test washes away staining but NOT a bruise.
