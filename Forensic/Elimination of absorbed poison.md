[[Gen toxicology]]
### **Basic Concepts** 🧼

#### **Definition**

**Elimination of absorbed poison** refers to accelerate the clearance and excretion of toxic substances from the systemic circulation _after_ they have already been absorbed into the bloodstream.

#### **Introduction**

In clinical toxicology, while preventing further absorption (such as via gastric lavage) is crucial in the early hours, once a poison enters the systemic circulation, the medical team must focus on **enhancing its elimination** to prevent multi-organ failure and death.

---

### **The "What, Why, How, When, Where, Who" of Enhanced Elimination** ❓

- **What?** Specialized medical techniques to actively clear absorbed toxins from the body.
- **Why?** To reduce the systemic "body burden" of a poison, minimizing cellular injury and decreasing mortality.
- **How?** By manipulating urinary pH, flushing the gastrointestinal tract, or using artificial blood-filtering circuits.
- **When?** Indicated under specific clinical conditions:
    1. Severe, life-threatening envenomation or poisoning.
    2. The poison is distributed primarily in the extracellular fluid and has low protein binding.
    3. The patient's condition progressively deteriorates despite optimal supportive care.
    4. The normal physiological route of elimination (renal or hepatic) is impaired.
- **Where?** High-dependency units, emergency casualty wards, or Intensive Care Units (ICUs).
- **Who?** Administered by Registered Medical Practitioners (RMPs), toxicologists, and nephrology teams.

### **1. Forced Diuresis and Alteration of Urinary pH** 🧪

Renal excretion of certain weak acids or bases can be enhanced by altering the pH of the urine, which prevents the reabsorption of the poison across the renal tubules (a phenomenon known as **ion trapping**).

#### **A. Forced Alkaline Diuresis**

- **Mechanism:** Administering **Sodium Bicarbonate (\(\text{NaHCO}_3\))** intravenously to raise urine pH (\(>7.5\)) and maintain a high urine output (\(3\text{–}6\text{ mL/kg/h}\)). This ionizes weak acids, trapping them in the renal tubule and preventing passive reabsorption.
- **Indications (Mnemonic: "Alka in PSM Class"):**
    - **P** - **P**henobarbitone (long-acting barbiturate)
    - **S** - **S**ulphonamides
    - **M** - **M**ethotrexate
    - **Class** - **C**hlorpropamide, **S**alicylates/NSAIDs.-aspirin

#### **B. Forced Acidic Diuresis (❌ Obsolete)**

- **Mechanism:** Historically used Ammonium Chloride to lower urine pH (\(<5.5\)) to trap weak bases.
- **Poisons Targeted (Historically):** Amphetamines, Cocaine, Strychnine, Quinine, Morphine.
- ★ **Important Exam Point:** This method is **no longer clinically recommended**. The risks of severe metabolic acidosis, cardiovascular instability, and precipitating **myoglobinuric renal failure** far outweigh its benefits.

---

### **2. Extracorporeal Elimination (Dialysis & Hemoperfusion)** 🏥

When systemic toxicity is critical or renal function is completely compromised, artificial filtering methods are deployed.

★ **Important Exam Point: Dialysis-Friendly vs. Dialysis-Unfriendly Poisons**:

|**Dialysis is USEFUL (Dialyzable)**|**Dialysis is NOT Useful (Non-dialyzable)**|
|:--|:--|
|• **M**ethanol / Ethylene Glycol• **A**lcohol (Ethanol, Isopropyl alcohol)• **S**alicylates• **B**arbiturates (Long-acting)• **L**ithium / Heavy metals• Bromide / Acetone|• **K**erosene / Hydrocarbons• **O**rganophosphorus Compounds (OPC)• **B**enzodiazepines• **A**mphetamines• **D**igoxin / Digitalis / Copper Sulfate|

#### **Hemoperfusion**

- **Mechanism:** Blood is passed directly through an extracorporeal column packed with activated charcoal or carbon/resin, which directly adsorbs the toxins.
- **Indications:** Severe poisoning due to **Paraquat**, caffeine, carbon tetrachloride (\(\text{CCl}_4\)), and hypnotic sedatives (such as short-acting barbiturates, meprobamate, or methaqualone).

---

### **3. Gastrointestinal Elimination Methods** 腸

#### **A. Whole-Bowel Irrigation (WBI)**

- **Mechanism:** Infusing large volumes of a non-absorbable osmotic solution (**Polyethylene Glycol - PEG**) either orally or via a gastric tube at a rate of **\(2\text{ L/h}\)** (\(0.5\text{ L/h}\) in children) until the rectal output runs completely clear.
- **Indications:**
    1. Ingestion of sustained-release or enteric-coated drug formulations.
    2. Heavy metal ingestions (Iron, Lead, Zinc) which are radiopaque and do not bind to charcoal.
    3. **Body Packers / Body Stuffers** carrying sealed packets of illicit drugs (Cocaine, Heroin) to prevent fatal packet rupture.

#### **B. Cathartics (Laxatives)**

- **Mechanism:** Administering osmotic agents like **Sorbitol** (\(1\text{–}2\text{ g/kg}\)) or Magnesium Sulfate (\(\text{MgSO}_4\)) to accelerate defecation and reduce the transit time of toxins in the gut.
- **Contraindications:** Ingestion of corrosives (danger of perforation) or pre-existing diarrhea. Magnesium cathartics are strictly contraindicated in renal failure.

### **Relevant Law (2023 Framework) 📜**

If a doctor is actively managing a patient undergoing elimination therapy for poisoning, they must be aware of their legal obligations:

- ★ **Section 33 Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023:** Mandates that every medical practitioner **must immediately inform the police** of all cases of suspected homicidal or suspicious poisoning.
- **Section 286 Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023:** Punishes **negligent conduct** with respect to handling poisonous substances.
- **Section 238 BNS, 2023:** Failing to preserve diagnostic samples (such as the first gastric aspirate or urine) to protect an accused from prosecution can charge the doctor with **destruction of evidence** (punishable with up to 7 years imprisonment).

### **High-Yield Exam Points**

- ★ **Alkaline Diuresis Target:** Keep the urine pH **\(>7.5\)** to trap weak acids.
- ★ **The Dialysis Exception:** Hemodialysis is highly effective for **Methanol** but completely useless for **Organophosphates** (OPC) due to their high lipid solubility and large volume of distribution.
- ★ **Acidic Diuresis Warning:** Do **not** recommend acidic diuresis in exams without stating that it is **clinically obsolete** due to the risk of myoglobinuric acute kidney injury.

### **Clinical Correlation**

👩‍⚕️ **Emergency Casualty Scenario:** A 32-year-old female is brought to the hospital in a stupor. Her husband suspects she ingested an overdose of her grandfather's **Phenobarbital** sleeping pills.

- **Doctor's Action:** The doctor secures her airway. Because Phenobarbital is a weak acid, the doctor initiates **Forced Alkaline Diuresis** using IV Sodium Bicarbonate. He monitors her urine pH to ensure it stays above 7.5. If her clinical state continues to deteriorate or she develops renal failure, the doctor immediately prepares to initiate **hemodialysis**.

### **Viva Questions & Model Answers**

**Q1: Why is hemodialysis not useful in Organophosphate (OPC) or Digoxin poisoning?**  
**Answer:** Because both OPC and Digoxin have a **high volume of distribution** (\(V_d\)) and are highly lipid-soluble, meaning they bind rapidly to tissues (fat and muscle) and are not present in high concentrations in the extracellular blood fluid to be filtered by a dialyzer.

**Q2: What is the fluid of choice for Whole-Bowel Irrigation, and what is its rate?**  
**Answer:** Polyethylene Glycol (PEG) electrolyte solution. It is administered at a rate of **\(2\text{ L/h}\) for adults** and **\(0.5\text{ L/h}\) for children**.

**Q3: Which heavy metal poisoning is an absolute contraindication for BAL therapy?**  
**Answer:** **Iron and Cadmium poisoning**, because the BAL-metal complex formed is highly toxic to the kidneys.

### **Common Mistakes Students Make**

- **Recommending Acidic Diuresis:** Students often list acidic diuresis as a routine treatment for amphetamine or cocaine poisoning. They must specify that it is **obsolete and dangerous**.
- **Confusing Dialysis Indications:** Writing that dialysis is useful for Benzodiazepines or Heroin. It is **not useful** for these drugs.

### **Quick Revision Notes (Last-minute Points)**

1. **Forced Alkaline Diuresis:** IV \(\text{NaHCO}_3\), targets urine pH \(>7.5\), traps Phenobarbital and Salicylates.
2. **Forced Acidic Diuresis:** Obsolete due to metabolic acidosis and renal failure risks.
3. **Whole-Bowel Irrigation:** Uses PEG (\(2\text{ L/h}\)); ideal for body packers and sustained-release drugs.
4. **Hemodialysis:** Highly effective for Methanol, Salicylates, Lithium, and Ethanol.
5. **Hemoperfusion:** Directly filters blood through charcoal; ideal for Paraquat.

#### **2 Marks: State the mechanism of Ion Trapping in toxicology.**

Ion trapping is a method where altering the pH of urine causes an absorbed drug to become ionized (charged) within the renal tubules. Since ionized molecules cannot passively diffuse across lipid membranes, they become "trapped" in the tubular lumen and are excreted rapidly in the urine.
