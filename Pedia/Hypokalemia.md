# Hypokalemia — Causes, Clinical Features & Management [⭐ High-Yield]

### 1. Definition [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Hypokalemia** is defined as a serum potassium concentration below **<3.5 mEq/L**.
- **Severe hypokalemia** is defined as serum potassium **<2.5 mEq/L** and represents a medical emergency due to the danger of life-threatening **cardiac arrhythmias** and **respiratory muscle paralysis**.

---

### 2. Etiology / Three Primary Causes [⭐ High-Yield]

Hypokalemia develops through three main pathophysiological mechanisms:

1. **Increased Losses (Renal and Extrarenal)**:
    
    - **Renal Losses**:
        - **Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA)**: Proximal (Type 2) or Distal (Type 1) RTA.
        - **Medications**: **Loop diuretics** (furosemide), **thiazide diuretics**, **aminoglycosides**, **amphotericin B**, and **corticosteroids**.
        - **Inherited Renal Tubulopathies**: **Bartter syndrome**, **Gitelman syndrome**, and **Liddle syndrome**.
        - **Mineralocorticoid Excess**: Cushing syndrome, primary hyperaldosteronism, and congenital adrenal hyperplasia (**CAH** due to 11-beta or 17-alpha hydroxylase deficiency).
    - **Extrarenal Losses**:
        - **Gastrointestinal Losses**: **Diarrhea**, **vomiting**, **nasogastric suction**, and **sweating**.
        - **Potassium-binding Resins**: **Sodium polystyrene sulfonate**.
2. **Transcellular / Intracellular Shift**:
    
    - **Metabolic Alkalosis**: Shifts potassium into cells in exchange for hydrogen ions.
    - **Hormonal & Pharmacological Factors**: **High insulin state**, **\(\beta_2\)-adrenergic agonists** (salbutamol), **theophylline**, and **hydroxychloroquine**.
    - **Specific Conditions**: **Refeeding syndrome**, **hypokalemic periodic paralysis**, and **thyrotoxic periodic paralysis**.
3. **Decreased Intake or Body Stores**:
    
    - **Severe Malnutrition** and **anorexia nervosa**.
    - Prolonged administration of **potassium-poor parenteral fluids**.

---

### 3. Classification

- **Mild Hypokalemia**: Serum potassium **3.0–3.4 mEq/L**.
- **Moderate Hypokalemia**: Serum potassium **2.5–2.9 mEq/L**.
- **Severe Hypokalemia**: Serum potassium **<2.5 mEq/L**.

- **Hyperpolarization**: Low extracellular potassium increases the resting membrane potential (makes it more negative), requiring a stronger stimulus for depolarization in skeletal muscle, smooth muscle, and cardiac myocytes.
- **Hypomagnesemia Link**: Concurrent **hypomagnesemia** removes normal inhibition of **ROMK channels**, leading to refractory renal potassium wasting until magnesium is repleted.

---

### 5. Clinical Features [⭐ High-Yield]

#### A. Symptoms

- General fatigue, muscle weakness, muscle cramps, myalgia, and constipation.

#### B. Signs

- **Neuromuscular System**:
    - **Hypotonia** and depressed deep tendon reflexes.
    - **Neck Flop**: Inability to hold head upright due to cervical muscle weakness.
    - Flaccid muscle paralysis progressing from lower extremities upward.
    - **Respiratory Muscle Weakness**: Intercostal and diaphragmatic weakness leading to respiratory failure.
- **Gastrointestinal System**:
    - **Abdominal Distension** and decreased bowel sounds.
    - **Paralytic Ileus**.
- **Cardiovascular System & ECG Changes**:
    - **ST Segment Depression**.
    - **T Wave Inversion or Flattening**.
    - **Prominent U Waves**.
    - **Apparent QTc Prolongation** (due to merged T and U waves).
    - Wide **P waves** and **PR interval prolongation**.

---

### 6. Diagnosis / Investigations

- **Serum Electrolytes**: Confirms serum potassium **<3.5 mEq/L**; check **serum magnesium** (to rule out refractory hypokalemia).
- **Electrocardiogram (ECG)**: Evaluates for **ST depression**, **T wave flattening**, and **prominent U waves**.
- **Urinary Potassium Handling**:
    - **Urine Potassium to Creatinine Ratio**: **<13 mEq/g** indicates extrarenal loss; **>13 mEq/g** indicates renal wasting.
    - **Transtubular Potassium Gradient (TTKG)**: **>7** indicates excess aldosterone effect; **<3** suggests extrarenal etiology.
- **Arterial Blood Gas (ABG)**: Differentiates metabolic alkalosis (vomiting, diuretics, Bartter/Gitelman) from metabolic acidosis (RTA, diarrhea).

---

### 7. Differential Diagnosis

|Feature|**Bartter Syndrome**|**Gitelman Syndrome**|**Distal RTA (Type 1)**|

|**Blood Pressure**|Normal or low|Normal or low|Normal|
|**Acid-Base Status**|Metabolic alkalosis|Metabolic alkalosis|**Metabolic acidosis** (Normal AG)|
|**Serum Magnesium**|Normal or low|**Severe Hypomagnesemia**|Normal|

### 8. Treatment / Management [⭐ High-Yield]

#### A. Non-Pharmacological Measures

- Address underlying etiology (stop diuretics, treat diarrhea/vomiting).
- Continuous ECG monitoring during intravenous replacement therapy.

#### B. Pharmacological Treatment [⭐ High-Yield]

|Drug|Dose|Route|Frequency|Duration|Key Caution|
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
|**Potassium Chloride (15% IV Solution)**|**0.5–1 mEq/kg/dose** (or **2 mEq/mL** stock)|IV Infusion|Over **1–2 hours**|Repeat based on serum K+ levels|Max rate **1 mEq/kg/hour**; max conc **60 mEq/L** (peripheral line), **80 mEq/L** (central line); avoid glucose fluids.|
|**Potassium Chloride (10% Oral Liquid)**|**2–4 mEq/kg/day**|Oral|Divided into **3–4 doses**|Until serum K+ normalizes|Dilute with juice/water to prevent GI irritation.|
|**Magnesium Sulfate (50% Solution)**|**0.3–0.5 mL/kg** (or **25–50 mg/kg**)|IM / Slow IV|Single dose|As needed|Essential for correcting concurrent hypomagnesemia to reverse refractory hypokalemia.|

---

### 9. Complications

- Cardiac arrest due to **ventricular arrhythmias**.
- **Respiratory Failure** secondary to diaphragmatic and intercostal paralysis.
- Rhabdomyolysis due to impaired skeletal muscle perfusion.

---

### 10. Prognosis

- Excellent with early recognition and appropriate potassium repletion.
- High mortality if severe hypokalemia (**<2.5 mEq/L**) is untreated, leading to fatal arrhythmias.

---

- **L** - **Low magnesium**: Refractory hypokalemia
- **E** - **Extrarenal loss**: Diarrhea, vomiting
- **M** - **Muscle weakness**: Skeletal and respiratory muscles
- **I** - **Insulin/Beta-agonists**: Drives K+ intracellularly
- **A** - **Apnea**: Secondary to respiratory muscle paralysis

### Clinical Pearl

> **Never administer IV potassium as a rapid bolus.** Intravenous potassium chloride must always be diluted and infused slowly under cardiac monitoring (max rate **1 mEq/kg/hour**) to avoid inducing fatal hyperkalemia and sudden cardiac arrest.

---

### Exam Trap

- **The Mix-up**: Treating refractory hypokalemia with higher doses of potassium alone without checking magnesium levels.
- **Distinguishing Fact**: **Hypomagnesemia** uninhibits **ROMK channels** in the distal nephron, causing ongoing renal potassium wasting. Potassium levels will not normalize until **magnesium sulfate** is co-administered.

- **Labels in order**:

    2. **T Wave**: Flattened or inverted **T wave**.
    3. **U Wave**: **Prominent U wave** following the T wave.
    4. **Apparent QTc**: Prolonged QT interval due to T-U wave fusion.
- **Arrows/connections**: Progressive drop in K+ (<2.5 mEq/L) → Flat T wave + Prominent U wave → Ventricular Ectopics → Ventricular Fibrillation.
- **Extra Mark Annotation**: Prominent **U wave** is the pathognomonic hallmark sign of severe hypokalemia on ECG.

### Quick Revision

- **Definition**: Serum K+ **<3.5 mEq/L**; severe if **<2.5 mEq/L**.
- **Three Main Causes**: Increased losses (GI/renal), transcellular shifts (alkalosis, insulin), and decreased intake.
- **Key Clinical Signs**: **Neck flop**, hypotonia, flaccid paralysis, paralytic ileus, and respiratory depression.
- **Classic ECG Triad**: **ST depression**, **flattened T waves**, and **prominent U waves**.
- **Emergency IV Therapy**: **KCl 0.5–1 mEq/kg** over 1–2 hours under ECG monitoring (never bolus).
- **Refractory Rule**: Always check and correct **serum magnesium**.

---

### One-Line Exam Opener

- **Hypokalemia is a critical pediatric electrolyte disorder defined as serum potassium <3.5 mEq/L, characterized by neuromuscular weakness, paralytic ileus, and pathognomonic ECG changes including prominent U waves.**
