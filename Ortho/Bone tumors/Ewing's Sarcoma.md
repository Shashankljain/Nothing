# Ewing's Sarcoma [⭐ High-Yield]

---

### 1. Definition [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Ewing's Sarcoma**: A highly malignant primary bone tumor belonging to the family of **primitive neuroectodermal tumors (PNET)**, arising from undifferentiated neural crest mesenchymal cells within the medullary canal of bone.
- **Age Incidence**: Most common malignant bone tumor in the **1st decade of life**; peak overall occurrence in the **2nd decade (5–20 years)**.
- **Sex Predilection**: **Males > Females** (1.5:1 ratio).
- **Anatomical Site**: Predominantly affects the **diaphysis (shaft) of long bones** (femur > tibia > humerus) and flat bones (pelvis, scapula, ribs).

---

### 2. Etiology & Genetics [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Cytogenetic Abnormality**: Reciprocal chromosomal translocation **t(11;22)(q24;q12)** is present in **>90% of cases**, creating the **EWSR1-FLI1** fusion oncogene.
- **Other Translocations**: t(21;22) and t(7;22); associated with Trisomy 8 and Trisomy 12.
- **Etiological Nature**: Non-hereditary, purely somatic gene rearrangement; no known environmental or pre-malignant precursors.

- **Gross Pathology**: Soft, greyish-white, friable tissue with extensive areas of hemorrhage and necrosis; frequently breaks down into liquid necrotic debris resembling **pus** (often misdiagnosed as acute osteomyelitis).
- **Histopathology (HPE)**: Uniform, densely packed sheets of **small round blue cells** with clear cytoplasm containing abundant glycogen (**PAS positive, diastase sensitive**); forms **Homer-Wright pseudo-rosettes**.
- **Immunohistochemistry (IHC)**: Strong membrane positivity for **MIC-2 (CD99)** (specific diagnostic marker).

---

### 4. Clinical Features [⭐ High-Yield]

#### A. Symptoms

- **Local Symptoms**: Pain and swelling over the mid-shaft (diaphysis) of the affected long bone or pelvis, progressively increasing over weeks to months.
- **Systemic Symptoms (Mimicking Osteomyelitis)**: Low-to-moderate grade **fever**, malaise, weight loss, and secondary loss of appetite.
- **History of Trauma**: Incidental minor trauma frequently precedes the onset of symptoms, drawing attention to the pre-existing swelling.

#### B. Examination Signs

- **Local Signs**: Diffuse, warm, tender, firm-to-soft swelling over the mid-diaphysis.
- **Inflammatory Signs**: Stretched overlying skin with dilated superficial veins and mild erythema.
- **Systemic Signs**: Anemia, mild leukocytosis, and elevated ESR.

---

### 5. Diagnosis & Investigations [⭐ High-Yield]

#### A. Plain Radiography (X-Ray AP & Lateral Views)

- **Primary Site**: Centered in the **diaphysis (mid-shaft)** of long bones.
- **Bone Destruction**: Permeative, "moth-eaten" lytic bone destruction within the medullary canal.
- **Periosteal Reaction**: **"Onion-Peel" / Lamellated Appearance** — concentric multiple layers of subperiosteal new bone formed in response to episodic tumor extension.
- **Soft Tissue Component**: Large, circumferential soft tissue shadow surrounding the bone.

#### B. Laboratory & Advanced Imaging

- **Blood Parameters**: Markedly elevated **ESR**, high Serum LDH (prognostic marker), and leukocytosis.
- **MRI of Affected Bone (IOC for Local Extent)**: Evaluates full longitudinal intramedullary spread and soft tissue margins.
- **High-Resolution CT Chest & Bone Scan**: Detects early pulmonary micrometastases and skip bone metastases.
- **Biopsy (Gold Standard)**: Core needle or open biopsy demonstrating **CD99 / MIC-2 positive small round blue cells** with **t(11;22)** translocation.

#### A. Multimodal Chemotherapy Protocol

Combination chemotherapy is mandatory to treat subclinical systemic micrometastases present at diagnosis.

- **VDC / IE Alternating Regimen**: **Vincristine**, **Doxorubicin (Adriamycin)**, and **Cyclophosphamide** alternating with **Ifosfamide** and **Etoposide** for 14 cycles over 9–10 months.

#### B. Local Control (Surgery vs. Radiotherapy)

1. **Limb-Salvage Surgery (Preferred)**: Wide en-bloc resection of the involved bone segment followed by reconstruction with an endoprosthesis, allograft, or vascularized fibular graft.
2. **Radiotherapy**: Ewing's sarcoma is **highly radiosensitive** (unlike osteosarcoma). Radiotherapy is utilized for inoperable lesions (e.g., pelvic or spinal locations), margin-positive surgical resections, or as definitive local therapy when surgery is unfeasible.

### 8. Complications & Prognosis

- **Distant Metastasis**: Lungs, other bones, and bone marrow are the most common metastatic sites.
- **Pathological Fracture**: Occurs secondary to severe cortical erosion in the diaphyseal shaft.
- **Secondary Malignancy**: Radiotherapy-induced secondary sarcomas (e.g., post-radiation osteosarcoma).
- **Prognosis**: Modern multimodal therapy achieves a 5-year survival rate of **~70%** for localized non-metastatic disease.

### Clinical Pearl

- A child presenting with fever, elevated ESR, leukocytosis, and a painful diaphyseal bone swelling must be evaluated very carefully: if an incisional biopsy yields liquid necrotic debris, always send it for formal histopathology rather than assuming it is bacterial pus, as **Ewing's sarcoma necrotic tissue gross appearance perfectly mimics osteomyelitis**.

---

### Exam Trap

- **The Confusion**: Mistaking **Ewing's Sarcoma** for **Acute / Chronic Osteomyelitis** due to identical clinical features (fever, pain, raised ESR, leukocytosis).
- **Distinguishing Fact**: **Biopsy** resolves the confusion—Ewing's sarcoma shows **CD99-positive small round blue cells** and **t(11;22)** translocation, whereas osteomyelitis shows purulent inflammation with bacteria on gram stain/culture and no neoplastic cells.
