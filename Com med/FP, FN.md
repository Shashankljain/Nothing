[[Screening of disease]]
In the practice of clinical medicine and public health, no screening or diagnostic test is perfect; they all produce a certain proportion of incorrect results. For an MBBS student, understanding the implications of **false-positive** and **false-negative**results is critical for patient safety, ethical practice, and efficient health resource management.

---

### **I. Definitions and the 2x2 Matrix** ★ Important Exam Point

To understand the implications, one must first define these terms using the standard "Screening test result by diagnosis" table:

|Test Result|Diseased (Actual)|Not Diseased (Actual)|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Positive**|**a (True Positive)**|**b (False Positive)**|
|**Negative**|**c (False Negative)**|**d (True Negative)**|

- **False-Positive (FP):** A person who does **not** have the disease but is wrongly identified as having it by the test.
- **False-Negative (FN):** A person who actually **has** the disease but is wrongly told they do not have it.

---

### **II. Implications of ==False-Negative== Results** ★ Important Exam Point

False-negative results are often considered more dangerous in a clinical setting because they involve a missed diagnosis.

1. **False Reassurance:** The most significant implication is that the patient is given "false reassurance," leading them to believe they are healthy when they are actually sick.
2. **Delayed Treatment:** Patients may ignore the development of new signs and symptoms, assuming their previous negative test was accurate. This delay can be detrimental or even fatal if the disease is serious (e.g., cancer or HIV).
3. **Continued Transmission:** In the case of communicable diseases (like TB or COVID-19), an FN result allows the patient to remain in the community, continuing to spread the infection to others.
4. **Relationship with Sensitivity:** The number of false negatives is inversely related to **sensitivity**. A test with low sensitivity will yield a high number of false negatives.

---

### **III. Implications of False-Positive Results** ★ Important Exam Point

While not as immediately life-threatening as a missed diagnosis, false-positive results have serious systemic and psychological consequences.

1. **Patient Distress:** FPs cause unnecessary anxiety, discomfort, and worry for the patient and their family.
2. **Economic Burden:** The patient is subjected to further, often expensive and invasive, diagnostic "work-ups" to establish their true freedom from disease.
3. **Overloading the Health System:** High numbers of false positives burden diagnostic facilities and laboratories, potentially delaying care for those who truly need it.
4. **Discredit to Health Programmes:** Frequent false-positive errors bring "discredit" to screening programmes, reducing community trust and participation in future health initiatives.
5. **Relationship with Specificity:** The number of false positives is inversely related to **specificity**. A test with high specificity will have very few false positives.

---

### **IV. Choosing the "Lesser Evil": When to Prioritise?**

In clinical practice, the choice of cut-off points (the level at which a test is called positive) depends on the nature of the disease:

- **Priority on Sensitivity (Avoid False Negatives):**
    - **When?** If the disease is highly **lethal** (e.g., cervical or breast cancer) and early detection markedly improves the prognosis.
    - **Why?** In these cases, a proportion of false-positives is "tolerable," as they can be ruled out later during the diagnostic work-up, but a false-negative is unacceptable.
- **Priority on Specificity (Avoid False Positives):**
    - **When?** In a prevalent disease like **diabetes** where early treatment does not drastically alter the long-term outcome, or when the diagnostic work-up is very expensive.
    - **Why?** To prevent the health system from being "overburdened" with diagnostic demands from both true and false positives.

### **VI. Clinically Relevant Examples from Sources**

- **COVID-19:** A single negative RT-PCR result does not completely exclude infection if the clinical presentation is high; repeat testing is often required because false negatives can occur.
- **Tuberculosis (TB):** False-positive sputum smears can occur due to "environmental mycobacteria" or red stain retained by scratches on the slide. False-negatives often occur due to "faulty sampling" or inadequate time spent examining the slide.
- **Syphilis:** To avoid the implications of FPs, a two-step approach is used: a highly sensitive test (RPR) is used first, and those who test positive are confirmed with a more specific test (FTA-ABS).

---

### **High-Yield Exam Points** ★

- **Lead Time:** The extra time gained by early detection is wasted if an FN result occurs.
- **Prevalence Effect:** The "Positive Predictive Value" (the chance that a positive test is correct) falls as the prevalence of a disease in the community declines.
- **Rule of Thumb:** Sensitivity "Sees" the diseased (prevents FNs); Specificity "Spotted" the healthy (prevents FPs).

---

### **Frequently Asked Viva Questions**

- **Q: Which is worse in a cancer screening programme, an FP or an FN?**
    - **A:** An **FN (False-Negative)** is worse, because the patient loses the chance for an early cure. An FP can be corrected by a follow-up biopsy.
- **Q: Why does the WHO advise against indiscriminate mass screening?**
    - **A:** Because if the disease cannot be effectively treated, the screening effort merely creates FPs and FNs without altering the final outcome, causing unnecessary cost and anxiety.
- **Q: How can we reduce False-Negative results in TB microscopy?**
    - **A:** By examining at least **two** sputum specimens (one spot, one early morning) and ensuring the microscopist does not examine more than 20 slides per day to avoid fatigue.

---

### **Common Mistakes Students Make**

- **Thinking Sensitivity = Accuracy:** Sensitivity only measures the ability to catch the sick. True accuracy involves **both** sensitivity and specificity.
- **Confusing the terms:** Writing that an FN is a "healthy person who tested sick." (That is an **FP**).
- **Ignoring the "Denominator":** Forgetting that the number of FPs and FNs is directly influenced by the **prevalence**of the disease in the population being tested.
