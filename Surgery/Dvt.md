# Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)

### Definition

**Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)** is defined as the formation of a semisolid coagulum (thrombus) within the lumen of the deep veins of the body. It most commonly affects the deep veins of the lower limb (peroneal, anterior/posterior tibial, popliteal, femoral, and iliac veins).

---

### Basic Surgical Anatomy of Lower Limb Deep Veins

- **Distal Deep System (Calf Veins)**: Formed by three pairs of venae comitantes accompanying the crural arteries—the **peroneal**, **anterior tibial**, and **posterior tibial veins**. These run deep to the deep fascia and carry approximately 80% of lower limb venous return.
- **Proximal Deep System**:
    - The crural veins unite in the popliteal fossa to form the **popliteal vein**, which also receives soleal and gastrocnemius venous sinuses.
    - The popliteal vein ascends through the adductor hiatus to become the **femoral vein** in the subsartorial canal.
    - The femoral vein receives the **profunda femoris vein** to form the **common femoral vein (CFV)** in the groin.
    - The CFV passes deep to the inguinal ligament to continue as the **external iliac vein**, which joins the internal iliac vein to form the **common iliac vein**, ultimately draining into the **Inferior Vena Cava (IVC)**.

---

### Etiology / Causes / Risk Factors [⭐ High-Yield]

The etiology of DVT is governed by **Virchow's Triad**, comprising three primary pathophysiological mechanisms:

```
                  VIRCHOW'S TRIAD
                       /   \
                      /     \
                     /       \
        1. Stasis ------- 2. Endothelial Injury
                    \       /
                     \     /
                      \   /
              3. Hypercoagulability
```

#### 1. Venous Stasis

- Prolonged immobility (bedridden >3 days, long-haul flights/travel).
- Major surgical procedures (especially total hip or knee arthroplasty, pelvic surgery, total anesthesia time >90 minutes).
- Lower limb paralysis (e.g., stroke, spinal cord injury).
- Congestive heart failure, obesity (BMI >30 kg/m²), advanced age (>60 years), and pregnancy/puerperium.

#### 2. Endothelial Injury

- Direct operative or accidental trauma to the pelvis, hip, or lower limb bones.
- Previous history of DVT (damaged venous endothelium and destroyed valves).
- Central venous line placement or intravenous catheterization.

#### 3. Hypercoagulability (Prothrombotic State / Thrombophilia)

- **Congenital / Inherited**:
    - _Factor V Leiden Mutation_ (Activated Protein C resistance; present in 6%–7% of Caucasians, most common inherited cause).
    - _Deficiencies of Antithrombin III, Protein C, or Protein S_.
    - _Prothrombin Gene Mutation (G20210A)_ and _Dysfibrinogenemia_.
- **Acquired**:
    - _Malignancy_ (especially pelvic, abdominal, or metastatic carcinomas; release of tissue factor).
    - _High-dose estrogen therapy_ (oral contraceptive pills, hormone replacement therapy).
    - _Antiphospholipid Syndrome / Lupus Anticoagulant_.
    - _Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)_, _Nephrotic Syndrome_, _Polycythemia Vera_, _Paraproteinemia_, and _Behçet's Disease_.
    - _May-Thurner Syndrome_ (compression of the left common iliac vein by the overlying right common iliac artery).

---

### Classification

#### 1. Anatomical Classification

- **Distal (Calf) DVT**: Thrombus confined to the calf veins (crural, soleal, or gastrocnemius veins) below the popliteal fossa. Lower risk of pulmonary embolism (~10%), but can propagate proximally in 20% of cases.
- **Proximal DVT**: Thrombus involving the popliteal, femoral, or iliac veins. Carries a significantly higher risk of life-threatening pulmonary embolism (up to 50%) and severe post-thrombotic syndrome.

#### 2. Clinical Severity Classification

- **Uncomplicated DVT**: Localized swelling and pain without microvascular compromise.
- **Phlegmasia Alba Dolens ("Milk Leg")**: Severe thrombosis of major axial deep veins with preservation of collateral veins; the limb is markedly edematous, painful, and white/pale (commonly seen in pregnancy or iliofemoral occlusion).
- **Phlegmasia Cerulea Dolens**: Massive, near-total thrombosis of both major axial deep veins AND collateral venous pathways. Causes intense pain, massive swelling, and dark blue/cyanotic discoloration, leading to arterial insufficiency, venous gangrene, and shock.

- **Thrombus Nidus & Propagation**: Thrombi typically originate in low-flow pockets behind bicuspid venous valves or within soleal venous sinuses. Platelets aggregate on altered endothelium, catching RBCs and leukocytes within a fibrin meshwork to produce a **coralline thrombus**, which then propagates proximally as a loose red fibrin clot toward major venous junctions.
- **Hemodynamic Changes**: Venous outflow blockage elevates capillary hydrostatic pressure, leading to fluid extravasation into subcutaneous tissues (pitting edema).
- **Ischemic Sequel**: In phlegmasia cerulea dolens, extreme venous hypertension exceeds tissue capillary perfusion pressure, precipitating acute compartment syndrome, tissue ischemia, and venous gangrene.

### Clinical Features [⭐ High-Yield]

#### Symptoms

- **Leg Swelling**: Typically unilateral, sudden in onset, and progressive; most commonly affecting the calf, but extending to the thigh in iliofemoral DVT.
- **Leg Pain**: Present in ~50% of patients; described as a dull aching, heaviness, or bursting sensation in the calf or thigh.
- **Warmth and Erythema**: Feeling of heat and reddish/bluish discoloration over the affected limb segment.

#### Signs

- **Unilateral Pitting Edema**: Calf circumference **>3 cm larger** than the asymptomatic side (measured 10 cm below the tibial tuberosity).
- **Tenderness**: Localized tenderness on deep palpation along the course of the deep venous system (femoral triangle, popliteal fossa, and medial calf).
- **Moses' Sign**: Pain elicited on squeezing the calf muscles anteroposteriorly against the tibia.
- **Homans' Sign**: Resistance or pain in the calf on forcible passive dorsiflexion of the foot (neither sensitive nor specific; forcible dorsiflexion should be avoided due to theoretical embolization risk).
- **Collateral Superficial Veins**: Non-varicose, dilated collateral surface veins visible over the shin or lower abdomen.
- **Low-Grade Pyrexia**: Fever without an obvious infective focus (a classic cause of Post-Operative Day 4–5 fever).

### Diagnosis / Investigations [⭐ High-Yield]

#### 1. Diagnostic Scoring Criteria: Two-Level DVT Wells Score

|Clinical Feature|Points|
|:--|:--|
|Active cancer (treatment ongoing, within 6 months, or palliative)|+1|
|Paralysis, paresis, or recent plaster immobilization of lower extremities|+1|
|Recently bedridden for >3 days, or major surgery within 12 weeks requiring general/regional anesthesia|+1|
|Localized tenderness along the distribution of the deep venous system|+1|
|Entire leg swollen|+1|
|Calf swelling at least >3 cm larger than asymptomatic side (10 cm below tibial tuberosity)|+1|
|Pitting edema confined to the symptomatic leg|+1|
|Collateral superficial veins (non-varicose)|+1|
|Previously documented DVT|+1|
|Alternative diagnosis at least as likely as DVT (e.g., cellulitis, Baker's cyst, hematoma)|-2|

- **Score Categorization**:
    - **DVT Likely**: Score ≥2 points (High probability: 17%–53% risk). Proceed directly to Duplex Ultrasound.
    - **DVT Unlikely**: Score ≤1 point (Low probability: ~5% risk). Perform D-Dimer test first.

#### 2. Laboratory Findings

- **D-Dimer Assay**:
    - Cross-linked fibrin degradation product.
    - **High Negative Predictive Value (>99%)**: A normal D-Dimer level (<500 ng/mL) safely excludes DVT in patients categorized as "DVT Unlikely" by Wells score.
    - _Limitation_: Low specificity; elevated in postoperative states, trauma, malignancy, pregnancy, and sepsis.
- **Coagulation Screen & Baseline Labs**: Full blood count, PT/INR, aPTT, serum creatinine, and liver function tests prior to initiating anticoagulation.
- **Thrombophilia Workup**: Indicated in unprovoked DVT, young patients (<50 years), or those with a strong family history. Includes Factor V Leiden, Antithrombin III, Protein C & S levels, and Antiphospholipid antibodies.

#### 3. Imaging

- **Duplex Doppler Ultrasound (Color Doppler + B-Mode USG)** _(Investigation of Choice / Gold Standard)_:
    - **Key Diagnostic Finding**: **Non-compressibility of the vein** under probe compression (a normal vein collapses completely; a thrombosed vein remains rigid).
    - Direct visualization of intraluminal echogenic thrombus.
    - Absence or loss of normal respiratory phasic flow on spectral/color Doppler.
- **Computed Tomography Venography (CTV) / Magnetic Resonance Venography (MRV)**:
    - Indicated when duplex USG is inconclusive, or for assessing pelvic/iliac vein thrombosis, IVC involvement, or May-Thurner syndrome.
- **Ascending Contrast Venography**:
    - Historical gold standard; invasive injection of radiopaque dye into a foot vein demonstrating constant intraluminal filling defects. Currently reserved for endovascular planning.

#### 1. Pharmacological Anticoagulation (Mainstay of Treatment)

- **Initial Rapid Anticoagulation**:
    - Start immediately with **Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin (LMWH)** (e.g., Enoxaparin 1 mg/kg SC 12-hourly or 1.5 mg/kg SC 24-hourly) or **Unfractionated Heparin (UFH)** (preferred in severe renal failure with eGFR <30 mL/min).
- **Transition to Oral Anticoagulant**:
    - **Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs)** (e.g., Rivaroxaban, Apixaban, Dabigatran): First-line agents. Rivaroxaban and Apixaban can be used as monotherapy without prior heparin bridge.
    - **Warfarin (Vitamin K Antagonist)**: If DOACs are unavailable.
    - **Crucial Bridge Rule**: LMWH must be overlapped with Warfarin for **at least 5 days** AND until the INR is within therapeutic range (2.0–3.0) for **2 consecutive days**. (Warfarin initially depletes Protein C and S, creating a transient prothrombotic state).
- **Duration of Anticoagulation**:
    - _Provoked DVT_ (transient risk factor like surgery/trauma): **3 months**.
    - _Unprovoked DVT / Active Cancer / Recurrent DVT_: **6 months to Lifelong**.

#### 2. Thrombolytic Therapy

- **Catheter-Directed Thrombolysis (CDT)**:
    - Infusion of alteplase (tPA), streptokinase, or urokinase directly into the clot via a popliteal/femoral catheter.
    - _Indications_: Massive proximal iliofemoral DVT, phlegmasia cerulea dolens, early presentation (<14 days), low bleeding risk. Significantly reduces long-term Post-Thrombotic Syndrome (PTS).

#### 3. Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) Filters (e.g., Greenfield Filter)

Placed percutaneously under image guidance into the infrarenal IVC to trap emboli:

- **Indications**:
    1. Absolute contraindication to anticoagulation (e.g., recent hemorrhagic stroke, active massive GI bleeding).
    2. Recurrent pulmonary embolism despite adequate, therapeutic anticoagulation.
    3. Major complications secondary to anticoagulation.
    4. Free-floating extensive iliofemoral venous thrombus.

#### 4. Surgical Intervention

- **Venous Thrombectomy**: Operative clot extraction reserved for phlegmasia cerulea dolens with impending gangrene where thrombolysis is contraindicated or has failed.

#### 5. Prophylaxis Protocol (Prevention) [⭐ High-Yield]

All surgical inpatients must undergo VTE risk assessment within 24 hours of admission:

- **Mechanical Methods**: Early mobilization, graduated compression stockings (Class I/II, 14–24 mmHg), and intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) devices.
- **Pharmacological Prophylaxis**: LMWH (Enoxaparin 40 mg SC once daily) started 6–12 hours pre-operatively or post-operatively in moderate to high-risk surgical patients.

---

### Complications [⭐ High-Yield]

#### Acute Complications

1. **Pulmonary Embolism (PE)**: Most dangerous, life-threatening complication; occurs when a propagated clot breaks off and occludes the pulmonary arterial tree.
2. **Venous Gangrene**: Tissue necrosis resulting from total microvascular occlusion in phlegmasia cerulea dolens.

#### Chronic Complications

1. **Post-Thrombotic Syndrome (PTS) / Post-Thrombotic Limb**:
    - Occurs in up to 30%–50% of DVT patients within 2 years due to permanent destruction of venous valves and persistent venous occlusion.
    - _Features_: Chronic ambulatory venous hypertension, persistent calf swelling, varicose collaterals, skin hyperpigmentation (hemosiderosis), **lipodermatosclerosis** (woody induration with an **"inverted champagne bottle" leg**), and non-healing **venous leg ulcers** in the gaiter area.
2. **Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension (CTEPH)**: Progressive pulmonary vascular resistance and right heart failure secondary to unresolved, organized pulmonary emboli.

---

### Prognosis

With early diagnosis and prompt therapeutic anticoagulation, the 30-day mortality rate for uncomplicated DVT is <2%. Mortality is predominantly driven by massive pulmonary embolism or underlying terminal malignancy.

### Dosage Rule (Anticoagulants & Thrombolytics)

#### 1. Enoxaparin (Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin - LMWH)

- **Name**: Enoxaparin (Clexane)
- **Mechanism of Action**: Binds to antithrombin III, enhancing its inhibition of Factor Xa and Factor IIa (thrombin); Factor Xa to IIa anti-ratio is 3:1.
- **Dose**:
    - _Therapeutic DVT Dose_: 1 mg/kg body weight 12-hourly, OR 1.5 mg/kg once daily.
    - _Prophylactic DVT Dose_: 40 mg (0.4 mL) once daily.
- **Dosage / Route**: Subcutaneous (SC) injection into the abdominal wall.
- **Frequency**: 12-hourly or 24-hourly.
- **Features**: Predictable bioavailability; does not require routine laboratory coagulation monitoring (aPTT).
- **Side Effects**: Bleeding, Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT - less common than UFH), osteoporosis with prolonged use, injection site hematoma.
- **When to Start / Stop**: Start immediately upon confirming DVT; continue for at least 5 days until oral anticoagulants achieve therapeutic levels.

#### 2. Warfarin

- **Name**: Warfarin
- **Mechanism of Action**: Vitamin K antagonist; competitively inhibits Vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1), blocking gamma-carboxylation of Vitamin K-dependent clotting factors (**II, VII, IX, X**) and anticoagulant proteins C and S.
- **Dose**: 5 mg daily initial dose, adjusted according to International Normalized Ratio (INR).
- **Dosage / Route**: Oral tablet.
- **Frequency**: Once daily in the evening.
- **Features**: Requires strict therapeutic drug monitoring; target **INR = 2.0 to 3.0**.
- **Side Effects**: Major bleeding, skin necrosis (due to rapid Protein C depletion), teratogenicity (fetal warfarin syndrome; contraindicated in pregnancy).
- **When to Start / Stop**: Start on Day 1 alongside LMWH; stop LMWH only after INR is 2.0–3.0 for 2 consecutive days. Continue Warfarin for 3 to 6 months (or lifelong).

#### 3. Rivaroxaban

- **Name**: Rivaroxaban (Xarelto)
- **Mechanism of Action**: Direct, highly selective oral Factor Xa inhibitor; inhibits both free and clot-bound Factor Xa.
- **Dose**: 15 mg twice daily for the first 21 days, followed by 20 mg once daily.
- **Dosage / Route**: Oral tablet (taken with food).
- **Frequency**: 12-hourly for 21 days, then once daily.
- **Features**: No parenteral LMWH bridging required; no routine coagulation monitoring needed.
- **Side Effects**: Bleeding, anemia, elevated transaminases.
- **When to Start / Stop**: Start immediately upon DVT diagnosis; continue for 3 to 6 months.

### Clinical Pearl

In any patient presenting with acute unilateral lower limb swelling, **always measure calf circumference 10 cm below the tibial tuberosity**. A side-to-side difference **>3 cm** is the single most reliable physical exam finding supporting a clinical diagnosis of DVT.

---

### Exam Trap

- **The Mix-up**: Discontinuing Heparin/LMWH immediately on starting Warfarin therapy on Day 1.
- **The Distinguishing Fact**: Warfarin inhibits Protein C and S (which have short half-lives) faster than Factors II and X. Starting Warfarin without Heparin coverage induces a temporary hypercoagulable state that can lead to **Warfarin-induced skin necrosis** and thrombus propagation. **Always overlap Heparin with Warfarin for at least 5 days and until INR is ≥2.0 for 48 hours.**

### Quick Revision

- **Etiology**: Driven by Virchow's Triad—Venous Stasis, Endothelial Damage, and Hypercoagulability.
- **Gold Standard Diagnostic Test**: Duplex Doppler Ultrasound demonstrating **non-compressibility of the vein**.
- **Wells Criteria Cutoff**: Score ≥2 = DVT Likely (USG indicated); Score ≤1 = DVT Unlikely (D-Dimer indicated).
- **Primary Medical Management**: LMWH bridged with Warfarin (for 5 days until INR 2.0–3.0) or monotherapy with DOACs (Rivaroxaban).
- **Major Complications**: Acute = Pulmonary Embolism (PE); Chronic = Post-Thrombotic Syndrome (PTS) with lipodermatosclerosis.

---

### One-Line Exam Opener

_Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is the acute formation of an occlusive thrombus within the deep venous system of the lower limb, triggered by Virchow's triad of stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability, diagnosed definitively by non-compressible duplex ultrasonography, and managed primarily via prompt therapeutic anticoagulation._
