# Crush Syndrome

## 1. Definition

**Crush Syndrome** (also known as _Bywaters' syndrome_ or _traumatic rhabdomyolysis_) is the systemic manifestation of muscle breakdown resulting from prolonged crushing force applied to skeletal muscle. First classicially described by Eric Bywaters during the _London Blitz_ (1940–1941), it is characterized by the sudden release of toxic intracellular muscle contents into the systemic circulation upon decompression (reperfusion injury), leading to hypovolemic shock, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, myoglobinuria, and acute kidney injury (AKI).

---

## 2. Etiology / Causes / Risk Factors [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Mass Disasters & Traumatic Entrapment:** Structural collapses during earthquakes, mining accidents, explosions, mass-casualty bombing events, and severe road traffic accidents with prolonged entrapment (>1–2 hours).
- **Non-Traumatic Prolonged Immobilization:** Unconscious states secondary to drug overdose, alcohol intoxication, or cerebrovascular accidents leading to limb compression under body weight.
- **High-Energy Non-Crush Muscle Injuries:** Severe high-voltage electrical burns, circumferential third-degree burns, or acute compartment syndrome.

---

## 3. Pathophysiology (with Flowchart) [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Ischemic Phase:** Prolonged external pressure (>1–2 hours) compromises microvascular muscle perfusion, producing cellular hypoxia, ATP depletion, and failure of membrane Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pumps.
2. **Reperfusion Injury ("Extrication Shock"):** Release of the compressing weight restores circulation to the ischemic limb, triggering massive local capillary leak and releasing accumulated intracellular toxins into the systemic bloodstream.
3. **Third-Space Fluid Sequestration:** Up to 10–12 liters of fluid shifts rapidly into damaged muscle beds, producing profound intravascular hypovolemia, shock, and secondary renal vasoconstriction.
4. **Myoglobinuria & Acute Kidney Injury:** Free myoglobin is filtered at the glomerulus. In the presence of acidic urine, myoglobin reacts with Tamm-Horsfall proteins to form obstructive intratubular casts, causing direct tubular toxicity, acute tubular necrosis (ATN), and acute kidney injury.
5. **Electrolyte & Acid-Base Disturbance:**
    - _Hyperkalemia:_ Massive cardiac-toxic K⁺ surge from lysed myocytes.
    - _Hyperphosphatemia & Early Hypocalcemia:_ Phosphate release binds serum Ca²⁺, depositing calcium phosphate into necrotic muscle.
    - _Lactic Acidosis:_ Release of accumulated anaerobic metabolic waste.

## 4. Clinical Features

### A. Local Features (Crushed Limb)

- Swollen, tense, firm, and cold limb with skin pallor or purple/mottled ecchymosis.
- Severe pain out of proportion initially, progressing to loss of sensation (paraesthesia) and paralysis.
- Diminished or absent distal peripheral pulses (indicating secondary compartment syndrome).

### B. Systemic Features

- **Hypovolemia & Shock:** Tachycardia (>100/min), hypotension (systolic BP <90 mmHg), pale cool peripheries, prolonged capillary refill (>2 seconds).
- **Renal:** Oliguria (<0.5 mL/kg/h) or complete anuria; passage of dark red or tea-colored ("port-wine") urine due to myoglobinuria.
- **Cardiac:** Palpitations, bradycardia, or sudden cardiac arrest secondary to hyperkalemia.
- **Central Nervous System:** Restlessness, confusion, and drowsiness secondary to cerebral hypoperfusion and metabolic acidosis.

---

## 5. Diagnosis / Investigations [⭐ High-Yield]

### A. Laboratory Findings

- **Serum Creatinine Kinase (CK):** Most sensitive diagnostic marker. Serum CK levels are massively elevated (>10,000 U/L to >100,000 U/L).
- **Urine Analysis:** Port-wine or dark tea-colored urine. Urine dipstick is **strongly positive for blood / hemoglobin**(due to myoglobin cross-reactivity) but microscopic urine examination shows **absence of red blood cells**.
- **Serum Electrolytes:** Marked hyperkalemia (K⁺ >5.5 mEq/L), hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia, and early hypocalcemia.
- **Renal Function Tests:** Elevated blood urea (>20 mmol/L) and serum creatinine (>177 μmol/L or >2.0 mg/dL), confirming KDIGO Stage 2/3 AKI.
- **Arterial Blood Gas (ABG):** Metabolic acidosis with negative base excess (base deficit >4–6 mmol/L) and elevated serum lactate (>3–5 mmol/L).

### B. Imaging & Instrumental Tests

- **Ultrasound & Duplex Doppler:** Evaluates deep muscle swelling, hematomas, and confirms arterial patency.
- **Electrocardiogram (ECG):** Demonstrates classic hyperkalemic changes: tall peaked T waves, prolonged PR interval, widening of QRS complex, loss of P waves, and sine-wave pattern preceding ventricular fibrillation/cardiac arrest.

## 7. Treatment / Management (Rationale) [⭐ High-Yield]

### A. Pre-Extrication Resuscitation (Golden Rule)

- **Immediate Intravenous Fluid Loading:** Resuscitation **must begin BEFORE or during extrication** while the casualty is still trapped.
- _Rationale:_ Releasing the weight without prior hydration causes immediate "extrication collapse" due to sudden third-space fluid sequestration and lethal hyperkalemic cardiac arrest.
- _Protocol:_ Initiate 0.9% Normal Saline at **1000–1500 mL/h in adults**.

### B. Post-Extrication & Forced Alkaline Diuresis

- **Urinary Catheterization:** Insert Foley catheter immediately to monitor hourly urine output.
- **Forced Alkaline Diuresis Protocol:** Maintain a mannitol-alkaline diuresis of **up to 8 liters per day** once urine flow is confirmed.
- _Target Objectives:_ Maintain urine output **>200 mL/h** and urinary **pH >6.5**.
- _Rationale:_ Alkalinization of urine (pH >6.5) prevents myoglobin breakdown into toxic ferrihemate and blocks precipitation with Tamm-Horsfall proteins, preventing tubular obstruction.

### C. Renal Replacement Therapy (Hemodialysis)

- Indicated for refractory hyperkalemia (K⁺ >6.5 mEq/L), severe uncorrected metabolic acidosis (pH <7.1), fluid overload with pulmonary edema, or persistent anuric AKI despite fluid therapy.

### D. Surgical Management & Fasciotomy Rules [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Early Fasciotomy (<12 Hours Entrapment):** Indicated only if there is objective clinical or pressure evidence of active compartment syndrome in a viable limb, to decompress compartments and restore microperfusion.
- **Late Fasciotomy (>12 Hours Entrapment):** **STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED**. When entrapment exceeds 12 hours, the crushed muscle is already non-viable. Performing a late fasciotomy causes a massive release of accumulated myoglobin into circulation, produces uncontrollable bleeding from dead muscle beds, and introduces fatal secondary bacterial infection (gas gangrene) into necrotic tissue. Non-viable limbs require delayed amputation instead.

---

## 8. Complications

- **Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) & Tubular Necrosis:** Secondary to myoglobinuria, hypovolemia, and acid cast obstruction.
- **Lethal Hyperkalemic Cardiac Arrest:** Severe arrhythmogenesis within minutes of extrication.
- **Abdominal & Limb Compartment Syndrome:** Severe swelling within tight osteofascial compartments.
- **Gas Gangrene & Necrotizing Sepsis:** _Clostridium perfringens_ or polymicrobial infection of devitalized muscle.
- **Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) & MODS:** Cytokine release triggering acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and DIC.

---

## 9. Prognosis

- Without pre-extrication fluid resuscitation, mortality from extrication collapse and acute renal failure exceeds 50–70%.
- With early pre-extrication saline infusion, forced alkaline diuresis, and modern hemodialysis support, survival rates exceed **80–90%**, with full recovery of renal function in most survivors.

---

## 10. Clinical Pearl

In crush injury, **never wait for extrication before starting IV resuscitation**. The single most effective action to save life and prevent acute renal failure is to establish wide-bore IV access and infuse 0.9% Normal Saline at 1000–1500 mL/h while the patient is still trapped. Releasing a compressed limb without prior IV hydration triggers "extrication shock"—a sudden surge of lethal hyperkalemia and myoglobin flooding into an empty circulation, causing instant cardiac arrest.

---

## 11. Exam Trap

### Exam Trap

- **The Mix-Up:** Assuming that every crushed limb requires an immediate **fasciotomy**.
- **Distinguishing Fact:**
    - _Early Fasciotomy (<12 hours):_ Beneficial to decompress viable muscle with compartment syndrome.
    - _Late Fasciotomy (>12 hours entrapment):_ **CONTRAINDICATED**. In dead muscle, late fasciotomy converts a closed sterile wound into an open infected wound, triggers catastrophic unmanageable bleeding, and floods the systemic circulation with lethal amounts of myoglobin.

### Quick Revision

- **Definition:** Systemic manifestation of traumatic rhabdomyolysis following prolonged muscle compression (>1–2 hrs); causes hypovolemic shock, hyperkalemia, myoglobinuria, and AKI.
- **Golden Pre-Extrication Rule:** Start IV Normal Saline (1000–1500 mL/h) **before** releasing the crushing weight to prevent extrication shock and cardiac arrest.
- **Forced Alkaline Diuresis:** Administer IV Bicarbonate + Mannitol once urine flow is present; target urine pH >6.5 and urine output >200 mL/h to prevent myoglobin precipitation.
- **Fasciotomy Rule:** Early fasciotomy (<12 hrs) decompresses viable muscle; late fasciotomy (>12 hrs entrapment) is **contraindicated** due to lethal myoglobin surges, hemorrhage, and infection.
- **Dialysis Indication:** Refractory hyperkalemia (K⁺ >6.5 mEq/L), persistent metabolic acidosis, or anuric AKI.
