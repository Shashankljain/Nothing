# Groin Hernia Classification & Management of Femoral Hernia

### Definition [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Groin Hernia**: A protrusion of abdominal viscera or extraperitoneal fat through a defect in the anterolateral abdominal wall or pelvic floor within the groin region (comprising inguinal and femoral regions).
- **Femoral Hernia**: A form of groin hernia characterized by the protrusion of a peritoneal sac (containing bowel or omentum) or extraperitoneal fat through the **femoral ring** into the **femoral canal**, entering below the inguinal ligament and lying medial to the femoral vein.

---

### Etiology / Causes / Risk Factors [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Gender & Pelvic Anatomy** [⭐ High-Yield]: Femoral hernias account for approximately **20% of all groin hernias in women**, compared to **<1% in men**. This marked female preponderance is due to the wider female bony pelvis, larger femoral ring diameter, and structural changes during pregnancy.
- **Increased Intra-Abdominal Pressure**: Chronic cough (COPD), chronic constipation, urinary outflow obstruction (benign prostatic hyperplasia), ascites, heavy manual lifting, and recurrent pregnancies.
- **Tissue Degradation & Aging**: Loss of tissue elasticity, altered collagen metabolism (type I to type III collagen shift), and progressive weakness of the transversalis fascia and aponeurotic structures with advancing age.
- **Previous Inguinal Hernia Repair**: Alteration of groin anatomy following open anterior inguinal hernia repair (such as Lichtenstein repair) can increase stress on the femoral ring, predisposing to secondary femoral herniation.

---

### Classification & Enumeration of Groin Hernias [⭐ High-Yield]

Groin hernias are anatomically and pathologically enumerated as follows:

```
                            CLASSIFICATION OF GROIN HERNIAS
                                           |
     +-------------------------------------+-------------------------------------+
     |                                                                           |
Inguinal Hernias (Above Inguinal Ligament)                     Femoral Hernias (Below Inguinal Ligament)
* Indirect (Lateral / Oblique) Inguinal Hernia                 * Reducible Femoral Hernia
* Direct (Medial) Inguinal Hernia                              * Irreducible / Incarcerated Femoral Hernia
* Pantaloon (Dual / Combined Direct + Indirect)                * Strangulated / Gangrenous Femoral Hernia
```

#### 1. Inguinal Hernias (Upper Groin / Above Inguinal Ligament) [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Indirect (Lateral / Oblique) Inguinal Hernia**: Protrudes through the deep inguinal ring lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels, traversing the entire length of the inguinal canal inside the spermatic cord sheath into the scrotum.
- **Direct (Medial) Inguinal Hernia**: Protrudes directly forward through a weakened transversalis fascia in Hesselbach's triangle medial to the inferior epigastric vessels.
- **Pantaloon (Dual / Combined) Hernia**: Coexistence of both a direct and an indirect hernia sac separated by the inferior epigastric vessels on the same side.

#### 2. Femoral Hernia (Lower Groin / Below Inguinal Ligament) [⭐ High-Yield]

- Protrudes through the femoral ring into the femoral canal, emerging through the saphenous opening below the inguinal ligament.

#### 3. Rare / Special Groin Hernias

- **Obturator Hernia**: Protrudes through the obturator canal; presents with inner thigh pain radiating to the knee (**Howship-Romberg sign**).
- **Spigelian Hernia**: Protrudes through the Spigelian aponeurosis (linea semilunaris) near the arcuate line.
- **Perineal Hernia**: Protrudes through the levator ani or pelvic floor defects into the ischiorectal fossa or labium majus.

#### 4. European Hernia Society (EHS) Groin Hernia Classification [⭐ High-Yield]

Categorizes all groin hernias based on anatomical site, origin, and defect size:

- **Primary or Recurrent**: Designated as **P** (Primary) or **R** (Recurrent).
- **Anatomical Site**: Designated as **L** (Lateral/Indirect), **M** (Medial/Direct), or **F** (Femoral).
- **Defect Size**:
    - _Grade 1_: ≤1.5 cm (1 fingerbreadth).
    - _Grade 2_: 1.5–3 cm (1 to 3 fingerbreadths).
    - _Grade 3_: ≥3 cm (≥3 fingerbreadths). _(Example: A primary femoral hernia with a 2 cm defect is classified as **PF2**)_.

#### Anatomical Boundaries of the Femoral Canal [⭐ High-Yield]

The femoral canal is the rigid medial compartment of the femoral sheath (normally containing fat and the **node of Cloquet**) bounded by:

- **Anteriorly**: Inguinal ligament (Poupart's).
- **Posteriorly**: Pectineal ligament (Astley Cooper's) covering the pectineal line of the pubic bone.
- **Medially**: Sharp, crescentic free edge of the **lacunar ligament (Gimbernat's)**.
- **Laterally**: Femoral vein (separated by a fibrous septum).

#### Pathological Trajectory & High Risk of Strangulation

1. **Course**: The hernia sac descends vertically through the rigid femoral ring into the femoral canal. At the saphenous opening (fossa ovalis), it is prevented from descending further into the thigh by the attachment of cribriform fascia; it turns anteriorly and **curves superiorly over the inguinal ligament** into the subcutaneous tissue.
2. **Strangulation Mechanism**: Because the femoral ring is surrounded by rigid unyielding structures—especially the sharp, unyielding medial edge of **Gimbernat's lacunar ligament**—femoral hernias have the highest rate of incarceration and strangulation among all groin hernias.
3. **Richter's Hernia**: Commonly presents as a **Richter's type strangulation**, where only a portion of the circumference of the bowel wall (antimesenteric border) is trapped and strangulated within the narrow neck, causing bowel gangrene without complete mechanical intestinal obstruction.

---

### Clinical Features [⭐ High-Yield]

#### Symptoms

- **Groin Pain & Mass**: Painful or painless small bulge in the upper inner thigh/groin below the inguinal crease.
- **Acute Intestinal Obstruction**: Nausea, bilious vomiting, crampy abdominal pain, and abdominal distension.
- **Unexplained Obstruction in Elderly Females**: A strangulated femoral hernia frequently presents as unexplained small bowel obstruction without localized pain in elderly or obese women.

#### Physical Signs

- **Inspection & Site**: Globular swelling situated **below and lateral to the pubic tubercle**.
- **Cough Impulse**: Expansile cough impulse is **frequently ABSENT** because the narrow neck of the sac is tightly gripped by the rigid femoral ring.
- **Reducibility**: Highly irreducible; >50% of cases present as irreducible or incarcerated masses.
- **Strangulation Signs**: Severe localized tenderness, skin erythema, warmth, and brawny induration over the mass indicate gangrenous bowel.

---

### Diagnosis / Investigations [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Clinical Examination [Gold Standard Diagnostic Method]**: Differentiated from an inguinal hernia by establishing its relationship to the **pubic tubercle**:
    - _Femoral Hernia_: Emerges **below and lateral** to the pubic tubercle.
    - _Inguinal Hernia_: Emerges **above and medial** to the pubic tubercle.
- **Ultrasonography (USG Groin)**: Useful in obese patients to visualize the defect and confirm location relative to the femoral vein.
- **Computed Tomography (CT Abdomen & Pelvis)** [⭐ High-Yield]: CT scanning is the investigation of choice in patients presenting with unexplained small bowel obstruction to identify a small, occult strangulated femoral hernia.
- **Plain Abdominal X-ray**: Demonstrates dilated small bowel loops with multiple fluid levels in strangulated cases.

### Treatment / Management of Femoral Hernia [⭐ High-Yield]

```
                     MANAGEMENT OF FEMORAL HERNIA
                                    |
     +------------------------------+------------------------------+
     |                                                             |
Elective Surgical Repair                               Emergency Surgical Repair
(Mandatory for all elective cases)                    (For Incarcerated / Strangulated Cases)
* Low Approach (Lockwood)                             * High Preperitoneal Approach (McEvedy)
* Inguinal Approach (Lotheissen)                        - Allows formal bowel resection & anastomosis
* Laparoscopic Repair (TEP / TAPP)                    * Emergency Laparoscopic TAPP Repair
```

#### 1. General Surgical Principle

**All femoral hernias must be repaired surgically with prompt urgency upon diagnosis**. Watchful waiting or surgical trusses are **strictly contraindicated** due to the unacceptably high risk of bowel strangulation.

#### 2. Open Surgical Approaches [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Low Approach (Lockwood's Operation)** [⭐ High-Yield]:
    
    - _Incision_: Transverse incision directly over the swelling below the inguinal ligament.
    - _Technique_: The sac is dissected, opened, contents reduced, and the sac ligated at its neck. The femoral defect is closed by placing interrupted non-absorbable sutures between the **inguinal ligament above** and the **pectineal ligament (Astley Cooper's) below**, or by inserting a mesh plug.
    - _Indication_: Ideal for small, elective, reducible femoral hernias where there is **zero risk of bowel resection**.
2. **Inguinal Approach (Lotheissen's Operation)** [⭐ High-Yield]:
    
    - _Incision_: Standard oblique inguinal incision opening the external oblique aponeurosis.
    - _Technique_: The spermatic cord/round ligament is retracted, and the transversalis fascia opened to access the preperitoneal space. The sac is reduced by traction from above and pressure from below. The defect is repaired by suturing the conjoint tendon and transversalis fascia to the pectineal ligament.
    - _Indication_: Provides excellent access; useful when coexisting direct or indirect inguinal hernias are suspected.
3. **High Preperitoneal Approach (McEvedy's Operation / Nyhus Modification)** [⭐ High-Yield]:
    
    - _Incision_: Vertical paramedian or transverse incision (Nyhus modification) placed above the inguinal canal over the lateral border of the rectus sheath.
    - _Technique_: Enters the preperitoneal space deep to the rectus abdominis muscle. The femoral sac is reduced directly into the preperitoneal space.
    - _Indication_: **Gold-standard operation for emergency strangulated femoral hernias**. Allows generous peritoneal incision for inspection of ischemic bowel and formal bowel resection with end-to-end anastomosis.
    - _Crucial Surgical Advantage_: Formal bowel resection and anastomosis **CANNOT be performed via Lockwood's low approach** because the completed bowel anastomosis cannot be pushed back through the narrow femoral canal into the abdomen.

#### 3. Laparoscopic Repair (TEP & TAPP) [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Technique**: Totally Extraperitoneal (TEP) or Transabdominal Preperitoneal (TAPP) repair. A 10 × 15 cm synthetic mesh is placed in the preperitoneal space to cover the entire Myopectineal Orifice of Fruchaud (covering Hesselbach's triangle, the deep ring, and the femoral ring).
- **Indication**: Recommended as the first-line repair for **elective female groin hernias** to eliminate diagnostic confusion between indirect inguinal and femoral hernias and reduce early recurrence rates.

#### 4. Emergency Management of Strangulated Femoral Hernia [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Surgical Access**: High Preperitoneal McEvedy approach or TAPP laparoscopic repair.
- **Releasing the Constricting Neck**: If the hernia sac is tightly trapped, gently incise the medial boundary—the **lacunar ligament (Gimbernat's)**.
    - ⚠️ _Surgical Warning_: Exercise extreme caution for an **aberrant obturator artery ("corona mortis")**, which crosses the lacunar ligament in ~20% of individuals. Incising the ligament without vascular control risks catastrophic hemorrhage.
- **Assessment of Bowel Viability**: Wrap suspicious bowel in warm saline sponges for 5 minutes and administer 100% oxygen. If peristalsis and pink color return, reduce the bowel; if non-viable, perform formal bowel resection.
- **Wound Closure & Mesh Rule**: **Avoid synthetic mesh in established strangulation or gross intestinal contamination** due to high risk of mesh infection; perform primary tissue suture repair and plan secondary reconstruction if needed.

---

### Complications [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Hernia-Related Complications**: Acute mechanical small bowel obstruction, Richter's strangulation, gangrene of intestine/omentum, bowel perforation, and generalized peritonitis.
- **Surgical Complications**:
    - **Severe Hemorrhage**: Laceration of an aberrant obturator artery ("corona mortis") while dividing the lacunar ligament.
    - **Femoral Vein Injury / Thrombosis**: Accidental suture transfixion or narrowing of the femoral vein during low or high repair.
    - **Chronic Groin Pain**: Nerve entrapment of ilioinguinal, genitofemoral, or lateral femoral cutaneous nerves.
    - **Recurrence**: Higher recurrence rate following low suture repair compared to preperitoneal mesh placement.

---

### Prognosis

- Excellent outcomes following prompt elective repair (<1–2% recurrence with preperitoneal mesh). Elevated mortality (up to 10–20%) in elderly patients presenting late with strangulated gangrenous bowel requiring emergency resection.

---

### Clinical Pearl

In an elderly female presenting with **unexplained small bowel obstruction without any abdominal scar**, **always perform a meticulous palpation of the groin below the inguinal ligament for a small, hidden femoral hernia**.

---

### Exam Trap

- **The Mix-up**: Attempting to perform a formal small bowel resection and anastomosis through a **Low (Lockwood) approach** in a strangulated femoral hernia.
- **The Distinguishing Fact**: The narrow, rigid fibrous boundaries of the femoral canal make it physically impossible to return a completed bowel anastomosis back into the abdominal cavity from below. **Always use a High Preperitoneal (McEvedy) approach when bowel strangulation or resection is suspected**.

---

### Dosage Rule (Surgical Antibiotic Prophylaxis)

#### 1. Cefuroxime

- **Name**: Cefuroxime Sodium
- **Mechanism of Action**: Second-generation cephalosporin; binds penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) to inhibit bacterial cell wall peptidoglycan synthesis.
- **Dose**: 1.5 g IV.
- **Dosage / Route**: Intravenous injection.
- **Frequency**: Single dose at anesthesia induction (within 30 minutes prior to skin incision).
- **Features**: Indicated for emergency hernia surgery, strangulated bowel, or when mesh is inserted.
- **Side Effects**: Hypersensitivity reactions, rash, gastrointestinal upset.
- **When to Start / Stop**: Give single dose at induction; discontinue postoperatively unless active clinical infection/peritonitis is present.

- **L** – **L**ockwood low approach used for simple elective reducible hernias.

### Quick Revision

- **Groin Hernia Types**: Inguinal (Indirect, Direct, Pantaloon), Femoral, Obturator, Spigelian, Perineal.
- **Pubic Tubercle Rule**: Femoral hernia = **below & lateral**; Inguinal hernia = **above & medial**.
- **Anatomical Boundaries**: Anterior = Inguinal lig; Posterior = Pectineal lig; Medial = Lacunar lig; Lateral = Femoral vein.
- **Surgical Approaches**: Low (Lockwood) for simple elective; High (McEvedy) for emergency strangulated; Laparoscopic (TEP/TAPP) for elective females.
- **Surgical Hazard**: Watch for **corona mortis** (aberrant obturator artery) when dividing the lacunar ligament.

### One-Line Exam Opener

_Groin hernias encompass indirect, direct, and femoral types, with femoral hernias protruding through the femoral ring below the inguinal ligament, occurring predominantly in females, carrying a high risk of strangulation due to the rigid lacunar ligament, and requiring mandatory surgical repair via low (Lockwood), high (McEvedy), or laparoscopic (TEP/TAPP) approaches._
