# Congenital (Infantile) Hypertrophic Pyloric Stenosis (IHPS / CHPS)

### Definition [⭐ High-Yield]

**Infantile Hypertrophic Pyloric Stenosis (IHPS / CHPS)**, also known as idiopathic hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, is a congenital gastric outlet obstruction occurring in early infancy caused by idiopathic hypertrophy and hyperplasia of the circular muscle layer of the pylorus. It manifests as progressive, forceful, non-bilious projectile vomiting associated with weight loss, dehydration, and a characteristic metabolic electrolyte disturbance.

---

### Etiology / Causes / Risk Factors [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Incidence & Demographics**: Affects approximately 1 in 300 to 1 in 500 live births, with a strong male predominance (**Male : Female ratio = 4 : 1**). It classically affects the **first-born male child**.
- **Genetic & Family History**: Multifactorial inheritance pattern with a positive maternal family history.
- **Syndromic Associations**:
    - **Apert syndrome**.
    - **Cornelia de Lange syndrome**.
- **Biochemical & Pharmacological Factors**:
    - **Decreased neuronal Nitric Oxide Synthase (nNOS) levels** in pyloric tissue leading to impaired relaxation of the pyloric sphincter.
    - **Early postnatal exposure to macrolide antibiotics** (e.g., Erythromycin intake in the first 2 weeks of life).

#### Detailed Cascade of Electrolyte Abnormalities [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Initial Phase (Hypochloraemic Metabolic Alkalosis)**: Persistent vomiting leads to massive loss of gastric hydrochloric acid (HCl). The loss of hydrogen (H⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻) ions results in **hypochloraemic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis**.
2. **Compensatory Renal Phase**: Initially, the kidney attempts to compensate for systemic alkalosis by excreting sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃) in the urine.
3. **Aldosterone Activation & Hypokalemia**: Continued vomiting causes progressive dehydration and total body sodium (Na⁺) depletion. This activates the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS). High circulating aldosterone forces the renal distal tubules to actively reabsorb Na⁺ while excreting potassium (K⁺) into the urine, resulting in **hypokalemia**.
4. **Late Phase (Paradoxical Aciduria)**: As hypokalemia worsens, the renal tubules can no longer exchange K⁺ for Na⁺. To conserve Na⁺ and K⁺, the kidney excretes H⁺ ions into the urine in exchange for Na⁺. This produces **paradoxical aciduria** (excretion of acidic urine in the presence of severe systemic metabolic alkalosis).
5. **Net Electrolyte Profile**: **Hypochloraemic, hypokalemic, hyponatremic metabolic alkalosis with paradoxical aciduria**.

### Clinical Features [⭐ High-Yield]

#### Symptoms

- **Onset**: Infant is clinically normal at birth. Symptoms typically begin between **2 and 6 weeks of age**.
- **Projectile Non-Bilious Vomiting**: Hallmark feature. Vomiting starts as mild regurgitation, rapidly increasing in frequency and forcefulness. The vomitus contains curded milk and gastric juices but **never contains bile**.
- **Postprandial Hunger ("Hungry Vomiter")**: Unlike infective causes of vomiting (e.g., gastroenteritis, UTI, sepsis), the infant remains hungry immediately after vomiting and eagerly re-feeds.
- **Obstipation & Weight Loss**: Decreased frequency of bowel movements, progressive weight loss, and signs of dehydration.

#### Signs

- **Visible Gastric Peristalsis**: Waves of peristalsis passing across the upper abdomen from left to right, visible during or immediately after a feed.
- **Palpable Pyloric Olive ("Pyloric Tumor")** [⭐ High-Yield]: Pathognomonic physical sign. A firm, smooth, mobile, olive-shaped mass (~2 cm in size) felt in the right upper quadrant or epigastrium at the lateral border of the rectus abdominis. Best palpated during a **Test Feed** while the baby is relaxed and drinking sweet water or milk.

### Diagnosis / Investigations [⭐ High-Yield]

#### 1. Laboratory Findings

- **Serum Electrolytes & Arterial Blood Gas (ABG)**: Demonstrates low serum chloride (<95 mEq/L), low serum potassium (<3.5 mEq/L), elevated serum bicarbonate (>30 mEq/L), elevated arterial pH (>7.45), and elevated blood urea due to prerenal azotemia.
- **Urinalysis**: Demonstrates acidic urine pH (<6.0) despite systemic alkalosis (paradoxical aciduria).

#### 2. Imaging Studies

- **Targeted Abdominal Ultrasound (USG) [Investigation of Choice - IOC]**:
    - **Pyloric Muscle Thickness**: **>4 mm** (or >3.5 mm).
    - **Pyloric Channel Length**: **>16 mm**.
    - _Transverse View_: Shows the target or donut sign.
- **Barium Swallow / Contrast Study (Indicated only if USG is equivocal)**:
    - **String Sign**: Elongated, narrow pyloric canal.
    - **Double Tract Sign**: Parallel streaks of barium in the narrowed channel.
    - **Mushroom / Beak Sign**: Indentation of pylorus on the duodenal bulb.
- **Plain Abdominal Radiograph**: Shows a distended, gas-filled stomach with a **single bubble sign** and minimal or absent distal intestinal gas.

#### 1. Preoperative Medical Resuscitation (FIRST Priority) [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Rule**: Pyloric stenosis is a **medical emergency, NOT a surgical emergency**. Surgery must **never** be performed on an uncorrected, dehydrated infant with metabolic alkalosis, as general anesthesia in an alkalotic state can induce severe apnea.
- **Gastric Decompression**: Discontinue oral feeds immediately and empty the stomach using an 8–10 Fr nasogastric tube.
- **Intravenous Fluid & Electrolyte Resuscitation Protocol**:
    - Administer **0.9% Normal Saline + 0.15% KCl in 5% Glucose** at a rate of **6 to 7.5 mL/kg/hour** over 24 to 48 hours.
    - _Target Criteria for Surgery_: Serum chloride >100 mEq/L, serum potassium >3.5 mEq/L, and normal urinary output (>1 mL/kg/hour).

#### 2. Surgical Management (Ramstedt's Pyloromyotomy) [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Surgical Options**: Performed laparoscopically or open via a right upper quadrant / supraumbilical incision.
- **Operative Steps**:
    1. The hypertrophied pyloric "tumor" is delivered into the wound.
    2. A longitudinal incision is made through the avascular serosa along the anterior surface of the pylorus, extending from the gastric antrum to the duodenal fornix.
    3. A pyloric spreader (or blunt forceps) is used to split the thickened circular muscle fibers down to the underlying submucosa.
    4. The muscle is spread wide until the submucosa bulges out freely along the entire length of the pyloric channel.
    5. The integrity of the duodenal submucosa is tested by squeezing air from the stomach into the duodenum; if a tear occurs, it is closed with fine interrupted sutures and covered with an omental patch.
- **Postoperative Protocol**: Intravenous fluids are continued; oral feeds (breast milk or formula) are re-established within 24 hours.

---

### Complications

- **Preoperative**: Severe dehydration, hypovolemic shock, aspiration pneumonia, and weight loss.
- **Intraoperative**: Incomplete myotomy (causes persistent postoperative vomiting), or accidental duodenal mucosal perforation.
- **Postoperative**: Transient gastroesophageal reflux, surgical site infection, or wound dehiscence.

---

### Clinical Pearl

In an infant presenting with hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, **never take the child directly to the operating theater upon diagnosis**. CHPS is a metabolic emergency requiring 24 to 48 hours of resuscitation with **0.9% Normal Saline with 0.15% KCl in 5% Glucose** to correct chloride deficits and metabolic alkalosis before safe anesthesia and surgery can proceed.

---

### Exam Trap

- **The Mix-up**: Confusing the nature of vomiting in Congenital Hypertrophic Pyloric Stenosis with that of Duodenal Atresia.
- **The Distinguishing Fact**: CHPS presents at **2–6 weeks of age with non-bilious projectile vomiting** and a **single bubble sign** on X-ray, because the obstruction is proximal to the ampulla of Vater. Duodenal Atresia presents **at birth with bilious vomiting** and a **double bubble sign** on X-ray.

---

### Dosage Rule (Resuscitation & Electrolyte Fluid Protocol)

#### 0.9% Normal Saline + 0.15% KCl in 5% Glucose

- **Name**: Isotonic Normal Saline with Potassium Chloride in Dextrose.
- **Indication**: Preoperative fluid resuscitation and electrolyte correction in IHPS.
- **Dose & Rate**: **6 to 7.5 mL/kg/hour** (or an initial bolus of 10–20 mL/kg 0.9% Normal Saline if severely shocked).
- **Route**: Intravenous infusion.
- **Duration**: Administered over **24 to 48 hours** until serum chloride (>100 mEq/L) and acid-base parameters normalize.
- **Mechanism**: Normal saline supplies chloride ions (Cl⁻) needed by the kidneys to reabsorb H⁺ and eliminate bicarbonate, thereby reversing metabolic alkalosis.
