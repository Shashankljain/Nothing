# Hypokalemia

### Definition

**Hypokalemia** (hypokalaemia) is defined as a reduction in the serum potassium concentration below **3.5 mmol/L** (normal plasma concentration = 3.5–5.3 mmol/L). Mild hypokalemia is defined as a serum level of 3.0–3.5 mmol/L, whereas **severe hypokalemia** is defined as a level **<2.5 to 3.0 mmol/L**, which poses an immediate threat of lethal cardiac arrhythmias and neuromuscular paralysis.

---

### Etiology / Causes / Risk Factors [⭐ High-Yield]

Potassium is predominantly an intracellular cation (~150 mmol/L intracellularly vs. 3.5–5.3 mmol/L in plasma). Hypokalemia arises from gastrointestinal losses, increased renal excretion, transcellular shifts, or inadequate intake.

#### 1. Gastrointestinal Losses [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Gastric Content Loss**: Severe vomiting or prolonged nasogastric (Ryles tube) aspiration (e.g., in pyloric stenosis or bowel obstruction). Loss of HCl and gastric fluid triggers hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis.
- **Intestinal Losses**: High-output enterocutaneous fistulae, short bowel syndrome (>4 L/day loss), ileostomy flux, chronic severe diarrhoea, or villous adenoma of the rectum.

#### 2. Renal & Endocrine Causes

- **Primary Aldosteronism (Conn's Syndrome)**: Aldosterone-producing adenoma or bilateral adrenal hyperplasia causing uninhibited distal renal tubule Na⁺ reabsorption in exchange for accelerated K⁺ and H⁺ excretion into urine.
- **Cushing's Syndrome**: Excess cortisol exhibits mineralocorticoid action, driving renal potassium wasting.
- **Diuretic Therapy**: Non-potassium-sparing loop diuretics (furosemide) and thiazide diuretics.
- **Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA)**: Types 1 and 2 RTA cause renal K⁺ wasting.

#### 3. Transcellular Shift (Intracellular K⁺ Influx) [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Refeeding Syndrome**: Sudden administration of large quantities of carbohydrate/nutrition to a chronically malnourished or starved patient triggers a massive surge of endogenous insulin. Insulin activates Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, driving potassium, phosphate (PO₄³⁻), and magnesium (Mg²⁺) out of plasma into cells, precipitating life-threatening hypokalemia and hypophosphatemia.
- **Alkalosis**: Systemic metabolic or respiratory alkalosis shifts extracellular H⁺ ions out of cells in exchange for K⁺ entering cells.
- **Insulin Therapy & β₂-Agonists**: Exogenous insulin infusions or bronchodilators drive potassium into the intracellular space.

#### 4. Surgical & Iatrogenic Factors

- **Inadequate Maintenance**: Prolonged administration of potassium-free IV fluids in fasted surgical patients.
- **Timing Rule in Trauma/Surgery**: Potassium is **NOT replaced on Day 1 postoperatively** because tissue injury and cellular breakdown cause acute K⁺ efflux into the circulation. K⁺ replacement is routinely started from **Day 2**onward once urine output is established.

---

### Classification

1. **Mild Hypokalemia**: Serum K⁺ = 3.0 to 3.5 mmol/L (often asymptomatic or mild fatigue).
2. **Moderate Hypokalemia**: Serum K⁺ = 2.5 to 3.0 mmol/L (muscle cramps, constipation, ECG alterations).
3. **Severe Hypokalemia**: Serum K⁺ <2.5 mmol/L (flaccid paralysis, paralytic ileus, respiratory depression, lethal cardiac dysrhythmias).

- **Membrane Hyperpolarization**: The resting membrane potential of excitable cells is determined by the ratio of intracellular to extracellular K⁺. Decreased extracellular K⁺ increases this ratio, hyperpolarizing the cell membrane (making it more negative) and requiring a larger stimulus to initiate an action potential.
- **Cardiac Electrophysiology**: Delays ventricular repolarization, prolongs the relative refractory period, and promotes re-entrant circuits, predisposing to fatal ventricular arrhythmias.
- **Renal Paradoxical Aciduria**: In hypokalemia with metabolic alkalosis (e.g., pyloric stenosis), the renal proximal tubule reabsorbs Na⁺ in exchange for H⁺ instead of K⁺, leading to **paradoxical aciduria** despite systemic alkalosis.

### Clinical Features [⭐ High-Yield]

#### Symptoms

- **Neuromuscular**: Muscle fatigue, weakness starting in the lower extremities and ascending, muscle cramps, and paresthesias. In severe cases, respiratory muscle paralysis leads to dyspnoea.
- **Gastrointestinal**: Anorexia, nausea, constipation, abdominal distension, and severe **paralytic ileus** (paralytic ileus is frequently driven or prolonged by underlying hypokalemia).
- **Cardiovascular**: Palpitations, lightheadedness, and syncope due to dysrhythmias.
- **Renal**: Polyuria and polydipsia resulting from hypokalemia-induced nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.

#### Signs

- **Musculoskeletal**: Hypotonia, generalized hyporeflexia, and flaccid weakness.
- **Abdominal**: Abdominal distension, tympanic note, and quiet or absent bowel sounds (ileus).
- **Cardiovascular**: Irregular pulse (ectopics), hypotension, or cardiac arrest.

---

### Diagnosis / Investigations [⭐ High-Yield]

#### 1. Laboratory Findings

- **Serum Electrolytes**: Confirms serum K⁺ <3.5 mmol/L. Daily requirements for a 70-kg adult are ~1 mmol/kg/day (~50–90 mmol/day).
- **Serum Magnesium (Mg²⁺)**: Essential to measure; hypomagnesemia impairs the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump and enhances renal K⁺ wasting, making hypokalemia refractory to K⁺ replacement until Mg²⁺ is corrected.
- **Arterial Blood Gas (ABG)**: Shows metabolic alkalosis (high pH, high HCO₃⁻) in vomiting/pyloric stenosis or Conn's syndrome.
- **Urinary Electrolytes**: Spot urine K⁺ >20 mmol/L suggests renal wasting (diuretics, aldosteronism); urine K⁺ <20 mmol/L suggests GI loss or transcellular shift.

#### 2. Electrocardiogram (ECG) Changes [⭐ High-Yield]

Sequence of ECG abnormalities in progressive hypokalemia:

1. **Flattening or inversion of T waves**.
2. **ST-segment depression**.
3. **Appearance of prominent U waves** (a positive wave immediately following the T wave, best seen in precordial leads V₂–V₄).
4. **Prolongation of PR interval** and QTc prolongation.
5. **Severe Arrhythmias**: Premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), Atrial Fibrillation (AF), Ventricular Tachycardia (VT), Ventricular Fibrillation (VF), and cardiac arrest.

#### 1. General Principles

- **Timing in Surgery**: Do NOT give potassium on Postoperative Day 1. Start replacement from Day 2 once normal urine output is documented.
- **Treat Underlying Cause**: Stop loop/thiazide diuretics, treat vomiting/diarrhoea, or correct alkalosis.

#### 2. Mild to Moderate Hypokalemia (K⁺ 3.0–3.5 mmol/L)

- **Oral Replacement**: Preferred route if the gut is functional. Oral Potassium Chloride (KCl) tablets or syrup (20–40 mmol 8–12 hourly).
- **Dietary Sources**: Bananas, citrus fruits, potato skins, nuts.

#### 3. Severe or Symptomatic Hypokalemia (K⁺ <3.0 mmol/L, ECG Changes, or Ileus) [⭐ High-Yield]

Intravenous Potassium Chloride (KCl) administration requires strict adherence to safety protocols to prevent fatal hyperkalemic cardiac arrest:

#### Mandatory IV Potassium Administration Rules [⭐ High-Yield / Exam Trap]:

1. **Rule of Urine Output**: NEVER administer IV potassium until an adequate urine output of **>0.5 mL/kg/hour** (or >30 mL/hour) is established. In oliguric/anuric patients, infused K⁺ cannot be excreted, leading to rapid fatal hyperkalemia.
2. **Maximum Concentration Limit**: Maximum concentration for peripheral IV line infusion is **40 mmol/L** of KCl in 0.9% Normal Saline or Glucose-Saline (e.g., 20–40 mmol KCl added per 1000 mL bag). Higher concentrations cause severe pain and peripheral thrombophlebitis.
3. **Maximum Infusion Rate Limit**: Standard IV infusion rate is **10–20 mmol/hour**. The maximum rate must **NEVER exceed 20 mmol/hour** (or 20 mEq/h) and requires continuous ECG monitoring in an ICU/HDU setting.
4. **Correction of Hypomagnesemia**: Always measure and correct serum magnesium simultaneously (give IV Magnesium Sulfate 1–2 g over 1 hour).

---

### Complications [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Cardiac Arrest**: Lethal dysrhythmias (VT, VF, asystole in diastole).
- **Paralytic Ileus**: Severe intestinal smooth muscle Atony causing pseudo-obstruction, distension, and aspiration risk.
- **Rhabdomyolysis**: Severe muscle breakdown releasing myoglobin into blood, leading to myoglobinuric acute kidney injury.
- **Potentiated Digoxin Toxicity**: Hypokalemia enhances myocardial binding of digoxin, inducing digitalis toxicity and lethal arrhythmias even at therapeutic digoxin levels.

---

### Dosage Rule (Potassium Chloride - KCl)

- **Name**: Potassium Chloride (KCl)
- **Mechanism of Action**: Primary intracellular cation; restores extracellular/intracellular ionic concentration gradient, normalizing resting membrane potential and cellular repolarization.
- **Dose**:
    - _Daily Maintenance_: 1 mmol/kg/day (~50–90 mmol/day in adults).
    - _Correction Deficit_: 20–40 mmol per dose added to IV crystalloids.
- **Dosage / Route**:
    - Oral: Tablets or liquid elixir (with meals to avoid gastric irritation).
    - IV Infusion: Diluted in 0.9% Normal Saline or Glucose-Saline (NEVER IV push / bolus).
- **Frequency**: Continuous IV infusion or divided oral doses 8–12 hourly.
- **Features**: Must be thoroughly mixed in fluid bags before infusion.
- **Side Effects**: Peripheral thrombophlebitis, local vein pain, hyperkalemia if overcorrected, cardiac arrhythmias, oral ulceration.
- **When to Start / Stop**: Start from Postoperative Day 2 (avoid on Day 1). Stop immediately if serum K⁺ exceeds >4.5–5.0 mmol/L or if oliguria/anuria develops.

---

### Clinical Pearl

In a surgical patient with refractory hypokalemia that fails to normalize despite aggressive intravenous potassium replacement, **check and replace serum magnesium immediately**. Magnesium depletion inhibits the renal Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump, causing continuous urinary potassium wasting regardless of the amount of KCl infused.

---

### Exam Trap

- **The Mix-up**: Adding potassium chloride (KCl) to intravenous maintenance fluids on Postoperative Day 1 or giving concentrated IV KCl as a rapid bolus.
- **The Distinguishing Fact**: Surgical trauma causes massive cell injury and intracellular K⁺ efflux into the bloodstream on Day 1. Giving K⁺ on Day 1 or giving an IV bolus triggers hyperkalemic cardiac arrest in diastole. **Always delay K⁺ replacement until Day 2, establish urine output >0.5 mL/kg/h, and restrict IV infusion rates to ≤20 mmol/hour.**

    4. _T wave_ — flattened, biphasic, or inverted.
    5. _U wave_ — prominent positive deflection following T wave (pathognomonic).
- **Connections**: Low Extracellular K⁺ → Delayed Ventricular Repolarization → ST Depression + T-wave Inversion + Prominent U Wave.
- **Annotation for Extra Marks**: _"Prominent U waves following flattened T waves are the hallmark ECG finding of severe hypokalemia."_

### Quick Revision

- **Definition**: Serum potassium <3.5 mmol/L (Severe if <2.5–3.0 mmol/L).
- **Key Causes**: Vomiting/NG suction (pyloric stenosis), high-output fistulae, Conn's/Cushing's, refeeding syndrome, and loop diuretics.
- **ECG Changes**: T-wave flattening, ST depression, and **prominent U waves**.
- **Postoperative Rule**: Do NOT give K⁺ on Day 1 (efflux from cell injury); start from Day 2 once urine output is verified.
- **IV KCl Safety Rules**: Urine output must be >0.5 mL/kg/h, max peripheral concentration = 40 mmol/L, max infusion rate = 20 mmol/h, and always correct Mg²⁺.
