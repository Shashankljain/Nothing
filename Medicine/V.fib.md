# Ventricular Fibrillation

## 1. Definition [⭐ High-Yield]

**Ventricular Fibrillation (VF)** is a chaotic, life-threatening cardiac arrhythmia characterized by grossly irregular, rapid, and fractionated electrical activation of the ventricular myocardium. This results in complete loss of coordinated ventricular contraction, causing immediate failure of cardiac pump function, total collapse of cardiac output, and sudden cardiac arrest. VF is fatal within 2 to 5 minutes unless prompt cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and electrical defibrillation are initiated, making it the single most common cause of sudden cardiac death (SCD).

### A. Coronary Artery Disease (CAD) — Accounts for ~85% of Cases [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Acute Myocardial Infarction (AMI) / Acute Ischemia:** Primary trigger of VF in the early phase (within the first hour) of myocardial infarction due to acute ischemic membrane instability and injury currents.
- **Old Myocardial Infarction:** Chronic myocardial scar tissue providing a substrate for re-entrant ventricular tachyarrhythmias that degenerate into VF.

### B. Structural Heart Disease — Accounts for ~10% of Cases

- **Aortic Stenosis:** Severe left ventricular pressure overload and subendocardial ischemia.
- **Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM):** Myofiber disarray, asymmetrical septal hypertrophy, and intramyocardial fibrosis.
- **Dilated Cardiomyopathy (DCM):** Progressive ventricular dilation and patchy myocardial fibrosis.
- **Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy (ARVC / ARVD):** Fibrofatty replacement of right ventricular myocardium (most commonly involving mutations in the _PKP-2_ desmosomal gene).
- **Congenital Heart Diseases & Myocarditis.**

### C. No Structural Heart Disease / Primary Electrical Disorders — Accounts for ~5% of Cases

- **Inherited Cardiac Channelopathies:**
    - **Long QT Syndrome (LQTS):** Mutations in cardiac K⁺ or Na⁺ channels leading to Torsades de Pointes degenerating into VF.
    - **Brugada Syndrome:** Loss-of-function mutation in the _SCN5A_ sodium channel gene.
    - **Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) Syndrome:** Antegrade conduction across a rapidly conducting accessory pathway during pre-excited atrial fibrillation.
- **Electrolyte & Metabolic Derangements:** Severe **hypokalemia** (<3.0 mEq/L), **hypomagnesemia** (<1.5 mEq/L), severe acidosis, and accidental profound **hypothermia** (core temperature <30°C; presence of Osborne J waves).
- **Proarrhythmic Drug Toxicity:** Digoxin toxicity, Class Ia / Ic / III antiarrhythmic agents, tricyclic antidepressants, quinolone antibiotics.
- **Environmental / Traumatic:** Electrocution, lightning strike, drowning, and _commotio cordis_ (blunt precordial impact occurring during the vulnerable phase of repolarization).

## 3. Classification

Ventricular Fibrillation is classified based on its electrocardiographic amplitude and clinical onset:

### A. Based on ECG Waveform Amplitude

1. **Coarse Ventricular Fibrillation:** Characterized by relatively high-amplitude (>3 mm), rapid, irregular fibrillatory waves. Indicates a recent onset of cardiac arrest with higher myocardial ATP reserves and a greater likelihood of successful electrical defibrillation.
2. **Fine Ventricular Fibrillation:** Characterized by low-amplitude (<3 mm), shallow, smooth fibrillatory waves. Indicates prolonged cardiac arrest, severe myocardial hypoxia, and depleted high-energy phosphate stores. Fine VF can easily mimic asystole on a single ECG lead.

### B. Based on Clinical Onset in Acute MI

- **Primary VF:** Occurs spontaneously within the first 24–48 hours of an acute MI in the absence of heart failure or cardiogenic shock. Carries a good long-term prognosis if resuscitated promptly.
- **Secondary VF:** Occurs late after acute MI as a complication of severe left ventricular failure, cardiogenic shock, or ventricular aneurysm. Carries a poor long-term prognosis.

## 4. Pathophysiology (with Flowchart) [⭐ High-Yield]

### Pathogenetic Sequence

1. **Initiating Trigger:** An ectopic ventricular premature beat (VPC / R-on-T phenomenon) or rapid Ventricular Tachycardia (VT) strikes the myocardium during its vulnerable period of repolarization (the peak or downfall of the T wave).
2. **Substrate & Electrophysiological Inhomogeneity:** Regional ischemia, altered membrane potential, or fibrotic scarring creates non-uniform conduction velocity and localized variation in refractoriness across adjacent ventricular muscle fibers.
3. **Formation of Multiple Micro Re-entry Circuits:** The single wave of depolarization fragments into multiple chaotic, self-sustaining micro re-entrant wavelets (fractionation of impulses) that continuously loop around functional obstacles in the ventricles.
4. **Mechanical Failure & Cardiac Arrest:** The chaotic electrical firing (at rates >300–500/min) prevents synchronous ventricular myocardial contraction. The ventricles merely quiver continuously without generating stroke volume or cardiac output.
5. **Irreversible Ischemia:** Sudden circulatory arrest causes cessation of cerebral and coronary blood flow, leading to rapid loss of consciousness within 10–15 seconds and biological brain death within 4–6 minutes if uncorrected.

## 5. Clinical Features

### A. Symptoms

Due to immediate collapse of cerebral arterial perfusion, there are **no sustained prodromal symptoms** during the event itself. Immediately preceding symptoms (if conscious beforehand) include:

- Severe, sudden chest tightness or crushing retrosternal pain (if caused by acute MI).
- Sudden severe dizziness, lightheadedness, or racing palpitations (during pre-monitory VT).

### B. Signs (Triad of Cardiac Arrest)

1. **Sudden Loss of Consciousness / Unresponsiveness:** Patient collapses suddenly without warning.
2. **Absence of Central Arterial Pulses:** Carotid and femoral arterial pulses are completely non-palpable.
3. **Apnea or Abnormal Agonal Gasping:** Breathing ceases completely or manifests as infrequent, noisy agonal gasps.
4. **Physical Appearance:** Death-like pallor, central cyanosis, wide unreactive pupils (within 45–60 seconds of arrest), and complete muscular flaccidity.
5. **Heart Sounds & Blood Pressure:** Heart sounds are inaudible; blood pressure is completely unmeasurable.

### A. Emergency Rhythm Diagnosis (Cardiac Monitor / Defibrillator Pads) [⭐ High-Yield]

- **ECG Waveform Morphology:** Grossly chaotic, disorganized, bizarre baseline oscillations lacking recognizable P waves, QRS complexes, or T waves.
- **Rate & Rhythm:** Extremely rapid (rate undeterminable, typically >300–500 wavelets/min) and totally irregular in amplitude, contour, and frequency. (See **Diagram 1**).
- **Rule-out Fine VF Artifacts:** If a flatline/asystole pattern appears, switch monitor leads, increase ECG gain, and confirm that fine VF is not mimicking asystole.

### B. Post-Return of Spontaneous Circulation (ROSC) Investigations

Once sinus rhythm and spontaneous circulation are restored, perform:

1. **12-Lead Electrocardiogram:** Evaluates for acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), Brugada pattern (coved ST elevation in V₁–V₂), QT prolongation, or WPW pre-excitation (delta waves).
2. **Cardiac Biomarkers:** Serial Serum Cardiac Troponin I / T and Creatine Kinase-MB (CK-MB) to confirm underlying myocardial necrosis.
3. **Serum Biochemistry & ABG:** Serum potassium (K⁺), magnesium (Mg²⁺), calcium (Ca²⁺), and arterial blood gas (ABG) to detect metabolic acidosis and hypoxemia.
4. **Transthoracic Echocardiography:** Assesses regional wall motion abnormalities, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), valvular lesions, or congenital structural anomalies.
5. **Coronary Angiography:** Urgent cardiac catheterization to identify and revascularize occluded coronary arteries.

### A. Immediate Resuscitation Sequence (BLS & ALS Protocol) [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Activate Emergency Response & Start High-Quality CPR:**
    - Compress lower half of sternum at **100–120 compressions/min** to a depth of **5–6 cm**.
    - Compression-to-ventilation ratio = **30:2**. Minimize all interruptions in compressions to <10 seconds.
2. **Immediate Unsynchronized Defibrillation:**
    - Apply defibrillator pads to the upper right sternal border (infraclavicular) and cardiac apex (left mid-axillary line).
    - Deliver **1 unsynchronized biphasic shock at 150 to 200 Joules** (or 360 J monophasic).
3. **Immediate Resume CPR:**
    - **Resume CPR (30:2) for 2 full minutes IMMEDIATELY after shock delivery** without pausing to evaluate rhythm or palpate pulse.
4. **Re-assess Rhythm & Repeat Shock Cycle:**
    - After 2 minutes of CPR, re-evaluate rhythm. If VF persists, deliver a **2nd biphasic shock (150–200 J)** and immediately resume CPR for 2 minutes.

### B. Pharmacotherapy Guidelines & Dosage Rules [⭐ High-Yield]

#### 1. Adrenaline (Epinephrine)

- **Name:** Adrenaline (Epinephrine).
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 196, 199; Davidson p. 457). Potent direct-acting sympathomimetic agonist:
    
    - _α₁-Adrenoceptor Stimulation:_ Produces intense systemic arteriolar vasoconstriction, elevating systemic vascular resistance and raising aortic diastolic pressure during chest compressions, significantly improving **coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure**.
    - _β₁-Adrenoceptor Stimulation:_ Increases cardiac automaticity and SA node discharge.
    
    ```
    Adrenaline → α₁ Activation → Systemic Vasoconstriction → ↑ Aortic Diastolic Pressure → ↑ Coronary Perfusion
    ```
    
- **Dose:** **1 mg** (10 mL of 1:10,000 solution or 1 mL of 1:1,000 solution diluted in 10 mL saline).
- **Dosage / Route:** Intravenous (IV) or Intraosseous (IO) push, followed by a 20 mL saline flush.
- **Frequency:** Administered every **3 to 5 minutes** during ACLS (every alternate 2-minute CPR cycle).
- **Features:** Primary vasopressor in cardiac arrest algorithms.
- **Side Effects:** Severe post-ROSC tachycardia, malignant ventricular arrhythmias, extreme hypertension, increased myocardial oxygen demand.
- **When to Start / Stop:** In shockable VF/pulseless VT, administer **after the 3rd shock** (during CPR). Stop once ROSC is achieved.

#### 2. Amiodarone

- **Name:** Amiodarone.
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 573–578; Davidson p. 481). Broad-spectrum Class III antiarrhythmic agent:
    
    - Blocks myocardial delayed-rectifier potassium (K⁺) channels, prolonging action potential duration (APD) and effective refractory period (ERP).
    - Exhibits additional Class I (Na⁺ channel block), Class II (β-blockade), and Class IV (Ca²⁺ channel block) properties. Suppresses multiple micro re-entry wavelets in the ventricles.
    
    ```
    Amiodarone → Blocks Delayed Rectifier K⁺ Channels → Prolongs Action Potential & ERP → Suppresses Re-entry
    ```
    
- **Dose:** Initial bolus **300 mg** (diluted in 20 mL of 5% Dextrose). A secondary dose of **150 mg** can be administered for refractory VF.
- **Dosage / Route:** Intravenous (IV) or Intraosseous (IO) rapid bolus.
- **Frequency:** Single 300 mg bolus after 3rd shock; optional 150 mg after 5th shock.
- **Features:** First-line antiarrhythmic agent for shock-refractory VF and pulseless VT.
- **Side Effects:** Hypotension, bradycardia upon ROSC, phlebitis at injection site.
- **When to Start / Stop:** Administer **after the 3rd shock** in shock-refractory VF.

#### 3. Lidocaine (Alternative Antiarrhythmic)

- **Name:** Lidocaine (Lignocaine).
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 574, 575; Davidson p. 481). Class Ib antiarrhythmic agent that selectively blocks inactivated Na⁺ channels, shortening action potential duration and effective refractory period in ischemic ventricular myocardium while suppressing ectopic automaticity.
- **Dose:** Initial bolus **1.0 to 1.5 mg/kg IV/IO** (typically 50–100 mg). Second dose of 0.5–0.75 mg/kg can be given after 5–10 minutes (maximum total dose 3 mg/kg).
- **Dosage / Route:** Intravenous (IV) or Intraosseous (IO) bolus.
- **Features:** Second-line alternative antiarrhythmic when amiodarone is unavailable.

### C. Reversible Causes Evaluation (The 4 Hs & 4 Ts)

Throughout resuscitation, systematically identify and treat reversible factors:

- **4 Hs:** **H**ypoxia, **H**ypovolemia, **H**ypo-/Hyperkalemia (and metabolic acidosis), **H**ypothermia.
- **4 Ts:** **T**hrombosis (coronary acute MI or pulmonary PE), **T**ension pneumothorax, **T**amponade (cardiac), **T**oxins.

---

### D. Secondary Prevention & Post-Resuscitation Care

1. **Targeted Temperature Management (TTM):** Maintain core body temperature at 32°C to 36°C for at least 24 hours in comatose post-ROSC survivors to minimize anoxic brain damage.
2. **Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillator (ICD) Implantation:** Indicated for secondary prevention in all survivors of VF or pulseless VT cardiac arrest that occurred in the absence of a transient or reversible cause (Davidson p. 483, Box 16.35).
3. **Long-term Medical Therapy:** Beta-blockers (bisoprolol/metoprolol) and ACE inhibitors/ARBs to prevent ischemic remodeling and recurrent fatal arrhythmias.

## 9. Complications

1. **Anoxic Ischemic Encephalopathy:** Cerebral hypoxia causing permanent neurological deficit, vegetative state, or brain death.
2. **Post-Cardiac Arrest Myocardial Stunning:** Severe transient left ventricular dysfunction and cardiogenic shock following ROSC.
3. **CPR-Related Traumatic Injuries:** Rib fractures, sternal fractures, hemothorax, pneumothorax, liver lacerations, and gastric rupture.
4. **Aspiration Pneumonia:** Secondary to loss of protective airway reflexes during cardiac arrest.

---

## 10. Prognosis

- **Time-Critical Survival:** Without CPR and defibrillation, survival decreases by **7% to 10% for every minute of delay**.
- **Primary VF in Acute MI:** Early resuscitation within 1–2 minutes yields a high ROSC rate (>70–80%) with excellent long-term survival comparable to uncomplicated MI patients.
- **Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest (OHCA):** Overall survival to hospital discharge remains 8–12%, heavily dependent on bystander CPR and early public access defibrillation (AED).

## 13. Clinical Pearl

> **Clinical Pearl:** Never mistake **Fine Ventricular Fibrillation** for **Asystole**! Fine VF exhibits tiny, low-amplitude baseline ripples that can look like a flatline on a single ECG lead. If asystole is suspected, immediately increase the ECG gain (amplitude) and check a second orthogonal lead. If there is any doubt between fine VF and asystole, **treat as VF and deliver an immediate unsynchronized 150–200 J biphasic shock**—defibrillating fine VF can restore sinus rhythm, whereas delivering a shock for true asystole causes further myocardial injury.

---

## 14. Exam Trap

### Exam Trap

- **The Mix-Up:** Pausing chest compressions immediately after delivering a 150–200 J shock in VF to check for a carotid pulse or re-assess the ECG monitor.
- **Distinguishing Fact:** Mechanical cardiac output rarely returns instantly after electrical defibrillation. Pausing compressions causes a catastrophic collapse in coronary perfusion pressure, which requires up to 60 seconds of continuous chest compressions to rebuild.
- **Resolution:** **Immediately resume high-quality chest compressions (CPR 30:2) for 2 full minutes right after shock delivery** before evaluating rhythm or checking for a pulse.

---

## 15. Quick Revision

- **Definition:** Chaotic, fractionated activation of ventricles resulting in uncoordinated quivering, zero cardiac output, and cardiac arrest.
- **Commonest Etiology:** Coronary Artery Disease (85% of cases; especially acute myocardial infarction).
- **ECG Hallmark:** Chaotic baseline waves without P waves, QRS complexes, or T waves.
- **First-Line ACLS Treatment:** Immediate high-quality CPR (30:2) + unsynchronized 150–200 J biphasic shock.
- **Core ACLS Pharmacotherapy:** Adrenaline 1 mg IV every 3–5 mins (after 3rd shock) + Amiodarone 300 mg IV bolus (after 3rd shock).
- **Secondary Prevention:** Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillator (ICD) implantation in survivors without a reversible cause.

> **Ventricular fibrillation is a catastrophic, fatal cardiac arrhythmia characterized by chaotic, uncoordinated ventricular electrical depolarization that abolishes cardiac output and precipitates immediate cardiac arrest, requiring urgent CPR, electrical defibrillation, and intravenous antiarrhythmic therapy.**
