# Acute Viral Hepatitis

## 1. Definition [⭐ High-Yield]

**Acute Viral Hepatitis** is a systemic infection predominantly affecting the liver, characterized by diffuse acute inflammation, hepatocyte necrosis, ballooning degeneration, and elevated serum transaminases, caused by primary hepatotropic viruses—most notably **Hepatitis A Virus (HAV)**, **Hepatitis B Virus (HBV)**, **Hepatitis C Virus (HCV)**, **Hepatitis D Virus (HDV)**, and **Hepatitis E Virus (HEV)** [Robbins 3 p. 556, 560].

## 3. Classification / Clinical Stages

Acute viral hepatitis clinically progresses through **four distinct phases**:

1. **Incubation Period:** Asymptomatic viral replication; peak infectivity occurs toward the end of this phase [Robbins 3 p. 560].
2. **Pre-icteric (Prodromal) Phase:** Duration 3 to 10 days; characterized by non-specific constitutional symptoms (anorexia, nausea, fatigue, low-grade fever, serum-sickness-like rash/arthralgia in HBV) [Robbins 3 p. 560].
3. **Icteric Phase:** Duration 1 to 4 weeks; marked by overt dark tea-colored urine, scleral icterus, pale stools, tender hepatomegaly, and peak elevation of serum aminotransferases [Robbins 3 p. 560].
4. **Convalescent Phase:** Clinical symptoms and jaundice abate; liver function tests gradually normalize over 2 to 12 weeks [Robbins 3 p. 560].

## 4. Pathophysiology (with Flowchart) [⭐ High-Yield]

### Pathogenetic Sequence

1. **Viral Ingestion / Inoculation & Hepatocyte Tropism:**
    - HAV/HEV are ingested via contaminated water/food, cross the intestinal mucosa, and reach the liver via the portal circulation [Robbins 3 p. 556, 560].
    - HBV enters systemic circulation via blood or secretions and targets hepatocytes via binding of large HBsAg to the sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP) receptor [Robbins 3 p. 556].
2. **Cellular Immunity & Hepatocyte Injury:** Most hepatotropic viruses (HAV, HBV, HEV) are **not directly cytopathic**. Tissue injury is mediated primarily by **CD8⁺ Cytotoxic T-lymphocytes and Natural Killer (NK) cells**that recognize viral antigens presented on MHC Class I molecules on infected hepatocyte membranes [Robbins 3 p. 556, 560].
3. **Histopathological Changes:**
    - **Ballooning Degeneration & Apoptosis:** Swollen, pale hepatocytes undergo cytotoxic lysis or shrink into intensely eosinophilic apoptotic bodies (**Councilman Bodies**) [Robbins 3 p. 560].
    - **Lobular & Portal Inflammation:** Infiltration of portal tracts and lobules by CD8⁺ T-lymphocytes and pigment-laden macrophages scavenging cellular debris [Robbins 3 p. 560].
    - **Cholestasis & Ductular Reaction:** Bile canalicular plugs and bile stasis during the icteric phase [Robbins 3 p. 560].
4. **Outcome Variations:**
    - _Resolution:_ Intact immune response clears infected hepatocytes, followed by complete parenchymal regeneration [Robbins 3 p. 556].
    - _Fulminant Hepatic Failure:_ Massive confluent lobular necrosis causing acute liver failure (<1% of HAV/HBV; ~20% in pregnant women with HEV) [Robbins 3 p. 556, 560].
    - _Chronicity (HBV):_ Inadequate T-cell response leads to persistent viral replication, chronic hepatitis, and risk of cirrhosis [Robbins 3 p. 556].

## 5. Clinical Features [⭐ High-Yield]

### A. Symptoms [⭐ High-Yield]

#### 1. Pre-icteric (Prodromal) Phase

- **Constitutional Symptoms:** Marked fatigue, malaise, generalized myalgia, low-grade fever, and headache.
- **Gastrointestinal Symptoms:** Anorexia (profound loss of appetite), nausea, vomiting, altered taste perception (loss of taste for tobacco/smoking in smokers), and right upper quadrant abdominal discomfort.
- **Immune-Complex Phenomena (especially in Acute HBV):** Serum-sickness-like syndrome with urticarial skin rash, polyarthralgia, or frank arthritis.

#### 2. Icteric Phase

- **Dark Tea-Colored Urine:** Earliest objective sign, preceding scleral icterus by 1 to 5 days.
- **Jaundice:** Yellowish discoloration of sclera, mucous membranes, and skin.
- **Pale / Clay-Colored Stools:** Due to transient impairment of biliary excretion.
- **Pruritus:** Generalized itching secondary to bile salt accumulation in skin.
- _Note:_ Constitutional prodromal symptoms often improve once overt clinical jaundice appears.

---

### B. Physical Signs [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Scleral Icterus & Skin Jaundice:** Best evaluated under natural daylight on the bulbar conjunctiva.
2. **Tender Hepatomegaly:** Smooth, soft, mildly enlarged, and tender liver margin palpated in the right hypochondrium.
3. **Mild Splenomegaly & Cervical Lymphadenopathy:** Present in 10% to 20% of acute hepatitis patients.
4. **Spider Angiomas & Palmar Erythema:** Rare in acute self-limiting hepatitis; their presence suggests pre-existing chronic liver disease or alcohol abuse.
5. **Signs of Acute Fulminant Hepatic Failure (Red Flags):** Asterixis ("flapping tremor"), confusion, drowsiness, prolonged PT/INR, shrinking liver span, and rapid development of ascites/encephalopathy.

## 6. Diagnosis / Laboratory Investigations [⭐ High-Yield]

### A. Liver Function Tests (Biochemical Findings) [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Serum Aminotransferases (ALT & AST):** **Markedly elevated (>10 to 100 times normal limits; typically >1000 U/L)**. **ALT is characteristically higher than AST (ALT > AST)**, reflecting direct hepatocellular injury.
- **Serum Bilirubin:** Elevated (both conjugated and unconjugated components), typically ranging from 5 to 20 mg/dL.
- **Serum Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) & GGT:** Mildly to moderately elevated (<3 times upper limit of normal); higher elevations indicate a cholestatic variant.
- **Prothrombin Time (PT) / INR [⭐ Critical Marker]:** Normal or mildly prolonged in uncomplicated acute hepatitis. **Prolongation of PT (INR >1.5) is the single most sensitive indicator of severe hepatocellular necrosis and impending acute liver failure**.

1. **HBsAg (Hepatitis B Surface Antigen):** Appears 1 to 10 weeks post-exposure; indicates active HBV infection. Persistence >6 months defines chronic HBV infection [Robbins 3 p. 556].
2. **IgM anti-HBc (IgM Anti-Hepatitis B Core Antibody) [⭐ High-Yield]:** Appears shortly after HBsAg. **It is the key diagnostic marker for Acute Hepatitis B** and remains positive during the **"Window Period"** (the clinical window where HBsAg has cleared but Anti-HBs has not yet risen) [Robbins 3 p. 556].
3. **HBeAg & HBV DNA:** Reflect active viral replication, high infectivity, and degree of liver injury [Robbins 3 p. 556].
4. **Anti-HBs:** Indicates resolution of acute infection, clearance of viral particles, and development of lifelong protective immunity [Robbins 3 p. 556].

## 8. Treatment / Management [⭐ High-Yield]

### A. General Supportive & Symptomatic Management (All Acute Viral Hepatitis)

1. **Rest & Activity:** Bed rest guided by patient tolerance; resume normal light activity as symptoms and jaundice clear.
2. **Dietary Management:** High-calorie, well-balanced diet. Small, frequent meals rich in carbohydrates are best tolerated in the morning (nausea worsens later in the day). Avoid fat restriction unless severe nausea exists.
3. **Avoidance of Hepatotoxins:** Complete avoidance of alcohol, paracetamol (or strictly <2 g/day), sedatives, and hepatotoxic medications.
4. **Monitoring:** Serial LFTs and Prothrombin Time (PT/INR) monitoring every 1 to 2 weeks to screen for impending liver failure.

---

### B. Pharmacotherapy & Dosage Protocols (Dosage Rules) [⭐ High-Yield]

#### 1. Management of Acute Uncomplicated Hepatitis A, B, and E

- **Routine Antiviral Therapy is NOT Indicated** for acute uncomplicated HAV, HBV, or HEV infection, as >95% of adult cases resolve spontaneously [Davidson 1 p. 875, Kdt 3 p. 854].
- **Indications for Antiviral Therapy in Acute HBV:**
    1. Severe acute hepatitis B (marked prolonged jaundice, INR >1.5, or deep encephalopathy).
    2. Immunocompromised hosts.

#### 2. Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate (TDF) — Anti-HBV Nucleotide Analogue

- **Name:** Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate.
- **Mechanism of Action:** (Kdt 3 Textbook p. 854, 855). Acyclic nucleotide monophosphate analogue of AMP. Intracellularly phosphorylated by cellular kinases to active **Tenofovir Diphosphate**. Competitively inhibits **HBV DNA Polymerase** and reverse transcriptase, and gets incorporated into nascent viral DNA causing **chain termination**.
    
    ```
    Tenofovir Disoproxil → Cellular Kinases → Tenofovir Diphosphate → Inhibits HBV DNA Polymerase → Viral DNA Chain Termination
    ```
    
- **Receptors / Target:** HBV DNA Polymerase / Reverse Transcriptase.
- **Dose:** **300 mg PO once daily (OD)**.
- **Dosage / Route:** Oral tablet (swallowed whole with or without food).
- **Frequency:** Once daily.
- **Features:** First-line potent antiviral agent for Hepatitis B; extremely low rate of viral resistance; active against lamivudine-resistant strains.
- **Side Effects:** Nausea, flatulence, abdominal pain, mild rise in serum creatinine, rare renal tubular dysfunction (Fanconi syndrome), bone mineral density loss.
- **When to Start / Stop:** Start in severe acute HBV (INR >1.5, fulminant course) or chronic active HBV; continue until HBsAg clearance or sustained seroconversion (at least 6–12 months post-HBeAg seroconversion).

#### 3. Entecavir — Anti-HBV Guanosine Analogue

- **Name:** Entecavir.
- **Mechanism of Action:** (Kdt 3 Textbook p. 854, 855). Guanosine nucleoside analogue. Phosphorylated to active **Entecavir Triphosphate**, which competitively inhibits all 3 functions of **HBV DNA Polymerase**: (1) base priming, (2) reverse transcription of negative strand, and (3) synthesis of positive strand HBV DNA.
- **Dose:** **0.5 mg PO once daily (OD)** for treatment-naive patients; **1.0 mg PO OD** for lamivudine-resistant cases.
- **Dosage / Route:** Oral (must be taken on an **empty stomach**, 2 hours before or after meals).
- **Frequency:** Once daily.
- **Features:** First-line highly potent agent with high barrier to resistance in treatment-naive patients.
- **Side Effects:** Headache, fatigue, dizziness, nausea, rare lactic acidosis in decompensated cirrhosis.
- **When to Start / Stop:** Same indications as Tenofovir; adjust dose in renal impairment.

#### 4. Symptomatic Management of Pruritus (Cholestatic Phase)

- **Cholestyramine:** **4 g PO 12-hourly** before meals to bind bile acids in the intestinal lumen [Kdt 1 p. 685].

---

### C. Prevention & Immunoprophylaxis [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Hepatitis A Prophylaxis:**
    - _Active Immunization:_ Inactivated HAV Vaccine (2 doses at 0 and 6–12 months IM); recommended for travelers, MSM, and chronic liver disease patients.
    - _Post-Exposure Prophylaxis:_ Single dose of HAV vaccine or Standard Immune Globulin (0.02 mL/kg IM) given within 2 weeks of exposure.
2. **Hepatitis B Prophylaxis [⭐ High-Yield]:**
    - _Active Immunization:_ Recombinant HBsAg Vaccine (3-dose schedule at **0, 1, and 6 months IM** in deltoid muscle). Target anti-HBs titer **>10 mIU/mL**.
    - _Post-Exposure / Perinatal Prophylaxis:_ Hepatitis B Immune Globulin (**HBIG 0.06 mL/kg IM**) plus first dose of HBV vaccine administered at separate sites within 12 hours of birth (for infants of HBsAg-positive mothers) or within 24 hours of needle-stick injury.
3. **Hepatitis E Prophylaxis:** Recombinant HEV vaccine (Hecolin) approved in certain endemic regions; emphasis on clean municipal drinking water supply.

## 9. Complications [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Acute Fulminant Hepatic Failure:** Massive liver necrosis causing acute liver failure, encephalopathy, coagulopathy, cerebral edema, and multiorgan failure (<1% of HAV/HBV; ~20% in pregnant women with HEV) [Robbins 3 p. 556, 560].
2. **Chronicity, Cirrhosis & Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC):** Occurs in 5–10% of adult-acquired HBV and >80% of HCV infections [Robbins 3 p. 556].
3. **Prolonged Cholestatic Hepatitis:** Seen in HAV and HEV; persistent deep jaundice and pruritus lasting >2 to 6 months, but eventually resolves completely.
4. **Relapsing Hepatitis:** Recurrence of symptoms and ALT surge within 6 months of recovery (occurs in 3–20% of HAV cases).
5. **Extrahepatic Immune-Complex Complications:**
    - _HBV:_ Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN), Membranous Glomerulonephritis, and Serum-Sickness Syndrome [Robbins 3 p. 556].
    - _HCV:_ Mixed Essential Cryoglobulinemia and Porphyria Cutanea Tarda.

---

## 10. Prognosis

- **Hepatitis A & E:** Excellent prognosis in immunocompetent non-pregnant individuals (>99% complete recovery with lifelong immunity) [Robbins 3 p. 556]. High mortality in pregnant females infected with HEV (~20%).
- **Hepatitis B:** Adult-acquired HBV resolves spontaneously in 90–95% of cases; neonatally acquired HBV progresses to chronic infection in ~90% of cases [Robbins 3 p. 556].

## 13. Clinical Pearl

> **Clinical Pearl:** In evaluating a patient suspected of acute hepatitis B during the symptomatic or early recovery phase, **HBsAg may occasionally fall below detectable levels before Anti-HBs has developed**! This diagnostic gap is termed the **"Window Period"**. During this window, ordering only HBsAg and Anti-HBs will yield falsely negative results. **IgM anti-HBc is the ONLY positive serological marker during the Window Period**, making it mandatory for accurately diagnosing acute HBV infection!

---

## 14. Exam Trap

### Exam Trap

- **The Mix-Up:** Prescribing **Tenofovir or Entecavir** routinely for every patient presenting with **Acute Uncomplicated Hepatitis B**.
- **Distinguishing Fact:** Over **90% to 95% of adult patients with acute HBV clear the virus spontaneously**without antiviral intervention. Routine antiviral therapy does not speed up recovery or reduce chronicity in uncomplicated cases.
- **Resolution:** **Antiviral drugs (Tenofovir/Entecavir) are NOT indicated for routine acute HBV**! They are reserved strictly for **Severe Acute Hepatitis B** (marked by INR >1.5, protracted deep jaundice, or encephalopathy) or immunocompromised patients.

---

### Quick Revision

- **Etiology & Transmission:** HAV and HEV are transmitted via the **fecal-oral route** (water/food); HBV, HCV, and HDV are transmitted **parenterally, sexually, and perinatally** [Robbins 3 p. 556].
- **High-Yield Serology:**
    - **HAV:** IgM anti-HAV = Acute infection [Robbins 3 p. 556].
    - **HBV:** HBsAg = Active infection; **IgM anti-HBc = Acute HBV / Window Period marker**; Anti-HBs = Recovery/Immunity [Robbins 3 p. 556].
    - **HEV:** IgM anti-HEV = Acute infection [Robbins 3 p. 556].
- **Pregnancy Risk:** HEV infection causes **~20% mortality** due to acute fulminant liver failure in pregnant women [Robbins 3 p. 560].
- **LFT Pattern:** Marked ALT and AST elevation (>1000 U/L) with **ALT > AST**. Prolonged **PT/INR >1.5** is the best marker of impending liver failure.
- **Management:** Supportive care for acute HAV, HBV, and HEV. Antivirals (Tenofovir 300 mg OD / Entecavir 0.5 mg OD) reserved strictly for severe acute HBV (INR >1.5) or chronic hepatitis [Kdt 3 p. 854, 855].

### One-Line Exam Opener

> **Acute viral hepatitis is a systemic infection caused primarily by hepatotropic viruses (HAV, HBV, HCV, HDV, HEV), characterized by immune-mediated hepatocyte ballooning degeneration and apoptosis (Councilman bodies), marked ALT elevation >1000 U/L, and diagnosed via virus-specific serological markers such as IgM anti-HAV, IgM anti-HBc, and IgM anti-HEV.**
