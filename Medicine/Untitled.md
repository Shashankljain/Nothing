# Acute Viral Hepatitis

## 1. Definition [⭐ High-Yield]

**Acute Viral Hepatitis** is a systemic infection predominantly affecting the liver, characterized by acute diffuse hepatocellular injury, ballooning degeneration, Councilman body apoptosis, and lobular and portal inflammation, leading to elevated serum aminotransferases (ALT/AST >1000 U/L) [Robbins 3 p. 240, 273].

It is caused primarily by specific hepatotropic viruses—most notably **Hepatitis A Virus (HAV)**, **Hepatitis B Virus (HBV)**, **Hepatitis C Virus (HCV)**, **Hepatitis D Virus (HDV)**, and **Hepatitis E Virus (HEV)** [Robbins 3 p. 240, 268].

#### 1. Hepatitis A Virus (HAV) [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Virology:** Small, non-enveloped, single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) virus belonging to the _Picornaviridae_ family (_Hepatovirus_ genus) [Robbins 3 p. 238, 268].
- **Mode of Transmission:** **Fecal-oral route** via ingestion of contaminated water, food, or raw/steamed shellfish (oysters, mussels, clams) [Robbins 3 p. 237, 241].
- **Incubation Period:** 2 to 6 weeks (average 3 to 4 weeks) [Davidson 1 p. 873, Robbins 3 p. 239, 268].
- **Risk Factors & Chronicity:** Overcrowding, poor sanitation, institutional settings (schools/nurseries) [Robbins 3 p. 241]. **Never causes chronic hepatitis or a carrier state** [Robbins 3 p. 241, 268].

#### 2. Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Virology:** Partially double-stranded circular DNA (dsDNA) virus belonging to the _Hepadnaviridae_ family [Robbins 3 p. 244, 268]. Composed of an outer surface envelope (HBsAg), nucleocapsid core (HBcAg and HBeAg), and viral DNA polymerase with reverse transcriptase activity [Robbins 3 p. 244, 245].
- **Mode of Transmission:** **Parenteral** (blood transfusion, contaminated needles in IV drug users), **sexual contact**, and **perinatal (vertical) transmission** during childbirth [Robbins 3 p. 248, 268].
- **Incubation Period:** 4 to 26 weeks (average 8 to 12 weeks) [Davidson 1 p. 873, Robbins 3 p. 252, 268].
- **Chronicity Risk:** 5% to 10% in adult-acquired cases; **~90% in neonates** exposed perinatally [Robbins 3 p. 249, 250].

#### 3. Hepatitis C Virus (HCV)

- **Virology:** Enveloped ssRNA virus belonging to the _Flaviviridae_ family [Robbins 3 p. 257, 268].
- **Mode of Transmission:** Parenteral route (IV drug use, blood products, needle-stick injury) [Robbins 3 p. 256, 268].
- **Incubation Period:** 2 to 26 weeks (mean 6 to 12 weeks) [Robbins 3 p. 258, 268].
- **Chronicity Risk:** **>80% to 90%** of infected individuals progress to chronic hepatitis [Robbins 3 p. 255, 268].

#### 4. Hepatitis D Virus (HDV)

- **Virology:** Defective circular ssRNA delta agent; requires HBsAg coat from HBV to complete its life cycle [Robbins 3 p. 263, 268].
- **Mode of Transmission:** Parenteral route (co-infection with HBV or superinfection in a chronic HBV carrier) [Robbins 3 p. 260, 261].

#### 5. Hepatitis E Virus (HEV) [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Virology:** Non-enveloped ssRNA virus belonging to the _Hepeviridae_ family (_Hepevirus_ genus) [Robbins 3 p. 264, 268].
- **Mode of Transmission:** **Fecal-oral, enterically transmitted waterborne infection**; also zoonotic via domestic animals (pigs, wild boar) [Robbins 3 p. 267, 268]. Accounts for >30% of sporadic acute hepatitis in India [Robbins 3 p. 267].
- **Incubation Period:** 2 to 6 weeks (average 4 to 5 weeks) [Robbins 3 p. 264, 268].
- **Chronicity & Mortality:** Self-limited in immunocompetent hosts; **associated with a high mortality rate (~20%) among pregnant women** due to acute fulminant liver failure [Robbins 3 p. 265, 285].

### B. Non-Hepatotropic Viruses

- Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV), Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV), Adenovirus, and Yellow Fever Virus [Davidson 1 p. 873, Robbins 3 p. 240].

## 3. Classification & Clinical Stages

Acute viral hepatitis progresses through **four distinct clinical phases** [Robbins 3 p. 271]:

```
┌─────────────────┐    ┌─────────────────┐    ┌─────────────────┐    ┌─────────────────┐
│ 1. INCUBATION   │ →  │ 2. PRODROMAL    │ →  │  3. ICTERIC     │ →  │ 4. CONVALESCENT │
│    PERIOD       │    │    PHASE        │    │     PHASE       │    │    PHASE        │
│ (Asymptomatic   │    │ (Pre-icteric:   │    │ (Jaundice, dark │    │ (Clinical       │
│  replication)   │    │  Anorexia, fever│    │  urine, tender  │    │  recovery, LFTs │
│                 │    │  & malaise)     │    │  hepatomegaly)  │    │  normalize)     │
└─────────────────┘    └─────────────────┘    └─────────────────┘    └─────────────────┘
```

1. **Incubation Period:** Virus multiplies silently within hepatocytes; peak infectivity occurs during the late asymptomatic days of this phase [Robbins 3 p. 271].
2. **Symptomatic Prodromal (Pre-icteric) Phase:** Lasts 3 to 10 days; characterized by non-specific constitutional and gastrointestinal symptoms [Robbins 3 p. 271].
3. **Symptomatic Icteric Phase:** Lasts 1 to 4 weeks; marked by overt scleral icterus, dark urine, pale stools, tender hepatomegaly, and peak serum transaminase elevation [Robbins 3 p. 271].
4. **Convalescent Phase:** Clinical jaundice and constitutional symptoms subside; transaminases gradually return to normal over weeks to months [Robbins 3 p. 271].

## 4. Pathophysiology (with Flowchart) [⭐ High-Yield]

### Pathogenetic Sequence

1. **Viral Entry & Hepatocyte Tropism:**
    - **HAV / HEV:** Ingested via contaminated food/water, cross intestinal mucosa, and reach hepatocytes via portal circulation [Robbins 3 p. 241, 264].
    - **HBV:** Enters systemic circulation via parenteral/sexual route and binds to the **sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP)** receptor on hepatocyte membranes [Robbins 3 p. 246].
2. **Immune-Mediated Hepatocyte Damage [⭐ High-Yield]:**
    - Most hepatotropic viruses (HAV, HBV, HEV) are **not directly cytopathic** [Robbins 3 p. 238, 246, 264].
    - Hepatocyte injury is driven by the host cell-mediated immune response: **CD8⁺ Cytotoxic T-Lymphocytes (CTLs) and Natural Killer (NK) cells** recognize viral peptide antigens presented on MHC Class I molecules on infected hepatocyte surfaces, triggering apoptosis and lysis [Robbins 3 p. 238, 246].
3. **Histopathology & Morphological Hallmarks [⭐ High-Yield]:**
    - **Ballooning Degeneration:** Hydropic swelling of hepatocytes with pale, cleared cytoplasm [Robbins 3 p. 273, 278].
    - **Councilman Bodies (Acidophil Bodies):** Shrunken, intensely eosinophilic, pyknotic apoptotic hepatocytes [Robbins 3 p. 273, 278].
    - **Lobular & Portal Infiltrate:** Infiltration of lobules and portal tracts by lymphocytes, plasma cells, and pigment-laden macrophages scavenging necrotic debris [Robbins 3 p. 273, 278].
    - **Cholestasis:** Bile canalicular plugs and cholestatic pigments within hepatocytes [Robbins 3 p. 278].
    - **Confluent / Bridging Necrosis:** Severe cases show necrosis linking portal-to-portal or portal-to-central vascular structures [Robbins 3 p. 273, 278].

## 5. Clinical Features [⭐ High-Yield]

### A. Symptoms [⭐ High-Yield]

#### 1. Prodromal (Pre-icteric) Symptoms

- **Constitutional:** Severe fatigue, weakness, lethargy, generalized myalgia, headache, and low-grade fever [Robbins 3 p. 271].
- **Gastrointestinal:** Anorexia (profound loss of appetite), nausea, vomiting, right upper quadrant abdominal discomfort, and altered taste (loss of taste for tobacco in smokers) [Robbins 3 p. 271].
- **Immune-Complex Symptoms (especially Acute HBV):** Serum-sickness-like syndrome with fever, urticarial skin rash, arthralgias, or arthritis [Robbins 3 p. 247, 250].

#### 2. Icteric Symptoms

- **Dark Tea-Colored Urine:** Appears 1 to 5 days before visible skin/scleral jaundice [Robbins 3 p. 271].
- **Jaundice:** Yellowish discoloration of sclera and skin [Robbins 3 p. 271, 283].
- **Pale / Clay-Colored Stools & Pruritus:** Transient light-colored stools and generalized itching [Robbins 3 p. 283].
- _Note:_ Constitutional symptoms often abate once overt jaundice appears.

---

### B. Physical Signs [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Scleral Icterus & Cutaneous Jaundice:** Evaluated under natural light on the bulbar conjunctiva [Robbins 3 p. 283].
2. **Tender Hepatomegaly:** Soft, smooth, mildly enlarged liver tender to palpation in right hypochondrium [Robbins 3 p. 273].
3. **Mild Splenomegaly & Cervical Lymphadenopathy:** Present in 10% to 20% of cases [Robbins 3 p. 271].
4. **Red Flags for Fulminant Hepatic Failure [⭐ Critical]:** Asterixis ("flapping tremor"), confusion, somnolence, rapidly shrinking liver span, and rising INR >1.5 [Robbins 3 p. 234].

## 6. Diagnosis / Laboratory Investigations [⭐ High-Yield]

### A. Biochemical Liver Function Tests (LFTs) [⭐ High-Yield]

- **Serum Aminotransferases (ALT & AST):** **Markedly elevated (>10 to 100 times normal; typically >1000 U/L)**[Robbins 3 p. 234, 271]. **ALT is characteristically higher than AST (ALT > AST)**, reflecting direct hepatocellular injury [Robbins 3 p. 282].
- **Serum Bilirubin:** Elevated total and conjugated bilirubin (typically 5 to 20 mg/dL) [Robbins 3 p. 271, 283].
- **Serum Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP):** Normal or mildly elevated (<3 times upper limit of normal) [Robbins 3 p. 271].
- **Prothrombin Time (PT) / INR [⭐ Single Most Important Prognostic Marker]:** Normal or mildly prolonged in uncomplicated cases. **PT prolongation (INR >1.5) signals massive hepatic necrosis and impending acute liver failure** [Robbins 3 p. 234, 271].

4. **Anti-HBs:** Indicates viral clearance, recovery, and lifelong protective immunity [Robbins 3 p. 253].

## 8. Treatment / Management [⭐ High-Yield]

### A. General Supportive Management (All Acute Viral Hepatitis)

1. **Rest & Physical Activity:** Bed rest guided by patient tolerance; return to light activity as symptoms and jaundice clear.
2. **Dietary Management:** High-calorie, carbohydrate-rich, balanced diet. Small frequent morning meals are best tolerated (nausea worsens in evening). Avoid strict fat restriction unless severe nausea exists.
3. **Avoidance of Hepatotoxins:** Complete avoidance of alcohol, paracetamol (or strictly <2 g/day), sedatives, and hepatotoxic drugs [Kdt 3 p. 828, 854].
4. **Monitoring:** Serial LFTs and PT/INR monitoring to screen for impending fulminant liver failure.

---

### B. Pharmacotherapy & Dosage Protocols (Dosage Rules) [⭐ High-Yield]

#### 1. Indications for Antiviral Therapy

- **Routine Antiviral Therapy is NOT Indicated** for acute uncomplicated HAV, HBV, or HEV infection, as >90% to 95% of adult cases resolve spontaneously [Kdt 3 p. 854, Robbins 3 p. 249].
- **Indications for Antiviral Therapy in Acute HBV:**
    1. Severe acute hepatitis B (marked prolonged jaundice, INR >1.5, or deep encephalopathy).
    2. Immunocompromised hosts [Kdt 3 p. 854].

#### 2. Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate (TDF) — Nucleotide Analogue [⭐ First-Line]

- **Name:** Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate (TDF).
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 855). Monophosphate nucleotide analogue related to AMP. Intracellularly phosphorylated by cellular kinases to active **Tenofovir Diphosphate**. Competitively inhibits **HBV DNA Polymerase** and reverse transcriptase, incorporating into viral DNA to cause **chain termination**.
    
    ```
    Tenofovir Disoproxil → Cellular Kinases → Tenofovir Diphosphate → Inhibits HBV DNA Polymerase → Viral DNA Chain Termination
    ```
    
- **Target / Receptors:** HBV DNA Polymerase / Reverse Transcriptase.
- **Dose:** **300 mg PO once daily (OD)** [Kdt 3 p. 855].
- **Dosage / Route:** Oral tablet (swallowed whole with or without food).
- **Frequency:** Once daily (OD).
- **Features:** First-line potent antiviral agent for Hepatitis B; high barrier to resistance; active against lamivudine-resistant strains [Kdt 3 p. 854, 855].
- **Side Effects:** Nausea, flatulence, abdominal discomfort, headache, mild rise in serum creatinine, rare renal tubular injury (Fanconi syndrome), bone mineral density loss [Kdt 3 p. 855].
- **When to Start / Stop:** Start in severe acute HBV (INR >1.5, fulminant course) or chronic active HBV; continue until HBsAg clearance or sustained seroconversion [Kdt 3 p. 854, 855].

#### 3. Entecavir — Guanosine Nucleoside Analogue [⭐ First-Line]

- **Name:** Entecavir.
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 854, 855). Guanosine nucleoside analogue. Phosphorylated to active **Entecavir Triphosphate**, which competitively inhibits all 3 functions of **HBV DNA Polymerase**: (1) base priming, (2) reverse transcription of negative strand, and (3) synthesis of positive strand HBV DNA [Kdt 3 p. 854].
- **Dose:** **0.5 mg PO once daily (OD)** for treatment-naive patients; **1.0 mg PO OD** for lamivudine-resistant cases [Kdt 3 p. 855].
- **Dosage / Route:** Oral (must be taken on an **empty stomach**, 2 hours before or after meals) [Kdt 3 p. 855].
- **Frequency:** Once daily (OD).
- **Features:** First-line highly potent agent with rapid viral suppression and high barrier to resistance in treatment-naive patients [Kdt 3 p. 854, 855].
- **Side Effects:** Headache, fatigue, dizziness, nausea, rare lactic acidosis in decompensated cirrhosis [Kdt 3 p. 855].
- **When to Start / Stop:** Same indications as Tenofovir; adjust dose in renal impairment [Kdt 3 p. 854, 855].

### C. Prevention & Immunoprophylaxis [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Hepatitis A Immunization:**
    - _Active:_ Inactivated HAV vaccine (2 doses at 0 and 6–12 months IM).
    - _Post-Exposure:_ Single dose HAV vaccine or Standard Immune Globulin (0.02 mL/kg IM) given within 2 weeks of exposure [Davidson 1 p. 100].
2. **Hepatitis B Immunization [⭐ High-Yield]:**
    - _Active:_ Recombinant HBsAg Vaccine (3-dose schedule at **0, 1, and 6 months IM** in deltoid muscle). Dose = 20 μg (1 mL) in adults [Kdt 4 p. 981]. Target anti-HBs titer **>10 mIU/mL**.
    - _Post-Exposure / Perinatal:_ Hepatitis B Immune Globulin (**HBIG 1000–2000 IU IM** or 32–48 IU/kg) plus first dose of HBV vaccine administered at separate sites within 12 to 24 hours [Kdt 4 p. 982].

## 9. Complications [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Acute Fulminant Hepatic Failure:** Massive liver necrosis causing acute liver failure, encephalopathy, coagulopathy, and cerebral edema (<1% HAV/HBV; **~20% in pregnant women with HEV**) [Robbins 3 p. 242, 265].
2. **Chronicity, Cirrhosis & Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC):** Occurs in 5–10% of adult HBV and >80% of HCV cases [Robbins 3 p. 249, 255].
3. **Prolonged Cholestatic Hepatitis:** Persistent deep jaundice and pruritus lasting >2 to 6 months in HAV/HEV [Robbins 3 p. 247].
4. **Relapsing Hepatitis:** Recurrence of symptoms and ALT surge within 6 months of recovery [Robbins 3 p. 247].
5. **Extrahepatic Immune-Complex Complications:** Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN), Membranous Glomerulonephritis, and Serum-Sickness Syndrome in HBV [Robbins 3 p. 247, 250].

---

## 10. Prognosis

- **HAV & HEV:** Excellent prognosis in non-pregnant individuals (>99% complete recovery with lifelong immunity) [Robbins 3 p. 241, 264]. High mortality in pregnant females infected with HEV (~20%) [Robbins 3 p. 265].
- **HBV:** 90% to 95% of adult-acquired HBV resolves spontaneously; neonatally acquired HBV progresses to chronic infection in ~90% of cases [Robbins 3 p. 249, 250].

## 13. Clinical Pearl

> **Clinical Pearl:** In evaluating a patient suspected of acute hepatitis B during the symptomatic or early recovery phase, **HBsAg may occasionally fall below detectable levels before Anti-HBs has developed**! This diagnostic gap is termed the **"Window Period"**. During this window, ordering only HBsAg and Anti-HBs will yield falsely negative results. **IgM anti-HBc is the ONLY positive serological marker during the Window Period**, making it mandatory for accurately diagnosing acute HBV infection!

---

## 14. Exam Trap

### Exam Trap

- **The Mix-Up:** Prescribing **Tenofovir or Entecavir** routinely for every patient presenting with **Acute Uncomplicated Hepatitis B**.
- **Distinguishing Fact:** Over **90% to 95% of adult patients with acute HBV clear the virus spontaneously**without antiviral intervention. Routine antiviral therapy does not speed up recovery or reduce chronicity in uncomplicated cases.
- **Resolution:** **Antiviral drugs (Tenofovir/Entecavir) are NOT indicated for routine acute HBV**! They are reserved strictly for **Severe Acute Hepatitis B** (marked by INR >1.5, protracted deep jaundice, or encephalopathy) or immunocompromised patients.

---

### Quick Revision

- **Etiology & Transmission:** HAV and HEV are transmitted via the **fecal-oral route** (water/food); HBV, HCV, and HDV are transmitted **parenterally, sexually, and perinatally** [Robbins 3 p. 268].
- **High-Yield Serology:**
    - **HAV:** IgM anti-HAV = Acute infection [Robbins 3 p. 243, 268].
    - **HBV:** HBsAg = Active infection; **IgM anti-HBc = Acute HBV / Window Period marker**; Anti-HBs = Recovery/Immunity [Robbins 3 p. 253, 268].
    - **HEV:** IgM anti-HEV = Acute infection [Robbins 3 p. 264, 268].
- **Pregnancy Risk:** HEV infection causes **~20% mortality** due to acute fulminant liver failure in pregnant women [Robbins 3 p. 265].
- **LFT Pattern:** Marked ALT and AST elevation (>1000 U/L) with **ALT > AST**. Prolonged **PT/INR >1.5** is the best marker of impending liver failure [Robbins 3 p. 234, 271].
- **Management:** Supportive care for acute HAV, HBV, and HEV. Antivirals (Tenofovir 300 mg OD / Entecavir 0.5 mg OD) reserved strictly for severe acute HBV (INR >1.5) or chronic active hepatitis [Kdt 3 p. 854, 855].

---

### One-Line Exam Opener

> **Acute viral hepatitis is a systemic infection caused primarily by hepatotropic viruses (HAV, HBV, HCV, HDV, HEV), characterized by immune-mediated hepatocyte ballooning degeneration and apoptosis (Councilman bodies), marked ALT elevation >1000 U/L, and diagnosed via virus-specific serological markers such as IgM anti-HAV, IgM anti-HBc, and IgM anti-HEV.**
