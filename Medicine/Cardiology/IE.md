# Infective Endocarditis

## 1. Definition [⭐ High-Yield]

**Infective Endocarditis (IE)** is a microbial infection of the heart valves or the mural endocardium that leads to the formation of bulky, friable vegetations composed of thrombotic debris (fibrin and platelets) and microorganisms, typically accompanied by the destruction of the underlying cardiac tissues.

---

## 2. Etiology / Causes / Risk Factors [⭐ High-Yield]

### A. Causative Microorganisms

1. **Streptococci (50–60% of cases):**
    - **Viridans Streptococci (_Streptococcus mitis_, _Streptococcus sanguis_):** Normal commensals of the oral cavity; enter the bloodstream during tooth-brushing or dental procedures, causing subacute endocarditis on pre-existing abnormal valves.
    - **Streptococcus gallolyticus subsp. gallolyticus (formerly _Streptococcus bovis_):** Enters from the bowel; strongly associated with **colonic carcinoma** (mandates colonoscopy).
2. **Staphylococci (25–35% of cases):**
    - **Staphylococcus aureus:** Most common cause of acute endocarditis overall and dominant pathogen in intravenous drug users (IVDUs), typically attacking the tricuspid valve.
    - **Coagulase-Negative Staphylococci (_Staphylococcus epidermidis_):** Major cause of prosthetic valve endocarditis occurring within 2 months of cardiac surgery.
3. **Enterococci (_Enterococcus faecalis_, _Enterococcus faecium_):** Enter blood from the urinary tract or gut, common in elderly males.
4. **HACEK Group (3–5% of cases):**
    - Fastidious Gram-negative oral commensals: _**H**_ _aemophilus aphrophilus_ (now _Aggregatibacter aphrophilus_), _**A**_ _ggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans_, _**C**_ _ardiobacterium hominis_, _**E**_ _ikenella corrodens_, and _**K**_ _ingella kingae_.
5. **Fungi & Intracellular Pathogens:**
    - _Candida_ and _Aspergillus_ in immunocompromised hosts or long-term indwelling IV lines.
    - _Coxiella burnetii_ (Q fever - farm animal contact) and _Bartonella_ species causing culture-negative endocarditis.

### B. Predisposing Cardiac & Host Risk Factors

- **High-Velocity Jet Damage:** High-pressure hemodynamic jets hitting endocardium (e.g., Ventricular Septal Defect, Mitral Regurgitation, Aortic Regurgitation) create endothelial erosion vulnerable to bacterial seeding. Low-pressure defects (like Atrial Septal Defect) carry minimal risk.
- **Valvular Pathology:** Rheumatic Heart Disease (RHD), floppy/prolapsed mitral valve (MVP), calcified aortic valve, bicuspid aortic valve.
- **Prosthetic Valves & Devices:** Mechanical or bioprosthetic valves, cardiac pacemakers.
- **Intravenous Drug Misuse (IVDU):** Introduces _S. aureus_ directly into systemic circulation.

---

## 3. Classification

### A. Based on Clinical Course & Virulence

1. **Acute Infective Endocarditis:** Stormy, rapid onset caused by highly virulent pathogens (_Staphylococcus aureus_) infecting a previously normal or abnormal valve, producing florid, destructive vegetations, ring abscesses, and rapid valve destruction.
2. **Subacute Infective Endocarditis:** Insidious, low-grade febrile course over weeks to months caused by low-virulence organisms (_Viridans Streptococci_) infecting previously damaged or deformed valves.

### B. Based on Substrate & Location

- **Native Valve Endocarditis (NVE)**.
- **Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE):**
    - _Early PVE (<2 months post-op):_ Usually acquired perioperatively by skin flora (_Staphylococcus epidermidis_).
    - _Late PVE (>1 year post-op):_ Spectrum resembles native valve disease (_S. viridans_, _S. aureus_).
- **Right-Sided Endocarditis:** Predominantly involves the tricuspid valve in IV drug users.

## 4. Pathophysiology (with Flowchart) [⭐ High-Yield]

### Pathogenetic Sequence

1. **Endothelial Damage & Non-Bacterial Thrombotic Endocarditis (NBTE):**
    - Turbulent blood flow or mechanical trauma disrupts valve endothelium, exposing subendothelial collagen.
    - Platelets and fibrin deposit at the injury site to form a sterile, non-bacterial thrombotic vegetation.
2. **Transient Bacteremia & Microbial Colonization:**
    - Trivial mucosal trauma (dental work, chewing, intravenous lines) introduces bacteria into the bloodstream.
    - Microorganisms adhere to the sterile fibrin-platelet meshwork.
3. **Vegetation Growth & Protection:**
    - Proliferating bacteria secrete extracellular matrix and attract further fibrin-platelet aggregation.
    - The avascular structure of valve cusps and protective fibrin encasement shelter proliferating colonies from host leukocytes and circulating antibodies.
4. **Tissue Destruction & Extracardiac Sequelae:**
    - Bacterial enzymes cause valve tissue destruction, leaflet perforation, chordal rupture, and ring abscess formation.
    - Friable vegetation fragments detach into circulation, causing **septic embolization** (ischemic stroke, splenic/renal infarcts, septic pulmonary emboli) and **immune-complex deposition** (glomerulonephritis, vasculitis).

## 5. Clinical Features [⭐ High-Yield]

### A. Symptoms

- **Constitutional Symptoms:** Persistent low-grade or high-spiking fever (≥38.0°C), chills, night sweats, fatigue, anorexia, weight loss, and malaise.
- **Cardiac Symptoms:** Progressive exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea secondary to acute valve disruption and heart failure.
- **Embolic / Extracardiac Symptoms:** Sudden focal neurological deficits (embolic stroke), sharp flank pain with hematuria (renal infarction), left upper quadrant abdominal pain (splenic infarction), or painful fingers/toes.

### B. Physical Signs

- **Cardiac Findings:**
    - Changing or new regurgitant heart murmur in >90% of cases (e.g., new blowing pan-systolic murmur of MR or early diastolic murmur of AR).
    - Signs of congestive heart failure (elevated JVP, S₃ gallop, bi-basal crackles).
- **Vascular / Embolic Stigmata:**
    - **Janeway Lesions:** Small, flat, **non-tender**, erythematous or hemorrhagic macules on the palms and soles caused by septic microemboli.
    - **Splinter Hemorrhages:** Linear, dark-red subungual longitudinal streaks under fingernails or toenails.
    - **Petechiae:** Found on conjunctivae, oral palatal mucosa, and skin.
- **Immunological Stigmata:**
    - **Osler Nodes:** Small, **tender**, painful erythematous subcutaneous nodules in the pulp of fingers and toes caused by immune-complex vasculitis.
    - **Roth Spots:** Oval retinal hemorrhages with pale, white central centers visible on fundoscopy.
    - **Glomerulonephritis:** Microscopic hematuria and proteinuria.
- **Late Physical Signs:** Digital clubbing (10%) and splenomegaly (30–40%) in long-standing subacute cases.

## 6. Diagnosis / Laboratory Features (Duke Criteria) [⭐ High-Yield]

### A. Modified Duke Criteria for Diagnosis

A **Definite Clinical Diagnosis** of IE requires:

- **2 Major Criteria**, OR
- **1 Major + 3 Minor Criteria**, OR
- **5 Minor Criteria**.

#### 1. Major Criteria

1. **Positive Blood Culture for Infective Endocarditis:**
    - Typical microorganism (_S. viridans_, _S. bovis_, HACEK group, _S. aureus_, or _Enterococci_) isolated from 2 separate blood cultures.
    - Persistently positive blood culture: Organisms drawn >12 hours apart, or 3 out of 3 (or majority of ≥4) positive cultures drawn over >1 hour.
2. **Evidence of Endocardial Involvement:**
    - Echocardiogram positive for IE: Oscillating intracardiac mass/vegetation on valve or supporting structures, intracardiac abscess, or new partial separation of prosthetic valve.
    - New valvular regurgitation (worsening or changing of pre-existing murmur not sufficient).

#### 2. Minor Criteria

1. **Predisposition:** Known predisposing heart condition or IV drug misuse.
2. **Fever:** Temperature ≥38.0°C.
3. **Vascular Phenomena:** Major arterial emboli, septic pulmonary infarcts, mycotic aneurysm, intracranial hemorrhage, conjunctival hemorrhage, or Janeway lesions.
4. **Immunological Phenomena:** Glomerulonephritis, Osler nodes, Roth spots, or positive Rheumatoid Factor.
5. **Microbiological Evidence:** Positive blood culture not meeting major criteria or serological evidence of active infection.

---

### B. Laboratory & Imaging Findings

1. **Blood Cultures (Most Crucial Initial Test):** Draw 3 sets of blood cultures from different venipuncture sites under strict aseptic technique prior to starting antibiotics.
2. **Echocardiography:**
    - **Transthoracic Echocardiography (TTE):** Sensitivity 50–90%; excellent first-line test.
    - **Transesophageal Echocardiography (TOE):** Sensitivity >95%; mandatory if TTE is negative or technically difficult, in prosthetic valves, or when ring abscess is suspected.
3. **Hematology & Acute Phase Reactants:**
    - Normocytic normochromic anemia of chronic disease.
    - Polymorphonuclear leukocytosis.
    - Markedly elevated C-Reactive Protein (CRP) and Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR).
4. **Urinalysis:** Microscopic hematuria (present in 60–70%) and proteinuria.
5. **Electrocardiogram (ECG):** New prolongation of PR interval or new **AV Block** indicates perivalvular extension into the cardiac conduction tissue (aortic root ring abscess).

### A. Empirical Antibiotic Regimens (Before Culture Results)

- **Subacute Native Valve Endocarditis:**
    - **Amoxicillin** 2 g IV 6 times daily + **Gentamicin** 1 mg/kg IV twice daily.
- **Acute Native Valve Endocarditis / Penicillin Allergy / MRSA Suspected:**
    - **Vancomycin** 1 g IV 12-hourly + **Gentamicin** 1 mg/kg IV 12-hourly.
- **Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis:**
    - **Vancomycin** 1 g IV 12-hourly + **Gentamicin** 1 mg/kg IV 12-hourly + **Rifampicin** 300–600 mg PO 12-hourly.

---

### B. Targeted Antibiotic Therapy (Based on Culture & MIC) [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Penicillin-Sensitive Streptococci (MIC ≤0.125 mg/L):**
    - **Benzylpenicillin** 1.2 g IV 4-hourly for 4 weeks (native valve) or 6 weeks (prosthetic valve).
    - _Alternative 2-Week Short Course:_ Benzylpenicillin 1.2 g IV 4-hourly + Gentamicin 1 mg/kg IV 12-hourly for 2 weeks (only for uncomplicated native valve cases).
2. **Methicillin-Sensitive _Staphylococcus aureus_ (MSSA):**
    - **Flucloxacillin** 2 g IV 4-hourly for 4–6 weeks.
3. **Methicillin-Resistant _Staphylococcus aureus_ (MRSA) / Penicillin Allergy:**
    - **Vancomycin** 1 g IV 12-hourly for 4–6 weeks.
4. **Enterococcal Endocarditis:**
    - **Amoxicillin** 2 g IV 4-hourly + **Gentamicin** 1 mg/kg IV 12-hourly for 4–6 weeks.

---

### C. Specific Dosage Rules for Core Antimicrobials

#### 1. Benzylpenicillin (Penicillin G)

- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 748; Davidson p. 528). β-lactam antibiotic that binds to Penicillin-Binding Proteins (PBPs) on the bacterial cell wall, inhibiting transpeptidation and cell wall peptidoglycan synthesis, exerting a bactericidal effect.
    
    ```
    β-Lactam Ring → Binds PBPs → Inhibits Transpeptidation → Cell Wall Lysis
    ```
    
- **Receptors / Target:** Bacterial Penicillin-Binding Proteins (PBPs).
- **Dose:** 1.2 g (2 million Units) IV every 4 hours (7.2 g/day).
- **Route:** Intravenous.
- **Frequency:** 6 times daily (every 4 hours).
- **Features:** Gold-standard bactericidal drug for penicillin-sensitive _Streptococci_.
- **Side Effects:** Hypersensitivity/anaphylaxis, Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction, seizures at high doses in renal failure, hyperkalemia (potassium salt).
- **When to Start / Stop:** Initiated after blood culture collection. Stop if severe Type I immediate hypersensitivity/anaphylaxis occurs.

#### 2. Gentamicin

- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 750; Davidson p. 528). Aminoglycoside that binds irreversibly to the 30S ribosomal subunit, causing mRNA misreading and inhibition of bacterial protein synthesis. Exhibits synergistic bactericidal action with cell wall-active agents (penicillins/vancomycin) in endocarditis.
- **Receptors / Target:** Bacterial 30S Ribosomal Subunit.
- **Dose:** 1 mg/kg IV 12-hourly (or 3 mg/kg IV once daily for oral streptococcal IE).
- **Route:** Intravenous slow infusion.
- **Frequency:** Twice daily (every 12 hours).
- **Features:** Used for synergistic killing in enterococcal and streptococcal endocarditis.
- **Side Effects:** Ototoxicity (vestibular/cochlear damage), nephrotoxicity (acute tubular necrosis).
- **When to Start / Stop:** Monitor trough levels (target pre-dose ≤1 mg/L, post-dose 3–5 mg/L). Stop or reduce dose if trough level >1 mg/L or serum creatinine rises.

#### 3. Vancomycin

- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 806; Davidson p. 528). Glycopeptide antibiotic that binds to the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus of cell wall peptidoglycan precursors, blocking cross-linking and peptidoglycan polymerization.
- **Receptors / Target:** D-Alanyl-D-Alanine dipeptide terminus.
- **Dose:** 1 g IV 12-hourly (infused over 60 minutes).
- **Route:** Intravenous slow infusion.
- **Frequency:** Twice daily (every 12 hours).
- **Features:** Drug of choice for MRSA and severe penicillin-allergic patients.
- **Side Effects:** "Red Man Syndrome" (histamine release during rapid IV infusion), ototoxicity, nephrotoxicity, thrombophlebitis.
- **When to Start / Stop:** Monitor pre-dose trough serum levels (target 15–20 mg/L). Infuse slowly over 60 minutes to prevent Red Man syndrome.

### D. Indications for Cardiac Surgery (Box 16.98) [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Severe Heart Failure** secondary to acute valve destruction or cusp perforation.
2. **Persistent / Uncontrolled Infection** despite optimal bactericidal antibiotic therapy.
3. **Fungal or Resistant Organisms** (_Candida_, _Aspergillus_, _S. aureus_).
4. **Perivalvular Extension:** Development of aortic root ring abscess, fistula, or new conduction block.
5. **Embolic Risk:** Large left-sided vegetations (>10 mm diameter) with recurrent embolic events.

---

## 9. Complications [⭐ High-Yield]

1. **Cardiac Complications (Most Common Cause of Mortality):** Congestive heart failure (40–50%), valve destruction, aortic root ring abscess, complete heart block (AV conduction block), myocarditis, and pericarditis.
2. **Embolic Complications:** Ischemic stroke (15%), cerebral mycotic aneurysms, splenic and renal infarcts, septic pulmonary emboli (in right-sided IE).
3. **Renal Complications:** Immune-complex glomerulonephritis, renal infarction, and acute tubular necrosis (due to nephrotoxic antibiotics like gentamicin/vancomycin).
4. **Neurological Complications:** Brain abscess, purulent bacterial meningitis, mycotic aneurysm rupture with intracranial hemorrhage.

---

## 10. Prevention / Prophylaxis Before Dental Procedures [⭐ High-Yield]

### A. Clinical Rationale & High-Risk Patient Group

High-risk dental procedures (manipulation of gingival tissue, periapical tooth region, or perforation of oral mucosa) can induce transient _Viridans Streptococci_ bacteremia. Prophylaxis is indicated **only** in high-risk cardiac patients:

1. Patients with a **prosthetic heart valve** or prosthetic material used for valve repair.
2. Patients with **previous history of infective endocarditis**.
3. Patients with **unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease** or repaired CHD with residual defects.

---

### B. Prophylactic Antibiotic Regimens (KDT p. 806, 896)

All prophylactic doses are administered as a **single dose 30 to 60 minutes BEFORE** the procedure.

## 12. Clinical Pearl

> **Clinical Pearl:** In any patient presenting with persistent unexplained fever and a new **AV Block (prolonged PR interval or Wenckebach) on ECG**, immediately suspect an **aortic root ring abscess** complicating aortic valve infective endocarditis! The aortic root lies in close anatomical proximity to the AV node and Bundle of His; perivalvular abscess extension destroys the conduction pathway, serving as a urgent indication for transesophageal echo (TOE) and emergency cardiac surgery.

---

## 13. Exam Trap

### Exam Trap

- **The Mix-Up:** Confusing **Janeway Lesions** and **Osler Nodes**.
- **Distinguishing Fact:**
    - **Janeway Lesions:** **NON-TENDER**, flat, macular erythematous lesions on palms/soles caused by **septic microemboli**.
    - **Osler Nodes:** **PAINFUL & TENDER**, raised subcutaneous nodular lesions on finger/toe pulps caused by **immune-complex deposition (vasculitis)**.

### Quick Revision

- **Commonest Pathogens:** _Streptococcus viridans_ (subacute native valve), _Staphylococcus aureus_ (acute & IVDU), _Staphylococcus epidermidis_ (early prosthetic valve).
- **Diagnosis:** Modified Duke Criteria (2 Major OR 1 Major + 3 Minor OR 5 Minor). Blood cultures (3 sets) + Echo (TOE >95% sensitive).
- **Classic Stigmata:** Janeway lesions (non-tender microemboli), Osler nodes (tender vasculitis), Roth spots (retinal hemorrhage), Splinter hemorrhages.
- **Medical Treatment:** High-dose IV bactericidal therapy for 4–6 weeks (e.g., Benzylpenicillin + Gentamicin or Vancomycin + Gentamicin).
- **Dental Prophylaxis:** Single oral dose of **Amoxicillin 2.0 g** (or Clindamycin 600 mg if penicillin-allergic) 30–60 minutes before high-risk dental procedures in high-risk patients.

> **Infective endocarditis is a lethal microbial infection of the endocardium characterized by friable, destructive, germ-laden vegetations that induce progressive valve failure, life-threatening systemic embolization, and immune-complex vasculitis.**
