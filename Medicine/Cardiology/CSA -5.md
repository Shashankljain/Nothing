# Chronic Stable Angina — Pharmacological Management

## 1. Definition [⭐ High-Yield]

**Chronic Stable Angina (Angina Pectoris)** is a clinical syndrome characterized by predictable, episodic retrosternal chest discomfort or pain, precipitated by physical exertion, emotional stress, or eating, and relieved within 5 to 10 minutes by rest or sublingual nitroglycerin. It arises from a transient imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and myocardial oxygen demand, caused by fixed atherosclerotic narrowing (>70% stenosis) of epicardial coronary arteries.

## 3. Pathophysiology & Drug Target Mechanisms (with Flowchart) [⭐ High-Yield]

### Pathogenetic Sequence

1. **Coronary Atherosclerosis:** Fixed structural narrowing of epicardial coronary arteries limits blood flow increases during exertion.
2. **Myocardial Oxygen Supply/Demand Mismatch:** Exertion increases heart rate, systolic blood pressure, and ventricular wall tension, triggering myocardial ischemia and accumulation of pain-producing metabolites (adenosine, lactic acid).
3. **Hemodynamic Targets of Pharmacotherapy:**
    - **Preload Reduction:** Venodilation increases peripheral venous pooling, reducing end-diastolic ventricular volume/pressure and restoring subendocardial blood flow (Nitrates, Nicorandil).
    - **Afterload Reduction:** Arteriolar dilation decreases total peripheral resistance and peak systolic wall tension (CCBs, Nitrates, Nicorandil).
    - **Heart Rate & Contractility Reduction:** Decreasing heart rate prolongs diastolic perfusion time and lowers oxygen consumption (β-Blockers, Verapamil, Diltiazem, Ivabradine).
    - **Metabolic & Electrical Modulation:** Direct cellular protection without changing heart rate or blood pressure (Ranolazine, Trimetazidine).

## 4. Detailed Pharmacotherapy & Dosage Protocols [⭐ High-Yield]

### A. Organic Nitrates (Glyceryl Trinitrate as Prototype) [⭐ High-Yield]

#### 1. Glyceryl Trinitrate (GTN / Nitroglycerin)

- **Name:** Glyceryl Trinitrate (GTN).
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 586). Denitrated in vascular smooth muscle cells by mitochondrial aldehyde dehydrogenase to release free radical **Nitric Oxide (NO)**. NO activates cytosolic soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC) → increases cyclic GMP (cGMP) → stimulates cGMP-dependent protein kinase (PKG) → causes dephosphorylation of myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) → smooth muscle relaxation.
    
    - _Preload Reduction:_ Dilates veins > arteries → peripheral venous pooling → ↓ venous return → ↓ LV end-diastolic size and pressure → relieves subendocardial "crunch".
    - _Afterload Reduction:_ Arteriolar dilation → ↓ total peripheral resistance.
    
    ```
    GTN -> Organic Denitration -> Nitric Oxide (NO) -> Soluble Guanylyl Cyclase -> ↑ cGMP -> MLCK Dephosphorylation -> Vascular Smooth Muscle Relaxation
    ```
    
- **Receptors / Target:** Soluble Guanylyl Cyclase (sGC).
- **Dose:**
    - _Sublingual Tablet:_ **0.5 mg** placed under the tongue at onset of pain or 5 minutes prior to exertion.
    - _Sublingual Spray:_ **0.4 to 0.8 mg** (1–2 sprays).
    - _Sustained Release Oral:_ **2.5 to 6.5 mg** SR capsule / **5 to 15 mg** SR tablet.
    - _Transdermal Patch:_ **5 to 10 mg** patch applied for 12–14 hours daily.
- **Dosage / Route:** Sublingual, oral, transdermal, or IV.
- **Frequency:** As needed (sublingual) or once/twice daily with a nitrate-free interval (oral/transdermal).
- **Features:** Unsurpassed rapid abortive therapy for acute anginal attacks (onset 2–3 minutes). Continuous use leads to **nitrate tolerance**, requiring a 6–8 hour nitrate-free interval daily (e.g., removing patch at night).
- **Side Effects:** Throbbing headache (most common), flushing, dizziness, postural hypotension, reflex tachycardia, methemoglobinemia (rare at clinical doses).
- **When to Start / Stop:** Start sublingually at symptom onset. **STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED** within 24 hours of taking phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE-5) inhibitors like **Sildenafil or Tadalafil** (causes catastrophic hypotension), and in systolic BP <90 mmHg, HR <50 or >100 bpm, or right ventricular infarction.

#### 2. Isosorbide Mononitrate (ISMN) & Isosorbide Dinitrate (ISDN)

- **Name:** Isosorbide-5-mononitrate, Isosorbide dinitrate.
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 586–589). Similar to GTN; releases NO in vascular smooth muscle. ISMN undergoes virtually no first-pass hepatic metabolism, yielding high oral bioavailability (nearly 100%) and long duration of action.
- **Receptors / Target:** Soluble Guanylyl Cyclase (sGC).
- **Dose:**
    - _Isosorbide Dinitrate:_ **5 mg** sublingual; **10 to 20 mg** PO 2–3 times daily.
    - _Isosorbide Mononitrate:_ **10 to 40 mg** PO twice daily (eccentric dosing at 8 AM and 2 PM to maintain a nocturnal nitrate-free interval).
- **Dosage / Route:** Sublingual or Oral.
- **Frequency:** Once or twice daily (eccentric schedule).
- **Features:** Oral maintenance prophylaxis for stable angina.

---

### B. Beta-Adrenoceptor Blockers (β-Blockers) [⭐ High-Yield]

#### 1. Metoprolol & Bisoprolol

- **Name:** Metoprolol (Succinate/Tartrate), Bisoprolol, Atenolol.
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 162, 591). Competitive, selective antagonists at cardiac **β₁-adrenoceptors**.
    
    - Reduces heart rate (negative chronotropy), myocardial contractility (negative inotropy), and mean arterial pressure → decreases myocardial O₂ demand during rest and exertion.
    - Heart rate slowing prolongs **diastolic filling duration**, improving subendocardial perfusion.
    - Favorably redistributes blood flow to ischemic subendocardial regions by decreasing ventricular wall tension.
    
    ```
    β₁-Adrenoceptor Blockade -> ↓ Heart Rate + ↓ Contractility + ↓ BP -> ↓ Myocardial Oxygen Demand + ↑ Diastolic Perfusion Time
    ```
    
- **Receptors / Target:** Cardiac β₁-Adrenoceptors.
- **Dose:**
    - _Metoprolol SR:_ **50 to 200 mg** PO once daily.
    - _Bisoprolol:_ **2.5 to 10 mg** PO once daily.
    - _Atenolol:_ **25 to 100 mg** PO once daily.
- **Dosage / Route:** Oral.
- **Frequency:** Once daily (for SR/long-acting preparations).
- **Features:** **First-line antianginal therapy for all chronic stable angina patients** (unless contraindicated). They are the **ONLY antianginal class proven to prolong life expectancy and reduce mortality/reinfarction** in patients with coronary artery disease.
- **Side Effects:** Bradycardia, fatigue, bronchospasm (in asthmatics), cold extremities, impotence, depression, masking of hypoglycemic symptoms in diabetics.
- **When to Start / Stop:** Start low and titrate to maintain resting HR at 55–60 bpm. **NEVER withdraw abruptly**—abrupt cessation precipitates **β-blocker withdrawal syndrome** (rebound hyperadrenergic state causing severe angina, arrhythmias, or acute MI). **Contraindicated** in severe bronchial asthma, severe bradycardia (HR <50 bpm), 2nd/3rd-degree AV block, and decompensated heart failure.

---

### C. Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs) [⭐ High-Yield]

#### 1. Amlodipine (Dihydropyridine CCB)

- **Name:** Amlodipine.
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 593–595). Inhibits voltage-sensitive **L-type Ca²⁺ channels** in vascular smooth muscle cells. Causes selective, potent arteriolar vasodilation → reduces total peripheral resistance (afterload) → ↓ myocardial O₂ demand. Does not depress SA node firing or AV conduction at clinical doses.
- **Receptors / Target:** L-type Voltage-Gated Calcium Channels.
- **Dose:** **2.5 to 10 mg** PO once daily.
- **Dosage / Route:** Oral.
- **Frequency:** Once daily.
- **Features:** Long-acting DHP (elimination t½ ~35–45 hours) with smooth onset, avoiding rapid reflex tachycardia. Excellent first-line choice in stable angina when β-blockers are contraindicated (e.g., in asthma/COPD) or as add-on therapy to β-blockers.
- **Side Effects:** Ankle pedal edema (due to precapillary arteriolar dilation, not fluid retention), flushing, headache, dizziness, palpitation.
- **When to Start / Stop:** Safe in obstructive airway disease and peripheral vascular disease.

#### 2. Verapamil & Diltiazem (Non-DHP Rate-Limiting CCBs)

- **Name:** Verapamil, Diltiazem.
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 593–594). Block L-type Ca²⁺ channels in both vascular smooth muscle and cardiac myocytes/nodal tissue.
    - _Verapamil:_ Marked direct negative inotropic, chronotropic, and dromotropic actions → slows SA node firing and AV nodal conduction → ↓ cardiac work.
    - _Diltiazem:_ Intermediate cardiac depression with prominent arteriolar vasodilation.
- **Receptors / Target:** L-type Calcium Channels in SA node, AV node, and myocardium.
- **Dose:**
    - _Verapamil:_ **40 to 120 mg** PO 3 times daily (or **120–240 mg** SR daily).
    - _Diltiazem:_ **30 to 60 mg** PO 3–4 times daily (or **90–180 mg** SR twice daily).
- **Dosage / Route:** Oral.
- **Frequency:** 3–4 times daily (or once/twice daily SR).
- **Features:** Used as alternative monotherapy in chronic stable angina when β-blockers are contraindicated.
- **Side Effects:** Constipation (especially verapamil), bradycardia, AV block, hypotension, exacerbation of heart failure.
- **When to Start / Stop:** **DO NOT COMBINE Verapamil or Diltiazem with Beta-Blockers** due to the high risk of severe additive bradycardia, complete AV block, and cardiac failure.

---

### D. Potassium Channel Openers

#### 1. Nicorandil

- **Name:** Nicorandil.
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 598). Dual-mechanism antianginal drug:
    
    1. _ATP-sensitive K⁺ Channel (K_ATP) Opener:_ Hyperpolarizes vascular smooth muscle membrane → closes L-type Ca²⁺ channels → arteriolar vasodilation → ↓ afterload.
    2. _Nitrate-like Action (NO Donor):_ Increases intracellular cGMP → venodilation → ↓ preload.
    
    - Simulates **ischaemic preconditioning** via mitochondrial K_ATP channel opening, protecting myocytes from ischemic injury.
- **Receptors / Target:** ATP-Sensitive Potassium Channels (K_ATP) & Soluble Guanylyl Cyclase.
- **Dose:** **5 to 20 mg** PO twice daily.
- **Dosage / Route:** Oral.
- **Frequency:** Twice daily.
- **Features:** Add-on / 2nd-line antianginal agent; does not exhibit nitrate-like tolerance.
- **Side Effects:** Severe, painful aphthous oral/gastrointestinal ulcers (characteristic side effect; requires drug cessation), throbbing headache, flushing, dizziness.
- **When to Start / Stop:** Add-on for refractory angina; stop immediately if persistent painful oral/mucosal ulceration develops.

### E. Novel & Add-On Antianginal Drugs

#### 1. Ivabradine (Pure Heart Rate Lowering Agent)

- **Name:** Ivabradine.
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 600). Selectively blocks cardiac pacemaker sino-atrial (SA) node **'f' (funny) cation channels** (I_f current). Slows Phase 4 diastolic depolarization → reduces heart rate without affecting myocardial contractility, blood pressure, or intracardiac conduction. Prolongs diastole, enhancing coronary perfusion.
- **Receptors / Target:** SA Node Hyperpolarization-Activated Cyclic Nucleotide-Gated (HCN) 'f' Channels.
- **Dose:** Initial **5 mg** PO twice daily; adjusted to **7.5 mg** PO twice daily (2.5 mg BD in elderly).
- **Dosage / Route:** Oral.
- **Frequency:** Twice daily with meals.
- **Features:** Indicated in chronic stable angina in patients with normal sinus rhythm who are intolerant to β-blockers or in whom β-blockers are contraindicated (e.g., severe asthma/COPD).
- **Side Effects:** Luminous visual phenomena / phosphenes (enhanced brightness in visual field due to retinal I_f channel block), severe bradycardia, first-degree AV block, headache.
- **When to Start / Stop:** Indicated if HR >70 bpm in sinus rhythm. **Contraindicated** if HR <60 bpm, in sick sinus syndrome, 3rd-degree AV block, or atrial fibrillation (ineffective in AF).

#### 2. Ranolazine (Late Sodium Current Inhibitor)

- **Name:** Ranolazine.
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 600). Selectively inhibits the **late inward sodium current (late I_Na)**in ischemic cardiomyocytes. Prevents intracellular Na⁺ accumulation → prevents reverse operation of Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger → reduces intracellular Ca²⁺ overload. Decreases myocardial diastolic wall tension and stiffness without altering heart rate or blood pressure.
- **Receptors / Target:** Cardiac Late Sodium Channels (late I_Na).
- **Dose:** **500 to 1000 mg** PO twice daily (extended-release).
- **Dosage / Route:** Oral.
- **Frequency:** Twice daily.
- **Features:** Useful add-on drug in resistant angina; does not lower BP or HR.
- **Side Effects:** Prolongation of Q-T interval on ECG, dizziness, constipation, nausea, headache.
- **When to Start / Stop:** Avoid concurrent use with other Q-T prolonging drugs or strong CYP3A4 inhibitors.

#### 3. Trimetazidine (Metabolic Modulator / pFOX Inhibitor)

- **Name:** Trimetazidine.
- **Mechanism of Action:** (KDT Textbook p. 599). Inhibits the mitochondrial long-chain **3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase (LC3-KAT)** enzyme, a key enzyme in fatty acid oxidation. Shifts myocardial energy metabolism from fatty acid oxidation to **glucose oxidation**. Since glucose oxidation requires less O₂ per mole of ATP generated, it optimizes myocardial oxygen utilization without affecting HR or BP.
- **Receptors / Target:** Mitochondrial LC3-KAT Enzyme.
- **Dose:** **20 mg** PO 3 times daily (or **35 mg** MR twice daily).
- **Dosage / Route:** Oral.
- **Frequency:** 2–3 times daily.
- **Features:** Add-on metabolic therapy for stable angina.
- **Side Effects:** Gastric burning, dizziness, muscle cramps, reversible parkinsonism/tremor in elderly patients.

## 5. Disease-Modifying & Cardioprotective Therapy [⭐ High-Yield]

Symptomatic antianginal drugs (nitrates, CCBs, ivabradine) relieve symptoms but **do not alter the natural history of CAD or prevent MI/death**. All patients with chronic stable angina MUST receive concurrent disease-modifying cardioprotective therapy:

1. **Antiplatelet Agents:** **Aspirin (75–150 mg/day)** or **Clopidogrel (75 mg/day)** to prevent acute coronary thrombosis.
2. **Lipid-Lowering Therapy:** High-intensity **Statins (Atorvastatin 40–80 mg / Rosuvastatin 20–40 mg)** to stabilize atheromatous plaques and lower LDL-cholesterol.
3. **ACE Inhibitors / ARBs:** **Enalapril (2.5–10 mg/day)** or **Ramipril (2.5–10 mg/day)** to prevent cardiac remodeling, lower cardiovascular mortality, and afford renoprotection.
4. **Beta-Blockers:** Afford primary cardioprotection and lower long-term mortality post-MI and in stable CAD.

---

## 6. Clinical Pearl

> **Clinical Pearl:** In a patient presenting with stable exertional angina who also suffers from **Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) or Bronchial Asthma**, non-selective and selective β-blockers can precipitate life-threatening bronchospasm. The safest first-line substitute for long-term prophylaxis is a long-acting **Dihydropyridine Calcium Channel Blocker (e.g., Amlodipine 5–10 mg/day)** or **Ivabradine (5 mg BD)** combined with a long-acting nitrate (Isosorbide mononitrate).

---

## 7. Exam Trap

### Exam Trap

- **The Mix-Up:** Prescribing **Verapamil or Diltiazem** concurrently with a **Beta-Blocker (e.g., Metoprolol)** for combination antianginal therapy.
- **Distinguishing Fact:** Both β-blockers and non-DHP CCBs possess potent additive negative chronotropic and dromotropic actions on the SA and AV nodes. Combining them can precipitate catastrophic **bradycardia, complete heart block, and severe acute cardiac failure**.
- **Resolution:** If combining a CCB with a β-blocker, **ALWAYS choose a Dihydropyridine CCB (e.g., Amlodipine)**.

### Quick Revision

- **Acute Abortive Therapy:** Sublingual GTN (0.5 mg) or Isosorbide dinitrate (5 mg); acts in 2–3 minutes via venodilation (preload reduction).
- **First-Line Prophylactic Therapy:** Beta-blockers (Metoprolol/Bisoprolol); the ONLY antianginal class that lowers long-term CAD mortality.
- **Beta-Blocker Alternative:** Amlodipine (5–10 mg OD) or Ivabradine (5 mg BD) in asthmatics/COPD.
- **Key Combination Rule:** Combine β-blocker with DHP CCB (Amlodipine); NEVER combine β-blocker with Verapamil/Diltiazem.
- **Mandatory Cardioprotective Triad:** Antiplatelet (Aspirin) + Statin (Atorvastatin) + ACEi (Enalapril).

---

### One-Line Exam Opener

> **Pharmacological management of chronic stable angina requires a dual-track strategy combining hemodynamic antianginal agents (beta-blockers, nitrates, calcium channel blockers) to restore myocardial oxygen supply-demand balance, alongside mandatory disease-modifying cardioprotective therapy (aspirin, statins, ACE inhibitors) to prevent acute coronary events and death.**
